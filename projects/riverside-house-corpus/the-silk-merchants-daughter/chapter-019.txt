Chapter 19: New Beginnings

She noticed the nausea first on a morning in the fourth month, when the crab-apple tree in the Zhang compound's eastern garden was heavy with blossom and the air smelled of coming rain.

It was a mild, insistent nausea that did not resolve by midmorning as the ordinary nausea of an overrich evening meal would, and when it returned the following morning with the same punctual persistence, Wei Lian put down her writing brush and sat very still for a moment, considering the evidence with the same methodical attention she brought to other kinds of problems. Zhang Wei was sixteen months old. He had been born the previous spring. She counted backward and then forward and then sat quietly for another moment with the numbers arranged clearly in her mind.

She did not tell anyone that day, or the day after. She had learned, over the preceding year and a half of her married life, the value of allowing certainty to consolidate before speaking. She confirmed the signs over two weeks: the nausea that visited reliably each morning and faded by midday, the altered sensitivity to certain smells that had accompanied Zhang Wei's early months, the particular fatigue of an afternoon that felt different in quality from ordinary tiredness. She also, on the eighth day, consulted the family's physician, an elderly man named Master Song who had attended the Zhang household for twenty years and who examined her with practiced discretion and confirmed what she had already concluded.

She told Zhang Ming on the evening of the ninth day, after Zhang Wei had been taken to bed and Lady Zhang had retired for her evening prayers. They were sitting in the inner garden where the last warmth of the spring day was still held in the stones, the sky above the compound walls turning the particular soft orange of a clear evening in the fourth month.

Zhang Ming's reaction was immediate and genuine: he reached across the table and covered her hand with his, his face open with the unguarded happiness she had come to associate with his most honest moments, the ones unmediated by the careful professional composure he wore in official contexts.

"When?" he asked.

"Master Song estimates the tenth month."

He turned her hand over in his and held it. "Are you well?"

"Perfectly well. The nausea is inconvenient but not serious. Master Song says it is consistent with a healthy early stage."

"What do you need?"

"Nothing yet." She paused, looking at the darkening garden, the first evening star appearing above the eastern wall. "But there are practical arrangements to consider."

Zhang Ming settled back in his chair with the expression she recognized as his listening posture: attentive, unhurried, ready to work through whatever she had been thinking about. It was one of the things she valued most in their partnership, that readiness to move immediately from feeling to planning, not because feeling was unimportant but because planning was how they expressed care for each other.

"The Sogdian quarter assessment Lord Sun has asked for," she said. "I was intending to do the fieldwork in the fifth and sixth months, visiting the western ward markets and compiling current price data. I cannot do that if I am visibly pregnant in the sixth or seventh month — it would draw attention and questions I do not want to answer."

"I can do the fieldwork visits if you brief me on what to look for."

"That will work for the observable data. For the conversations with merchant contacts, it will be less effective. There are three traders in the western ward who speak to me frankly because they have assessed me as someone who understands their business. They are polite to you but more guarded." She said this without apology; it was simply accurate. "We should move those conversations to the fifth month, before I am visibly changed."

"That gives us six weeks."

"Which is enough if we prioritize." She had already made a list, which she now produced from her sleeve. It was a short list, six items, ordered by urgency. Zhang Ming scanned it with the speed of a man accustomed to reviewing documents under time pressure.

"The ministry's autumn western routes assessment is in the eighth month," he noted. "You will be well advanced by then."

"I can work from home in the eighth and ninth months. Messengers are sufficient for most of the exchange that does not require personal meetings. You have been handling the formal presentation of my analysis since the beginning; that arrangement can continue without visible change."

Zhang Ming set down the list. "What do you want to stop?"

The directness of the question surprised her slightly and pleased her considerably. Many husbands in his position would have proposed a comprehensive cessation of everything that deviated from convention, framed as protective concern. Zhang Ming was asking what she chose.

"Nothing permanently," she said. "Temporarily, the field visits that require me to be present in public commercial spaces. I will work from home through the late pregnancy and for the first several months after, as I did after Zhang Wei. The consulting work can continue in reduced form during that period." She paused. "What I do not want to do is disappear entirely from Lord Sun's regard. The Sogdian quarter assessment is important. If I am absent from significant work for too long, I may find the informal arrangement has quietly lapsed by the time I wish to return to it."

"I will speak to Lord Sun. He is a pragmatic man. He understands that circumstances create pauses."

"Speak to him before my condition is publicly visible." She said this not from vanity but from a clear understanding of how social perception shaped professional relationships. A pregnant woman was a woman fulfilling her primary function, which was admirable, but it was also a woman whose intellectual contributions would be unconsciously discounted by many of the men she worked with, not from malice but from the deeply embedded assumption that a woman's mind, like her body, was engaged elsewhere when she was with child.

She had watched this happen to her mother's younger sister, an unusually sharp woman who had managed her husband's import business for fifteen years until her third pregnancy, after which her husband had slowly taken over the management again, apparently from protectiveness, and she had gradually ceased to participate. Wei Lian did not intend to follow that trajectory.

"I will speak to him this week," Zhang Ming said. He looked at her with the expression she associated with honesty that cost him something to say. "I am glad, Wei Lian. Genuinely glad. Zhang Wei is the best thing I have done in my life thus far, and I know you feel the same. Another child, a sibling for him — I am glad."

"So am I." She covered his hand with hers again. "I hope for a daughter this time."

"Do you?"

"I think about what it would mean to have a daughter. What I would want for her. What I would want to give her." She looked at the evening star, now fully visible over the eastern wall. "With Zhang Wei I think about the world he will inherit. With a daughter I think about the world she will have to negotiate. They are different thoughts."

Zhang Ming was quiet for a moment. "And if it is another son?"

"Then I will love him as I love Zhang Wei, and I will think about what kind of man I want to help him become." She turned to meet his eyes. "But I still hope for a daughter."

She told Lady Zhang the following morning, after the older woman had completed her daily review of the household accounts. They were sitting in the receiving room of the main compound building, cups of spring tea between them, the sound of the servants' early morning work audible from the courtyard beyond.

Lady Zhang received the news with a complex expression that moved through several stages in rapid succession. The first was unmistakable pleasure — the deep, proprietary satisfaction of a mother-in-law whose daughter-in-law has confirmed she is again fulfilling the household's fundamental expectation. The second was a more complicated look that Wei Lian had difficulty reading precisely: something between confirmation and a particular kind of vindication, as though an argument Lady Zhang had been conducting internally had just been settled in her favor.

The third expression, the one that surprised Wei Lian most, was something softer. It lasted only a moment before Lady Zhang's usual composed manner reasserted itself, but it was unmistakably genuine: a flicker of the woman beneath the household authority, remembering something, perhaps her own two pregnancies, perhaps the particular exhaustion and particular wonder of early motherhood.

"You are well?" Lady Zhang asked, her voice carrying more warmth than her usual register.

"Master Song says I am in good health. The nausea is typical for the early stage."

"It will pass. It always passes more easily the second time." Lady Zhang set down her teacup. "You should reduce your activities in the sixth month."

"I have already discussed arrangements with Zhang Ming."

Something passed briefly across Lady Zhang's face at this — the acknowledgment that Wei Lian had already planned, already managed, already arranged things with her husband before informing her mother-in-law. In an earlier period of their relationship, this sequencing would have caused friction. Lady Zhang would have felt consulted too late, her authority as household head insufficiently acknowledged.

That period had passed. In its place was something more nuanced: a recognition, on Lady Zhang's part, that Wei Lian organized her life in a particular way, in consultation with her husband, with efficiency and forward thinking, and that the outcomes of this organization were consistently good for the household. Lady Zhang was too intelligent to resent an arrangement that functioned well simply because its protocols differed from her own.

"You work too much," Lady Zhang said, which was both a criticism and a compliment and had been her standard assessment of Wei Lian for over a year. "But I suppose you will not stop entirely."

"I will manage carefully."

Lady Zhang was quiet for a moment, looking at her daughter-in-law with the specific assessment she reserved for serious matters. "The second child is different," she said at last. "With the first, you do not know what you are doing. With the second, you know exactly what you are in for." A pause. "You will find that more useful and more difficult in equal measure."

Wei Lian accepted this as the genuine advice it was. Lady Zhang's guidance on the practical matters of household and motherhood was always exact and always worth attending to; it was on the broader questions of a woman's ambitions that they had learned to speak carefully around each other.

"Thank you, honored mother," Wei Lian said.

Lady Zhang picked up her teacup again. "I expect you to rest properly, and to inform me immediately if Master Song has any concern about your health. These are not optional requirements."

"I will comply."

"Good." Lady Zhang took a sip of tea and then, with the air of a woman returning to business after a brief interruption, reached for the household accounts she had set aside. "The kitchen is using too much ginger. I have told Cook three times this season."

Wei Lian allowed herself a small internal smile. The conversation was over, and it had gone as well as she had expected and slightly better in one or two moments than she had feared.

In the weeks that followed, as spring deepened into the warm clarity of early summer, Wei Lian found herself carrying two concurrent streams of thought through her days. The first was the practical management of the transition: the six field visits to the western wards completed before her condition became visible, the briefings of Zhang Ming on what to observe in her place, the careful maintenance of her relationship with Lord Sun through a series of well-constructed written analyses delivered through Zhang Ming's hand. The second stream was less systematic and more surprising.

She had not expected to spend the pregnancy thinking about her mother.

Not about Lady Wei in any particular moment, but about the accumulated weight of everything her mother had done over thirty years of managing the Wei household: the precise social calibrations of a merchant wife in Chang'an's commercial world, the management of relationships with buyers and factors and neighbors and officials, the household accounts kept in her mother's careful hand, the education of four children in a world that placed competing expectations on women of good family. Her mother had not been extraordinary in the way that Wei Lian aspired to be extraordinary. But she had been extremely competent, in ways that Wei Lian was only now, at nineteen, beginning to fully see.

She thought about what to give a child. Not property or position — those were contingent on circumstances she could not control — but knowledge. The knowledge that was in her hands, in her eyes, in the accumulated years of observation and analysis. She had been writing it down already, in the private ledger, for herself. But the ledger was a record, not a teaching. A record was for the person who already knew how to read it.

A teaching was something different.

She did not begin writing it yet. The pregnancy was still early, and there was too much other work pressing. But she held the idea in the back of her mind, turning it occasionally like a stone whose full weight she was only beginning to assess. What would she want a daughter to know? Not what society would teach her — the household arts, the proper forms of address, the management of domestic relationships. Those would be taught whether Wei Lian intended them or not. What would a daughter need that she was unlikely to receive from any other source?

The routes, Wei Lian thought on a summer evening, sitting in the garden with Zhang Wei asleep against her shoulder, the sky above the compound walls turning the deep blue of late dusk. The rates of exchange between different currency systems. How to read a merchant's face when he is concealing the price he actually paid. What the smell of an adulterated dye tells you about the supply chain above it. How to trace a discrepancy backward through a ledger to the transaction where the error began, or the fraud, or the carelessness. How to write an analysis that is persuasive to a man who would prefer not to be persuaded by a woman.

These were not the lessons in any book that would be placed before a merchant's daughter. They were the lessons of her particular life, earned through her particular journey, existing nowhere except in her.

She shifted Zhang Wei to a more comfortable position on her shoulder and looked up at the first summer stars appearing over the Zhang compound walls. The plum trees were in full leaf now, the garden warm and fragrant. Inside, she could hear Lady Zhang's voice in the main hall, giving some instruction to a servant with the measured precision she brought to all household matters.

The world her daughter would be born into was the world she was helping to make, one invisible calculation at a time. She was not certain the world would be different in any way she could see. She was certain that knowledge given freely was never entirely wasted, and that a daughter who knew how to calculate an exchange rate and trace a supply chain and close an information gap was a daughter with one more door open than she might otherwise have had.

Outside the ward gate, Chang'an moved through its summer evening, a million people continuing the immense and ongoing business of the greatest city in the world. And in the Zhang compound's garden, Wei Lian sat with her sleeping son and her forming daughter and her private ledger of things worth knowing, and thought about what to pass forward into time.
