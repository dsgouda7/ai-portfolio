Chapter 15: The Voice of the Saint

The cavern entrance was at the waterline, a horizontal slot in the limestone face approximately three meters wide and one meter high at current water level. At low tide, Nacho had told them, the slot opened to about two meters — enough to pass through in a kayak or on the surface with minimal equipment. At high tide it was completely submerged.

They were two hours from low tide when they reached it.

Sofia had already passed through. Her light was visible inside — a pale glow that moved and then steadied, which meant she was in the interior space and standing on something.

Nacho brought the tender in close, moving carefully on the oars.

"The air draft is tight," he said. "We'll have to go through with the tender low — everyone down. Then we can pull through."

Jake crouched in the bow. Arturo crouched behind him. Elena was in the stern with Nacho.

The slot was close enough to touch on both sides. The rock was cold and wet and the smell of the interior was the specific smell of salt water and geology — calcium carbonate and marine biology and geological time. Jake went under the lintel by perhaps ten centimeters, keeping his head absolutely flat, and then the interior space opened around them.

It was larger than he had expected.

The main cavern was perhaps fifteen meters across and eight meters high at its highest point, cathedral-like in its arching limestone ceiling, which was crusted with the specific concretions of a place where seawater and freshwater met and reacted: stalactites and flowstone and the biological accretion of organisms that had found this place habitable for geological eons. A natural rock shelf ran along the north wall at about half a meter above the current water level, dry and level. On the shelf, Sofia was standing with her dive light raised, looking at the walls.

She turned as they entered.

Her expression, illuminated by the dive light from below, was extraordinary.

Not found-it excitement. Not treasure-hunter triumph. Something more serious than either of those, more specifically the expression of a person who has arrived somewhere that changes the scale of what they understood.

"The walls," she said.

Jake pulled himself up onto the rock shelf and looked.

The walls of the cavern were covered with marks. Not natural marks — the irregular pocketing and solution features of the limestone — but deliberate marks, carved with metal tools, in the regular grid pattern of a person who was creating a record. They covered the lower three meters of the north wall in a continuous pattern approximately eight meters long.

They were Latin letters.

Not a cipher — not the encoded text of the Codex. Plain Latin, in a clear upright hand that Jake's eye immediately associated with the writing style in Arturo's photographs of the Codex pages.

Diego de Almeida had carved a message in the wall.

Arturo was in the cavern. He had come over the gunwale of the tender with a speed that his appearance suggested he didn't have, and he was standing on the rock shelf, and he was reading the wall.

He read it for perhaps three minutes while the rest of them held their lights and their breath.

Then he turned. His face in the dive light was the face of a man who has arrived.

"He says—" Arturo stopped. He cleared his throat. He was speaking English, which told Jake he was aware of his audience and choosing to include them all. "'I, Diego de Almeida, courier of the Order, have placed here the trust I was given. I have placed it twice. I will tell no one where it is. I will write the way and the key. The way will be found by a learned person in a future time. The key will be heard by that person in this place.' And then—" His voice had a quality now that was not quite trembling but was close to it in the way that large emotions sit next to trembling without being it. "'Go deeper. The voice will guide you.'"

Nobody said anything for a moment.

Then Arturo said: "We need to wait. The low tide comes in approximately two hours. The full resonance—"

"We might not have two hours," Jake said quietly.

He was listening to the water outside. The sound of a second tender. Other voices, low.

Elena had heard it too. She looked at Jake.

"Montaldo's people," she said.

Jake looked at the cavern entrance. They had passed through it — Montaldo's people were outside, possibly at the entrance now, possibly about to come through.

He thought about the archive. Still below the water — the water level was high enough that whatever was in the chambers was underwater. He could not see into the chambers beyond the main cavern. The platform and the deposit were in deeper water, in chambers that Sofia would need to dive to reach.

He looked at Sofia. "The chambers behind this space — what can you see?"

She had been sweeping her light. "Another passage behind the north wall. I can see the opening — it goes deeper, eastward. The water in it is clear. I can see down maybe ten meters." She paused. "I can see shapes. Things. The wrong kind of shapes for geology."

"Can you go in?"

"I need the equipment from the bag."

"How long to gear up?"

"Three minutes."

Three minutes. Outside, a splash — something or someone entering the water near the cavern entrance.

Jake looked at Nacho, who had come up on the shelf.

"I'll go out and meet them," Nacho said quietly.

"They may be armed," Jake said.

"So might I," Nacho said, with the calm of a man who has spent his life making these assessments on the water.

Jake looked at him. "Don't—"

"I won't start anything," Nacho said. "But I'll finish it if I have to." He looked at the entrance. "You have a few minutes. The approach through the entrance takes time if you're not doing it in a planned way." He stepped carefully back toward the tender.

Then something happened.

It started below threshold — below hearing, the vibration through the rock shelf. Then it became audible: low-frequency, resonant, modal.

The tide was turning early, or the reef configuration was channeling the swell in the specific way that happened before full low tide, or Diego de Almeida had understood something about this cavern's acoustics that none of them had fully anticipated — whatever the reason, the sound began.

It began like breathing, or like the lowest register of a very large pipe organ, or like neither of those things because it was its own thing, specific to itself, and had been its own thing since before there were pipe organs or anyone who breathed. It was the Phrygian dominant mode, or something so close to it that the mind immediately heard the modal structure — the intervals, the raised second, the quality that Arabic theorists had called primordial, before-everything, the tonal expression of geological time.

It filled the cavern.

Arturo closed his eyes.

The sound was not loud but it was total — it seemed to come from the walls, from the floor, from the water, from everywhere simultaneously. It was what happened when the reef channeled a specific tidal frequency into a specific limestone architecture and the architecture responded with its own resonance, and the resonance had been doing this since before the Templars, since before Columbus, since before any human had set foot on this island, and would continue doing it after the archive was recovered and the history was written and the people who had come for it were gone.

Arturo opened his eyes.

"Phrygian dominant," he said softly. "Raised second. F-sharp over E in the modal framework Diego would have used." He was nodding. "The last cipher line. 'Go deeper. The voice will guide you.' The passage into the inner chambers is aligned with the direction of strongest resonance." He looked at the north wall. "Northeast. The strongest signal comes from northeast."

Sofia had her equipment on.

"Northeast," she said. "I see the passage." She put her regulator in. She went into the water.

---

Outside, voices.

Jake could not hear words but he could hear the specific pattern of people trying to be quiet while directing each other — the clipped, whispered direction of a professional team on approach. Two, possibly three people. At the cavern entrance, trying to work out the entry.

He thought about what he had and what they had and whether the arithmetic worked.

What he had: Sofia in the water, close to the chambers. Arturo with a translated cipher and a sound-based key that resolved the final section. Photographs — Sofia's waterproof camera had been going since they entered. A satellite phone.

What he didn't have: time, if Montaldo's people came through the entrance now.

He called Carla Hidalgo on the satellite phone.

She answered on the first ring.

"We're in the cavern," he said.

A pause. "You found it."

"We found it. The archive is in the inner chambers. Sofia Reyes is diving now." He could hear the sound behind his voice, even through the satellite connection — the resonance of the cavern, the low-frequency modality, the voice of the saint. "Montaldo's people are outside the entrance. I need you to call in what you have. Whatever federal maritime authority you've arranged."

"The Coast Guard has a patrol boat in the Windward Passage. I've been coordinating since yesterday." A pause. "What's your position?"

He read her the GPS coordinates from the satellite phone.

"Twenty minutes," she said. "The patrol boat is twenty minutes out."

"I need to know they'll move."

"They'll move," she said. "Jake. The FBI has an arrest warrant for Andrea Montaldo on U.S. territory, specifically the Hialeah kidnapping. Once she enters international waters in confirmed presence with the maritime patrol—"

"Twenty minutes," Jake said.

"Twenty minutes."

He put the phone away and looked at the cavern entrance.

The water in the entrance was moving differently — disturbed. Someone was in the water outside, approaching the slot.

Nacho's voice, from outside: calm, clear, pitched to carry without shouting. Speaking in Italian — Jake didn't speak Italian but he could hear the specific tone of a man stating terms.

Another voice, a woman's, responding.

Nacho again.

A pause.

Then: Nacho, in English, calling in. "They want to enter. I've told them this is a documented archaeological site and they need authorization."

"Does that—" Elena started.

"No," Jake said. "But it bought thirty seconds."

He looked at the water. Sofia's dive light was visible through the passage opening in the north wall, deeper, further back. Moving, but slowly.

He thought: what's she seeing?

He thought: don't rush her. Don't call her back.

He thought: twenty minutes.

He looked at Arturo, who was standing against the wall of the cavern with his notebook open and his expression that of a man who has arrived at a place outside time. The resonance was still going. Arturo was listening to it with his eyes open, nodding very slightly, the nod of a musician finally hearing the performance of a piece they have only read.

"It's right," Arturo said quietly. Not to Jake particularly. To the cave, or to Diego. "The Phrygian dominant. Exactly as described." A pause. "The last cipher line. It's right."

"Can you write it down?"

"I'm writing it down."

He was writing in his notebook. In the resonance. In the cave. In the dark, with dive lights and the specific circumstance of everything converging.

Voices outside the entrance.

Movement in the water at the slot.

Someone was coming through.

Jake moved to the tender and picked up the boat hook, the long aluminum pole for maneuvering in close quarters. He held it the way you hold something when you're not planning to use it as a weapon but haven't ruled it out.

The person who came through the slot was not Andrea Montaldo. It was a man — large, dark-clothed, moving carefully through the water with the practiced efficiency of someone who had trained for exactly this. He got through the slot and stood up in the shallow water near the entrance, and he looked at Jake, and Jake looked at him, and they assessed each other in the specific way of two people who have just arrived at the same junction from different directions.

The man was not holding a weapon. His hands were at his sides. He was wet and breathing hard from the approach.

"Mr. Malone," he said, in English with an Italian accent.

Jake was very still. "You know my name."

"We know many things," the man said. He looked around the cavern — the inscribed wall, the rock shelf, the passage in the north wall where Sofia's light was glowing. Something in his expression when he saw the wall inscriptions: not greed, not triumph. Something that looked more like relief.

Then another person came through the slot.

Jake expected Montaldo.

It was a priest.

A small man in his sixties, wearing dark clothing with the collar of a Roman Catholic priest visible above the wetsuit he'd clearly put on in a hurry. He stood in the water and looked around the cavern with the expression of someone who has been working toward something for a very long time.

"Father Bellini," Arturo said.

The priest looked at Arturo.

"Mr. Vasquez-Cortez," Father Bellini said.

They looked at each other across the resonant dark.

---

What happened next was not what Jake had been preparing for.

Father Giorgio Bellini — small, wet, standing in six inches of Caribbean water in a tidal cave with the resonance of seven hundred years of accumulated faith around him — said to Arturo Vasquez-Cortez:

"We've been looking for this for four hundred years."

And Arturo said: "I know."

And Bellini said: "What condition is the archive in?"

And Arturo said: "We don't know yet. Sofia is in the inner chambers."

And Bellini said: "What was the cipher key?"

And Arturo said: "The Phrygian dominant mode. Exactly as Diego described it."

They stood there in the cave with the sound of it around them and regarded each other, and Jake watched this and thought: they're not enemies.

They are, he realized, the most extreme possible version of colleagues. Two men who had spent different portions of their lives working toward the same object from different directions, one as a scholar and one as an institutional agent, and who had arrived at the object simultaneously and were now discovering that they wanted different things from it but could, possibly, negotiate.

"The archive," Bellini said. "The Holy See's position—"

"The Holy See's position is not my position," Arturo said quietly. "The archive belongs to history. To scholarship. To the world." He paused. "I'm prepared to discuss the appropriate institutional framework for its preservation and study. I'm not prepared to discuss its suppression."

Bellini looked at him. "Suppression is not the Holy See's current position."

Jake and Elena both looked at the priest.

"The current position," Bellini said carefully, "is that the documents in the archive — whatever they specifically contain — are best preserved and studied in a context where their interpretation is not dictated by institutions with political interests. The Holy See has— significant historical reasons for preferring this." He paused. "We do not want these documents destroyed. We do not want them suppressed. We want them in a context that can hold them appropriately."

"The Vatican Library," Arturo said.

"Among possible contexts." Bellini paused. "A joint custody arrangement — the Vatican Archives, the Portuguese National Archives, possibly a third institution with no religious affiliation — has been proposed internally." He looked at the inscribed wall. "We've been discussing the possibility for twenty years. Since we first learned that the Codex Almeida existed and that it might be genuinely translatable."

Jake looked at Arturo.

Arturo looked at the wall where Diego had carved his message.

"This will take more than one conversation," Arturo said.

"Yes," Bellini agreed.

The resonance moved around them all.

Outside, a boat's horn — two short blasts and one long. The Coast Guard signal.

Twenty minutes, almost exactly.
