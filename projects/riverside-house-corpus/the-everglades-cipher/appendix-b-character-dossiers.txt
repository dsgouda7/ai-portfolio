APPENDIX B: CHARACTER DOSSIERS

Complete Character Reference for The Everglades Cipher

---

JAKE MALONE
Private Investigator — Miami, Florida

Age: 44
Physical: Six feet, 185 pounds, the kind of build that is functional rather than maintained. Dark hair going grey at the temples. Brown eyes. The kind of face that is unremarkable until it pays attention to you, at which point it is very remarkable indeed.

Background: Born Miami, 1980. Father was a Dade County building inspector; mother taught third grade at a public school in Flagami. Attended Miami-Dade Community College, transferred to Florida International University, dropped out one semester short of a degree in criminal justice to take a job with a private investigation firm in Coral Gables. Worked as an investigator's assistant for four years, then as a licensed investigator for three years before starting his own practice.

His one significant relationship — a marriage to Kate Hernandez, an airline attendant from Fort Lauderdale — ended after six years, by mutual agreement. The mutual agreement was that Jake was not constitutionally capable of being regularly present, which is a trait that has professional advantages and relationship disadvantages. Kate is now married to a dentist in Fort Lauderdale. Jake has no hard feelings. He has a functional relationship with Kate that is more honest than their marriage was, which is the kind of outcome that takes years of personal accounting to achieve.

He lives on the Reluctant Witness, a 38-foot houseboat at a dock in Coconut Grove. He pays slip fees every month. He has not replaced the banner that flies from the stern — a slightly faded sign reading "J. MALONE — PRIVATE INVESTIGATIONS" — because it works, and replacing things that work is a form of vanity he doesn't have.

Practice: Malone Investigations, operating from a rented office above a dry cleaner on Tigertail Avenue. He does backgrounds, custody investigations, corporate fraud, and the occasional missing person. He has a referral network that has developed over twelve years and that produces enough work to keep him viable. He does not advertise.

Character: Jake is characterized by the professional trait of methodical attention — the habit of not moving until he has more information, and not asking questions until he has a hypothesis about the answer. This patience reads, to people who don't know him, as indolence. To people who do know him, it reads as the specific quality of a man who does not waste motion.

He is morally serious in a way that doesn't perform moral seriousness. He will not take a case he doesn't believe in, and he will not cut a corner that costs someone else. He has cut several corners that cost only himself, which is a different category.

He drinks rum. He drinks it moderately and deliberately, the way other people read, and he would describe it as a meditative practice if he were the kind of person who described things as meditative practices, which he is not.

He is, at the level of deep character, a romantic about the specific proposition that paying attention to the world as it actually is — rather than as it is convenient to believe it is — is both the right approach to professional life and the right approach to personal life. This romanticism is not expressed directly. It is expressed through the choice to keep doing the work.

Notable Cases Prior to this Novel:
— The Estrada divorce matter (Coral Gables, 2019): routine surveillance that became an insurance fraud investigation that became a federal wire fraud referral. Jake has mixed feelings about this one.
— The Caldwell corporate investigation (Doral, 2021): three months of document review and surveillance that established beyond reasonable doubt that a CFO was embezzling. The CFO was the client's husband. The client had suspected and needed to know. She took the evidence to her attorney, not the police, which was her choice to make.
— The Vasquez-Cortez matter (Miami to Caribbean, 2024): the one that will be in the newspaper and, eventually, in someone's academic footnote.

ELENA VASQUEZ-CORTEZ
Molecular Biologist — University of Miami

Age: 55
Physical: Medium height, lean, the posture of someone who has spent decades at lab benches and has compensated with daily running. Dark hair, which she has kept short for twenty years. The look of someone who is always slightly ahead of the conversation.

Background: Born Havana, July 1968. Brought to Miami by Arturo Vasquez-Cortez in 1969, at approximately fourteen months of age. Raised in the apartment above La Luz de los Libros in Little Havana. The only child in the household, and the child of a man who treated her as an intellectual equal from a very young age.

She attended Coral Gables High School, then the University of Miami, where she completed a BS in biochemistry. PhD from the University of California San Francisco in molecular genetics. Postdoctoral fellowship at the Salk Institute. Returned to Miami fifteen years ago to take a faculty position at UM's Department of Molecular and Cellular Pharmacology.

Her research focuses on the genetic mechanisms of cellular stress response — specifically, how cells manage the damage from oxidative stress and whether those mechanisms can be pharmacologically targeted for age-related disease. She has a research group of five, including two graduate students. She is a careful, highly regarded scientist who produces work that is reliable and not flashy, which is exactly what she values.

Personal: Never married. This is not a deficit in her life — she has examined it carefully and determined that the life she has built is complete. She has close friends, a good relationship with her colleagues, and the specific fullness of a person who has made real choices rather than drifting into convention.

Her relationship with Arturo has been the primary relationship of her life, in the sense that it shaped the terms on which she understood family and commitment. It was not without difficulties — she spent years not asking him the questions she needed to ask, because she was protecting him from her asking, and protecting herself from his answers, and this was a form of cost that she has been honest with herself about. The resolution of the Codex investigation freed her, and him, to be honest in ways that their relationship needed and had been deferring.

Relationship with Arturo: What they have is not what a daughter and father have who were biologically related and grew up in an ordinary family. It is something more specific: the relationship of two people who were thrown together by circumstances neither of them chose, who made the best of those circumstances with the best of their natures, and who arrived, at the end of fifty years, at a version of each other that was honest. She loves him. She has always loved him. She was not always capable of saying it directly; she is now.

The Havana discovery: Finding Jorge and Marta Fuentes gave her information she needed and that she is now integrating into her understanding of herself. The biographical fact of Rosa Castillo is present in her thinking the way that an early experimental result is present in a scientist's subsequent research: not directing everything, but informing it, giving the subsequent work a context that changes what it means.

Notable Characteristic: She does not perform emotion. She has feelings and she processes them, and when they are processed they become information, and she acts on them appropriately. This is what Jake Malone saw in her in the first meeting — that she was in grief and in motion simultaneously, which is the specific quality of a person who takes their feelings seriously enough to do something about them.

ARTURO VASQUEZ-CORTEZ
Antiquarian Bookseller — Little Havana, Miami

Age: 78
Physical: Small and precise. White hair that has been white for twenty years. Brown eyes with the specific depth of someone who has spent a lifetime reading very carefully. The hands of a handler of documents: practiced, deliberate.

Background: Born Porto, Portugal, 1946. His family were minor merchants — a general goods shop in the Ribeira district of Porto, selling to the dockers and sailors of the port. He was educated by Jesuits, an education that gave him Latin, precision, and the habit of argument. He came to Havana in the mid-1960s, to a distant cousin who was in the book trade, and spent three years in the Cuban book trade before the political situation made staying untenable.

He left Cuba in October 1969 with fourteen months of Elena and whatever he could pack in two bags. He came to Miami because it was close and because he had a contact there. He established La Luz de los Libros in 1973, in a Little Havana storefront that cost four hundred dollars a month and which he could barely afford, and which he has occupied ever since, the lease having never been broken.

The Codex: In 1989, in Seville, while cataloguing a private library on behalf of a client, he found the Codex Almeida in a box of miscellaneous papers. He recognized it as unusual — the paper, the age, the cipher indicators — within the first hour of examination. He spent three weeks examining it before concluding that it was what it appeared to be: a medieval navigation document, coded, describing the location of a significant archive.

He took it home. He did not tell anyone. He spent thirty-four years decoding it.

The decision not to tell Elena: He has been asked about this and has given different answers at different times, all of which are partially true. The partial truths are: he did not want to raise her hopes about something he couldn't verify; he did not want the Codex to become her problem before it was his solved problem; he wanted to give her the answer, not the question. The complete truth includes these and adds: he was afraid that if he told her, she would ask the question that would open the question about her origins, and he was not ready for that conversation until much later than he should have been.

He knows this. He has accounted for it. He cannot undo it.

Character: Arturo is what happens when a person of high intelligence and deep patience chooses a life that exercises both without public recognition. He is not ambitious in the way that requires an audience. He is ambitious in the way that requires being right. He spent thirty-four years on the Codex because it was worth doing correctly, and he would have spent sixty years if that was what it took.

He is generous with knowledge — every conversation he has is an opportunity to teach, and he is aware that some of his best students have been his customers, who came in for one book and left understanding something they hadn't planned to learn.

He is not sentimental about the Codex now that it is solved. The Codex was a problem. He solved it. The archive was real. The archive is found. The thing that comes next is: what other problems are there?

There are always other problems.

NACHO REYES (Juan Carlos Reyes)
Dive Charter Operator — Big Pine Key, Florida

Age: 62
Physical: The body of a man who has spent forty years in the water: compact, brown, with the specific economy of movement of someone who has been conserving energy in difficult conditions for a long time. Grey-streaked hair. The face of the Keys — weathered in the way that Keys residents are weathered, which is not ugly but is also not something you could call young.

Background: Born Key West, 1962, to a family that had been in the Keys for three generations on the Cuban side. His grandfather Ramon Reyes came to the Keys in 1940, one year before the 1941 expedition, from a commercial fishing life in Havana. Ramon had worked for the Santiesteban family's commercial fishing operations in the late 1930s, which is how he came to be on the 1941 expedition to the Caribbean islands.

Nacho's father, Mario Reyes, ran a commercial dive operation from Islamorada until his death in 1991. Nacho worked with his father from the age of fifteen, earned his commercial dive certification at nineteen, and took over the business when his father died. He moved the operation to Big Pine Key, which has better access to the backcountry and the less-trafficked reef sections, and has run it from there for thirty-three years.

The island knowledge: His grandfather told him about the 1941 expedition in 1970, when Nacho was eight. Ramon was dying — a slow cancer — and he wanted the knowledge to continue. He told Nacho the story in installments over several weeks: the Santiesteban family, the expedition, the Caribbean island, the sound, the cliff, the feeling that something was there. He did not describe it as a mystery or a treasure hunt. He described it as something he had heard and had never been able to explain, and which he wanted someone else to know about, in case the explanation ever became available.

Nacho kept this for forty-two years before making the call to Arturo.

His Daughter: Sofia Maria Reyes was born 1991, and has been her father's student in everything that matters since she was twelve. She has her father's patience and her grandfather's attention to the way water behaves, plus the formal scientific education that the preceding generations lacked. Nacho regards her professional trajectory — marine archaeology at the University of Miami — as the correct outcome for the intersection of family capability and historical circumstance.

Character: Nacho is a man of precision without pedantry. He describes things accurately because inaccuracy in his profession has costs — misdescribing a current or a depth can result in injury or death. This accuracy has become, over forty years, a general habit: he says what is true, not what is comfortable, and he expects the same from the people around him.

He is at home in water in the way that some people are at home in libraries. The ocean is his working environment and also his contemplative one — the place where he processes things. His logbooks are the record of a man who has been paying attention to the same system for thirty years and who has built, through patient accumulation, an understanding of it that no single expedition could achieve.

He is private about the family story — not secretive, but private. He did not speak of it publicly before this case and does not speak of it publicly after it, except in the context of the formal historical record. The story belongs to the record now. He belongs to his work.

SOFIA REYES
Marine Archaeologist — University of Miami (Graduate Student)

Age: 33
Physical: Her father's compact economy of movement. Her mother's face — her mother, who died when Sofia was fourteen, and who was from Marathon, and who Nacho still talks about on the anniversaries. Brown hair that she wears short for practical reasons. The specific competence of a person who is very good at the physical dimensions of their work.

Background: Born Big Pine Key, 1991. Grew up in her father's dive operation, in the water from age eight, certified at fourteen, commercial dive certificate at nineteen. Worked commercial diving — offshore construction, pipeline inspection, salvage — for eight years before deciding that she wanted the academic context for what she was already doing in practice. Applied to the marine archaeology graduate program at UM at thirty-one.

Her academic work combines the practical competence of a commercial diver with the analytical rigor of formal archaeological methodology. She is fast and precise underwater — she processes observation and makes decisions in the way of someone who has been making decisions underwater for twenty years — and she is bringing that practical intelligence to the academic discipline of site analysis and documentation.

The Cayo Espiritu paper is her first major academic contribution. The follow-on work — the Mediterranean acoustic survey hypothesis — is what she is developing for her dissertation.

Character: Sofia is her father's daughter in temperament: direct, efficient, patient in the way of someone who has learned patience from the water. She has no tolerance for pretension and no interest in playing the social games of academic life that have nothing to do with the quality of the work. She is building a reputation for exactly this: clarity about what the evidence shows and precision about what it doesn't.

She had a romantic relationship that ended two years ago, for reasons that both parties described as the inevitable outcome of two people with very high professional demands who were unable to align those demands. She has not found this difficult to accept. She is twenty-three, which is a decade ahead of when most people figure out what they're doing, and she has things to find.

FATHER GIORGIO BELLINI
Custodes Veritatis — Rome (Vatican)

Age: 67
Physical: Medium height, slight, the build of a man who is physically unremarkable and has learned to use this. Dark eyes. The hands of a man who has handled many documents. A face that is disciplined in its expressiveness — it shows exactly what he intends to show and nothing else.

Background: Born Florence, 1957. Entered a Jesuit novitiate at twenty-two. Academic formation in Rome — Gregorian University, PhD in medieval ecclesiastical history, specializing in the twelfth through fourteenth centuries. Recruited into what he describes as "research coordination" — the Custodes Veritatis — in the mid-1990s, when it became clear that his academic expertise and his institutional discretion made him useful for a specific function.

His function: to monitor and, when appropriate, engage with research that might touch on materials of historical sensitivity to the Church. This is not, in his description, about suppression. It is about ensuring that when significant historical materials emerge, the Church is part of the scholarly process rather than reactive to it. He has spent thirty years building a network of contacts in the academic world that allows him to know about significant finds before they become public controversies.

His engagement with the Vasquez-Cortez case began approximately three years before the events of this novel, when a contact at the Simancas archive in Valladolid reported that a Miami bookseller had been requesting access to the medieval Portuguese navigation records with increasing frequency and with increasingly specific requests. Bellini began monitoring, with the patience of an organization that operates on institutional timescales.

Character: Bellini is genuine. This is the most important thing to understand about him, because the institutions he represents have a history that makes genuineness in their agents easy to doubt. He genuinely believes that the archive's contents deserve scholarly study, full public access, and honest presentation. He genuinely believes that the Church's interest in the archive is preservation, not control. He is aware that his organization's historical record does not support this belief, and he has spent his career working to be the version of his organization that the belief describes.

He is not naive about institutional behavior. He knows that the pressures toward suppression exist within his organization and that they are not entirely unreasonable — some of the archive's theological documents will be genuinely difficult for the Church's self-presentation. He believes this difficulty should be faced honestly rather than avoided. He believes this because he is an historian, and he knows what the historical record says about the outcome of avoidance.

His personal religious conviction is simple and robust: he believes, and he knows that belief does not require historical innocence. The complicated history of the Church, of which this case is one example, does not undermine his faith. It is part of the faith, as all human complication is.

MAGDALENA SANTIESTEBAN
Businesswoman / Former Organized Crime Figure — Coral Gables, Miami

Age: 81
Physical: Small, very upright, with the bearing of a woman who was raised to hold herself correctly and has maintained this as a form of respect for the person who raised her. White hair. The eyes of someone who has been assessing people for sixty years.

Background: Born Miami, 1943, to the first American-born generation of the Santiesteban family. Her grandfather Ramon Francisco Santiesteban y Vidal had come from Havana in 1938; his son (her father) Eduardo had been born in Havana in 1935 and came to Miami with Ramon in 1938. Eduardo grew the family's commercial fishing operations into a broader set of business interests in the post-war Miami economy — import-export, real estate, some activities that were not legal and which the family does not discuss in detail.

Magdalena took over the family business operations at thirty-one, when her father died. She had two advantages: considerable intelligence and the family name, which in the Cuban-American business community of Miami in the 1970s meant access to relationships that others didn't have. She used these advantages to build a legitimate business portfolio while managing a transition away from the illegitimate activities, which took a decade and required sacrificing some short-term profitability for long-term security.

By the time of this novel, the Santiesteban family's business interests are entirely legitimate: real estate, commercial fishing (still, still the original business), and several investments in the Miami hotel and restaurant sector. She is wealthy in the way that several generations of intelligent accumulation produces: not ostentatiously wealthy, but permanently wealthy, which is different.

The island: She was told about Cayo Espiritu when she was nine years old, by her grandfather on what she understood was his deathbed (he recovered, but barely, and she understood that he had told her because he needed to tell someone). He told her: find a way to protect it. She has spent sixty years trying. None of the paths she tried — acquisition, development, conservation — worked until this case.

Character: Magdalena is a woman who is accustomed to patience being a form of power. In the business world and in the legal world and in the Miami Cuban-American political world, she has learned that the party who can wait longest frequently wins. She has waited longest in multiple contexts.

She is fair. She does not take things she has not earned. She does not allow things she has earned to be taken. These are clear principles that she has applied without modification for sixty years.

She is not sentimental, but she is not cold. The distinction is that she has organized her feelings around what is real rather than what is comfortable, which means that when sentiment is warranted — as it is in the conversation with Arturo about her grandfather — she gives it its full weight.

She is well past the age at which she needs to perform strength. She is strong; she knows she is strong; she doesn't need anyone's confirmation.

CARLA HIDALGO
Senior Detective — Miami-Dade Police Department, Missing Persons / Homicide Liaison

Age: 41
Physical: Medium height, athletic, with the posture of someone who does not sit at a desk any more than she has to. Black hair. The face that detective work produces: not harsh, but calibrated — aware at all times of what information is being given and received.

Background: Born Hialeah, 1983. The daughter of a Miami-Dade police officer (father) and a paralegal (mother). She grew up inside the mechanics of the legal system in a way that made her decision to join the department feel inevitable to everyone who knew her. Miami-Dade Police Academy at twenty-two. Detective grade at twenty-eight. Working the border between missing persons and homicide that the department's liaison unit was created to staff.

She is good at her job in the way that comes from caring about accuracy. She does not build cases to reach conclusions; she builds cases to find out. When the evidence doesn't support the preferred conclusion, she says so. This has not always made her popular. It has made her reliable.

Her working relationship with Jake Malone is one of a small number of professional relationships she maintains with private investigators — she maintains these relationships because they are useful, and she is careful about keeping them appropriate. Jake is the one she trusts most, which means she gives him information she would not give other PI contacts and expects him to behave accordingly. He does.

Character: Carla is professional in a way that is not mechanical. The professionalism is a choice she renews daily — a decision to do the job right, made again each morning, not a habit that runs automatically. This renewable quality is what keeps her from the specific exhaustion that police work produces in people who are trying to be moral inside a system that is not consistently moral.

She has a partner — a female detective named Rodriguez who she has worked with for three years and who is as good as she is — and she has a life outside the job that she protects fiercely from the job's tendency to consume everything.

She is, at the bottom of things, motivated by the same thing that motivates Jake Malone: the belief that finding out what actually happened is inherently important. Not for justice, necessarily — justice is what the system does with the finding, and the system is imperfect. But for the record. For the people who needed to know. For the world's accountability to itself.

TOMMY OKEECHOBEE
Environmental Attorney and Miccosukee Elder — Miami/Everglades

Age: 55
Physical: The specific bearing of someone who is simultaneously an elder and a professional — the authority of both, worn lightly. Black hair, braided. The hands of someone who works outdoors. An expression of chronic assessment: she is always reading the land around her.

Background: Born in the Everglades, 1969, on the Miccosukee Tribe's territory along the Tamiami Trail. Her father's family had been in the Big Cypress watershed for generations before the formal establishment of the Miccosukee reservation; her mother was from the Seminole tribe in Oklahoma, which gave her a pan-tribal perspective on federal Indian law that proved professionally useful.

University of Florida Law School. Federal Indian law and environmental law practice from 1994. A member of the Miccosukee Tribe's legal department and independently consulted for environmental organizations across South Florida.

Her involvement in this case is the most recent layer of a lifelong engagement with the ecological and legal status of the Everglades and surrounding waters. She has litigated against drainage projects, development permits, and the Army Corps of Engineers at various times in her career. She knows the Everglades watershed as a system — hydrologically, ecologically, legally — in the way that comes from forty years of paying attention to one thing.

Character: Tommy is precise about language in the way of a lawyer and also in the way of someone who has seen what happens when language about land is imprecise — the wrong word in a legal document, and forty square miles of wet prairie become developable. She uses words like a surgeon uses instruments: the right one, in the right place, with the minimum necessary force.

She is patient with people who are trying to understand what the land means and impatient with people who are using the land's existence for purposes that have nothing to do with the land itself. She has a long experience distinguishing between the two.

Her reference to the cypress planting in her note to Arturo — We planted cypress last week. It takes a while, but it grows — is the most direct statement of her general philosophy that appears in this novel.

ROBERTO SANCHEZ
Retired Postal Worker / Neighbor — Little Havana, Miami

Age: 72
Physical: Stocky, with the slow deliberate movement of a man who has been working with his hands for fifty years and who has learned to conserve effort intelligently. Dark eyes. A garden around his house that is the best garden on the block.

Background: Born Havana, 1952. Came to Miami in 1970 at eighteen, part of the sustained Cuban emigration of that era. His uncle was already in Little Havana and found him work. He started at the Hialeah postal facility in 1971 and worked there for thirty-four years before retirement.

He moved into the house three blocks from La Luz de los Libros in 1978 and has been in that house ever since. His connection to Arturo is the connection of neighbors who have known each other for forty years: not intimate, but deep in the specific way that neighborhood knowledge is deep. He knows what Arturo does and respects it. Arturo knows that Roberto's garden is the best one in the neighborhood and has told him so, more than once, which Roberto has received as the accurate observation it is.

The envelope: He had been given the envelope to keep by a friend who was leaving Miami in a hurry in 2019, and who had been given it by someone else before that, and so on — a chain of quiet keepers who kept it because they had been asked to by someone they trusted and who did not ask questions. He kept it in a desk drawer for five years. When Elena came to him, he gave it to her immediately, without hesitation, because he understood immediately what kind of situation it was.

Character: Roberto is the person in this novel who most directly embodies the Ruskin Bond quality the author's note describes: an ordinary person with an ordinary life who makes a dignified and significant choice when a significant choice is required of him. He does not seek significance. He does not need to be acknowledged. He gave the envelope, kept the garden, and went on with his life.

He thinks about things. He thinks about them carefully and then acts on the conclusions. He thinks about his grandmother a great deal, because she was often right.

His final scene in the novel — sitting in Arturo's shop, talking about his grandmother, about the island story, about careful patient work becoming something — is the scene that most directly states the novel's thematic proposition, because he states it in the terms of his own life, which are ordinary terms, and which are thereby more credible than the same proposition stated in extraordinary terms.
