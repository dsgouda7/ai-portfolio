Chapter 1: The Distance Between Grief and Business

The houseboat had a name: The Reluctant Witness. Jake Malone had not named it. The previous owner, a retired immigration judge named Horace Seffner, had nailed the plank above the cabin door sometime in the early nineties, and the letters had weathered to the color of old teeth in the years since. Jake kept the name because he found it accurate.

He was sitting on the rear deck with a glass of Tres Generaciones and the remnants of a Tuesday when she knocked on the dock gate. The gate was latched but not locked, because in twelve years of living in Coconut Grove Marina, nothing had ever been stolen from his dock. Miami had its own taxonomy of crime, and houseboat decks in the shadow of old banyans weren't in any thief's map.

"Mr. Malone?"

She was thirty-something, maybe thirty-four. Dark hair pulled back in the way that suggests efficiency rather than vanity. Linen pants and a cream blouse that had started the morning pressed and were now losing the argument with the humidity. She wore no jewelry except small gold studs in her ears that caught the dock light like captured stars. She was carrying a manila folder against her chest the way you carry something you are afraid to put down.

Jake set his glass on the cooler he used as a side table.

"Depends on what you need," he said.

"My father is missing. I was told you find people."

"I find some people. Pull that gate. Watch the second board, it's soft."

She navigated the gate and the soft board with the focus of someone accustomed to solving small problems. She stopped at the foot of the short gangway to the deck. Behind her, the marina hummed with the quiet industry of evening — water slapping fiberglass hulls, a television murmuring from inside the Catalina three slips down, the distant amber wash of Brickell towers across the bay.

"Elena Vasquez-Cortez," she said. "I'm a researcher at the University of Miami. Molecular biology. My father is Arturo Vasquez-Cortez. He owns a bookshop on Calle Ocho. He has been missing for seven days."

"Seven days is a police matter, Ms. Vasquez-Cortez."

"I know. I filed a report with Miami-Dade six days ago. Detective Carla Hidalgo has the case. She's been thorough and professional and has nothing." She looked at the folder in her arms. "I was told you used to be a detective. New York. That you're good with cases that don't fit the usual patterns."

"Who told you that?"

"Roberto Sanchez. He runs a botanica on Flagler. He said you helped him find his nephew two years ago. The nephew who everyone else said was in Costa Rica."

Roberto. Jake had indeed found that nephew — not in Costa Rica but in a Hialeah warehouse, quite alive, quite employed by people who were less enthused about his extraction. He hadn't billed Roberto for the three stitches.

"Come sit down," Jake said. "Can I get you something?"

"Water, please."

He went below and came back with a glass of cold water and a second Tres Generaciones for himself, which he then reconsidered and left on the ledge of the cabin door. He watched Elena Vasquez-Cortez take the folding chair he kept on the deck for clients — he always thought of it as the client chair, though sometimes it was just a place to rest a fishing rod — and settle herself in it with the practiced stillness of someone who had recently learned to suppress panic.

"Tell me about your father," he said.

She opened the folder. Inside was a single photograph, a man in his late sixties, stocky, with thick white hair and the kind of large-framed glasses that belonged to another decade. He was standing in a bookshop, one hand resting on a shelf, smiling at whoever held the camera with the specific warmth of a man at ease in his own domain. He looked like a professor or a priest. He looked like someone's father.

"He came to Miami from Havana in nineteen sixty-two," Elena said. "He was seven years old. His parents brought almost nothing. His father, my grandfather, had been a librarian at the Universidad de La Habana. Books were the family religion." She said this without irony. "My father built La Luz de los Libros — that's the shop — over thirty years. It's part of the cultural fabric of Little Havana. He knows everybody and everybody knows him. When he didn't open on a Tuesday morning, his neighbor, a woman named Sonia Rios who runs the café next door, called me. She said Sonia knew right away something was wrong because Arturo always opened Tuesday mornings by eight and always bought a café con leche from her first."

Jake folded his hands. "He lives above the shop?"

"Apartment on the second floor. I have a key. I went straight there. His bed was made. His coffee maker was clean and put away. His reading glasses were on the kitchen table, which matters because he can't read without them. No phone. No wallet. But his shoes — his good shoes, the ones he wore every day — were in the closet." She paused. "He left without his shoes."

Jake absorbed this. The shoes mattered. The shoes were the kind of detail that a person who was leaving voluntarily would not leave behind, because leaving behind your shoes is not something people do. Not in any scenario that ends well.

"The police report noted the shoes?" he asked.

"Detective Hidalgo noted them. She said it could mean he left in a hurry, possibly under duress, possibly voluntarily but disoriented. She looked into his recent medical history. He has high blood pressure but no cognitive issues. His doctor confirmed this."

"Finances?"

"Stable. The shop doesn't make him rich but it makes him comfortable. He owns the building — bought it in nineteen ninety-four when the neighborhood was different. It's worth considerably more now, but he'd never sell." She turned a page in the folder. "There's no evidence of large debts or gambling or any of the things Detective Hidalgo asked about. He had no known enemies. He had no conflicts with neighbors, suppliers, or customers that anyone knew of."

"What about his research?"

She looked up sharply. "What do you mean?"

"You said he was a bookman. Forty years of handling rare books, you develop interests. Did he have a particular focus? A project he was working on?"

She was quiet for a long moment. The water barely moved in the marina. Somewhere across the bay a container ship was moving north, its lights strung like a low-flying constellation.

"He was working on something," she said carefully. "For about the last three years, he'd become increasingly consumed by a specific area of research. Medieval maritime history. The Knights Templar. Spanish and Portuguese expeditions to the Caribbean in the period before the official discovery." She said the last phrase with audible skepticism. "Before Columbus."

Jake said nothing. He let the silence do its work.

"I know how it sounds," she continued. "He's not a crank. He was trained as an archivist before he became a bookseller. He acquired a manuscript in Seville in nineteen eighty-nine — he went to a library sale, one of those extraordinary events that happen once every decade when some European estate donates a collection — and among the items he purchased was what he believed to be a fragment of a fifteenth-century document with annotations in an earlier hand. He had it authenticated by a professor at the University of Florida. The professor said the paper stock was consistent with fourteenth-century Iberian provenance. The ink analysis was inconclusive."

"What was in the document?"

"That's what he spent thirty years trying to understand. It was in a mixture of Latin, Castilian, and what he believed to be a cipher — a systematic code overlaid on the text. He made progress slowly. He had limited resources. He wasn't a scholar by training, he was a bookseller. But he was methodical and patient." She smoothed the photograph with one finger. "About eighteen months ago, he wrote me a letter — he still writes letters, proper ones, with stamps — telling me he thought he had identified the cipher. He said he was close to being able to read the full document. He called it the Codex Almeida."

"Almeida. A name?"

"He believed the document was written by or for a Portuguese navigator named Diego de Almeida. A historical figure, apparently, though minor — there's one mention of him in a Portuguese maritime chronicle. My father believed this man was connected to the Knights Templar and had made at least one voyage to the Caribbean before fourteen ninety-two."

Jake watched a pelican settle on a pier post in the outer dock, prehistoric and patient. "And did he read it? The full document."

"I don't know. His letters stopped three months ago. I called, I visited — he seemed preoccupied but not alarmed. He said he was at a delicate stage. He asked me not to press him." She closed the folder. "I wish I had pressed him."

"Who else knew about this research?"

"His neighbor Sonia Rios knew he was working on something historical — she called it 'his da Vinci Code thing,' which he found insulting. A woman named Dr. Constance Fairweather — she's a medieval historian at Oxford — had been in correspondence with him for at least a year. He mentioned her in letters. And there was a man he'd started meeting with in the weeks before he disappeared. I don't know his name, but Sonia saw him twice. She described him as a large Cuban man, well-dressed, perhaps late fifties or early sixties. He arrived by car both times, a black SUV. The second time, a week before my father disappeared, they argued. She heard raised voices through the wall."

Jake picked up the Tres Generaciones after all.

"The Oxford historian," he said. "Dr. Fairweather. Have you contacted her?"

"I tried. The Oxford history department gave me her voicemail. I left two messages. She hasn't responded." Elena met his eyes. "She flew to Miami three days before my father disappeared. I checked the university's visitor log because he'd given her name to the library as a researcher who could access his reserved materials. She arrived on a Wednesday. He disappeared on a Saturday. I don't know when she left, or if she left."

Jake drank his rum. The pelican lifted off from its post and vanished into the soft dark above the marina.

"My rate is six hundred a day plus expenses," he said. "I need two days retainer to start."

"I can write a check tonight."

"Cash is better. There's an ATM at the Shell station on Mary Street, it's two minutes from here." He stood. "Come back when you have the cash and I'll have you sign a service agreement. I'll start tomorrow morning." He paused. "One more thing. The man your father argued with. Did your neighbor get anything else? Build, distinguishing features, any part of what was said?"

"She said he had a scar under his left eye. A curved one, like a crescent. She said he looked like someone used to being listened to."

Jake nodded slowly. "All right."

After Elena left — she returned forty minutes later with twelve hundred dollars and signed the agreement at the small table in his galley — Jake sat back on the deck in the deeper dark and listened to the water negotiate with the hulls around him. A man had vanished without his shoes. An Oxford historian was flying to Miami to visit a bookshop owner who was researching fourteenth-century Templars. A large, scar-faced Cuban man had argued with the missing man a week before his disappearance.

He reached for his yellow legal pad — he kept three of them, always in the same places, one in the cabin, one in the glove compartment of his Bronco, one in the office he rented above a dry cleaner on Tigertail — and wrote three words at the top of the first page.

ARTURO. OXFORD. ALMEIDA.

Below that he wrote: Before Columbus.

He looked at the words for a while. Then he added one more, which was less a word than a question:

Why now?

Because whatever Arturo Vasquez-Cortez had been researching for thirty years — whatever secret lay locked in the Codex Almeida — someone had apparently decided that now was the time to do something about it. Not decades ago when the old bookseller first acquired his mysterious manuscript. Not when he began corresponding with an Oxford medievalist. Now.

That was the shape of the case. Not an accident, not a random disappearance, not a medical episode. Something had changed recently. Something had happened that moved someone from patience to action.

Jake looked across the bay at the Brickell towers blazing against the black sky.

Miami had its own grammar of violence, its own syntax of want. He had lived in this city long enough to read it. And what this case was beginning to say, in its own dialect of missing shoes and Oxford letters and fourteen-century ciphers, was: someone found something first.

He wrote that down too: Found something first.

Then he went below to sleep, which he did, as he always did since the divorce, in the way of a man who has stopped fighting insomnia and simply waits for it to finish with him.

HISTORICAL INTERLUDE — Paris, October 1307

The city smelled of the Seine and roasting meat and the particular anxiety of a populace that knows something is about to happen but does not know precisely what.

Diego de Almeida was twenty-three years old and had been in Paris for eleven days when the arrests began. He was not French. He was not a Templar knight, not yet, though he had passed his first novitiate in the order's commandery at Tomar, in Portugal, and wore the white mantle only in private. He was what the order called a sergeant-brother, though in the informal hierarchy that governed his specific function he was something rarer and more dangerous: a courier.

He had come to Paris carrying three things. The first was a letter of introduction to the Master of the Temple from the Grand Commander at Tomar. The second was a vial of consecrated oil that he wore on a cord around his neck, not for any sacramental purpose but because its casing was hollow and the hollow contained a folded piece of vellum no larger than a man's palm, on which were written fourteen symbols in a hand Diego did not recognize. The third thing he carried was an instruction, given to him orally by a man whose name he had not been told, in a room in Tomar where the lamps had been shuttered: If you arrive and the white banners do not fly above the Temple, you go to the harbor at Marseille. You find the ship called the Santa Providência. You tell the captain: The Fisherman's cross is moved south. And then you do whatever the captain tells you.

He had arrived in Paris on a Thursday. The banners above the Temple had flown white. He had delivered his letter and been assigned to wait in the guesthouse of the Paris commandery, a narrow building of cold stone two streets from the main fortifications, smelling of tallow and men who prayed at hours that ordinary citizens slept through.

On Friday, the thirteenth, in the hours before the city woke, he heard horses.

In that world, before light on a Friday morning, horses meant soldiers. And the quantity of horses Diego heard — their hooves on the wet stone of the street outside, the jangling of harnesses, the clipped commands being issued just below the register of windows — meant not soldiers but an army.

He was dressed before the first crash reached him.

The crash was a gate. Not his gate — the main gate of the Temple. He heard it from two streets away as clearly as if it had been the door to his own room, perhaps because he had been lying awake for three hours listening to Paris breathe with that specific wrongness that cities breathe when something has been arranged.

By the time he reached his window the street below was full of royal officers, torches, and the kind of focused, practiced movement that speaks of a prepared force executing a prepared plan. He saw three Templar knights being brought out under guard. One of them was Brother Etienne, who had given Diego his letter of introduction. Brother Etienne was walking with his hands bound and his head held in the precise position of a man who has decided that dignity is the last thing left.

Diego pulled on his boots. He removed the cord with the vial from around his neck and tucked the vial into the lining of his boot, where the folded vellum had its own small pocket that had been sewn there in Tomar for exactly this eventuality. He took nothing else. He left by the rear of the guesthouse, through the kitchen garden, over a wall, and into the warren of streets that Paris kept in permanent shadow between its river and its churches.

He walked south.

He walked all that day and through the night, south along the Rhône valley, in the company of pilgrims going to Avignon, among whom he was simply one more dusty young man with worn boots and a serious expression. He ate bread a farmer's wife gave him in exchange for cutting wood. He slept in a barn. He did not stop.

He reached Marseille on the eleventh day.

The Santa Providência was a two-masted caravel with Portuguese registry, lying at anchor in the outer harbor, and the captain was a man named Fernão Pires who was built like a coastal cliff and spoke to Diego for two minutes before telling him: You sleep on the ship tonight. Tomorrow at dawn we sail. Don't ask where. Don't ask when we come back. Don't ask anything until I tell you to ask.

Diego did not ask. He climbed the gangway of the Santa Providência in the last light of a November evening in 1307 and watched Marseille shrink behind him as the ship moved south into the dark water, and he thought about Brother Etienne walking with his hands bound and his head held up, and he thought about the fourteen symbols on the piece of vellum in his boot, and he thought: we are carrying something they want very badly.

Then he stopped thinking about it, because thinking about things that were trying to kill you was less useful than watching the sea for weather, and the sea, at least, he understood.
