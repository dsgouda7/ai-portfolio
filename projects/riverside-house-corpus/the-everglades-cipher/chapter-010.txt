Chapter 10: Elena and Arturo

Elena drove to wherever Jake told her to bring the car, which was a Publix parking lot in Westchester, neutral and anonymous, full of the ordinary commerce of a Wednesday afternoon. She was there when they arrived. She was out of the car before it stopped.

Jake watched this reunion from a distance of about forty feet, which felt like the right distance for what was not his to witness. He saw Arturo get out of Tommy's truck and stop. Saw Elena stop six feet from him. Saw the six feet close. Heard nothing, because he had walked to the far side of the parking lot and stood with his back to them and gave them the minutes they needed.

Tommy came to stand beside him. She was watching the parking lot's traffic with the same attention she applied to the canal: systematically, without drama.

"She didn't know how much she needed to see him," Tommy said.

"No," Jake said.

"Neither did he."

They stood there for a while. Jake watched a family load groceries into an SUV and thought, as he often did at such moments, about what ordinary life looked like from the outside when you were standing in the middle of something not ordinary at all.

After ten minutes, Elena came to find him. Her face had been rearranged by the last fifteen minutes — not softer, exactly, but more settled. The controlled competence was still there, the scientist's precision, but it sat now over something warmer.

"He's eating," she said. "I had water and a sandwich in the car. He's eating and crying at the same time, which is not a combination I expected from him."

"Stress does that," Jake said.

"He told me he loves me," she said. "He says he says it all the time. He doesn't. He says 'te quiero' at the end of phone calls, which is affectionate but formal. Today he said it the other way." She paused. "The way you say it when you thought you might not get to say it again."

Jake said nothing.

"He also told me about the adoption." She looked at the ground. "He apologized. He said he had been finding reasons. He asked me to forgive him."

"Did you?"

"I told him I had known for years and had been waiting for him to bring it up." A brief pause. "He looked very guilty. I think I might have felt more vindicated about that than I should have."

Jake almost smiled. "He mentioned that being stubborn is different from remarkable."

She looked at him. "Did he say that about himself?"

"He did."

"He's both," she said. "He's stubborn and remarkable and he kept an enormous secret from me for thirty years and when this is over I am going to have a long and complicated conversation with him about honesty and family." She paused. "But not today."

Jake nodded. "Today he needs a doctor, a shower, and about sixteen hours of sleep."

"I have the doctor arranged." She had her phone in her hand. "She's a friend from the university — physician, discreet, no need to involve hospital systems if we want to keep a low profile for a day or two. Will that work with Detective Hidalgo's investigation?"

"I've told Carla he's found and safe. She wants a statement, but she agreed to give him twenty-four hours." Jake looked at the parking lot. Arturo was visible through Elena's car window, still eating. "The people who had him the second time — they'll know he's gone. If they were connected to whatever network tracked him from the Santiesteban situation to the fish camp, they'll be working to find him again."

"So he can't go home."

"Not yet."

Elena nodded. "My apartment. It's in Coral Gables. The building has security, proper security, not a doorman who buzzes you in. Two exits. I know all of my neighbors and none of them know anything about this." She looked at Jake. "Will that work?"

"For now. Don't tell anyone where you are. Tell your department you're taking emergency personal leave." He paused. "Does your building have a secure parking garage?"

"Yes. Underground, coded entry."

"Good. You'll use it." He turned to face her. "The people who had him are connected to people who have Vatican resources, or something that operates like Vatican resources. They are professional and they are motivated by institutional interest, which is often more durable than personal interest." He paused. "They know the island's name now, probably. If they don't, they'll deduce it from other information they've gathered. They no longer need Arturo to find the island. They may still need him to fully decode the final section of the Codex."

Elena processed this. "But if they have the island's name and location, and Fairweather has the translation—"

"They'd want Fairweather too." Jake looked at her. "Call Fairweather. Tell her to go nowhere. Tell her I'll call her in an hour."

Elena made the call while Jake returned to the car and spent three minutes with Arturo, who was, as described, eating a sandwich and crying simultaneously, with the specific dignity of a person who accepts that some emotional responses are ungovernable and doesn't apologize for them. He was wearing his own shoes and this seemed to have restored something fundamental.

"The letter you wrote me," Jake said. "You asked me to find you and to take care of Elena."

Arturo looked at him. The crumbs of the sandwich were in his beard. He had the look of someone who was, in this moment, entirely in the present — not the studied calm of someone suppressing the past few days but the specific presence of a person who has survived something and is grateful for the particularity of survival.

"You did both," Arturo said.

"I had a lot of help."

"The Keys man," Arturo said. "Nacho. You found him."

"He found me, partly. His grandfather worked for yours."

"I know. I know who Nacho is." Arturo looked out the window at the parking lot. "I've been protecting that connection — not telling the Santiestebans about him. They know about the first expedition but they don't know the connection still exists." He paused. "Nacho's grandfather was a good man. He didn't tell them about the sound."

"He told Nacho."

"Yes." Arturo looked at him. "And Nacho has been to the cavern."

"Three times."

Arturo closed his eyes briefly and when he opened them there was something in them that was not exhaustion and not grief but the opposite — a kind of aching recognition, the recognition of someone who has spent thirty years working toward a thing and has just received confirmation that the thing is entirely real.

"He heard it," Arturo said. "The sound."

"He heard it."

Arturo was quiet for a moment. The parking lot went about its business around them, oblivious.

"I've worked on the Codex for thirty-four years," he said softly. "When I decoded the first section — the cipher, the first stage — I was fifty-two years old. I sat at my desk in the shop and I held the document in my hands and I understood, for the first time, what Diego de Almeida had meant. Not the history — not yet. But the intention. The size of the intention. A man eight hundred years ago who put everything he knew into a form that would survive." He paused. "That's what books are. Or what they should be. Not just what we knew at the time. What we intended to pass on."

Jake let this sit for a moment.

"The final section," Jake said. "The verification cipher. The acoustic key."

"I think — even without being on the island, I think I'm close. I've been working on the tonal notation system that Diego used. It's elegant. He took the notation system from Arab music theory — the intervals, the modes — and overlaid it on the cipher. The key is: you hear the sound, you identify its modal structure, and the modal structure gives you the cipher's second key." Arturo looked at Jake. "I've been working backward from everything else I know about the island's geology — the cavern structure, the reef configuration that would produce certain resonant frequencies. I think I know what the sound sounds like. I think the key resolves to the Phrygian mode, the third mode of the major scale. But I need to confirm it." He paused. "By being there."

"I know someone who can take you there," Jake said.

Arturo looked at him.

"Captain Ignacio Reyes," Jake said. "Big Pine Key. He runs a forty-two-foot dive charter called La Determinada."

Arturo was very still for a moment.

"Nacho," he said.

"Yes."

"He would take us?"

"He's been waiting thirty-one years to go back with the right people."

Arturo Vasquez-Cortez sat in the passenger seat of his daughter's car in a Westchester parking lot with an empty sandwich bag in his lap and his own shoes on his feet and looked out the window at the irreducible fact of being alive in the world, which, when it has been in question, has a quality of radiance that is hard to describe without being maudlin about it, and he thought about an island in the Windward Passage called Cayo Espiritu and the sound of water in a cavern at high tide and seven hundred years of patient waiting.

"Let me see Elena," he said. "And then let me sleep. And then—" He looked at Jake. "And then let's go."

---

Carla Hidalgo took Arturo's statement at Elena's apartment the following morning, and to Jake's eye she did it with the particular economy of a detective who knows that the important information is the structure, not the details, and who gets the structure in four questions.

The four questions were: who took you, where were you, what did they want, and what did you tell them.

Arturo's answers were: Alejandro Reyes-Castillo's people first, then the woman named Andrea Montaldo's people. The Santiesteban warehouse in Hialeah, then the fish camp in the Everglades margin. The island's exact location. Nothing.

Carla noted everything without expression.

After the statement she excused Arturo to the kitchen, where Elena and the university physician were attending to him, and sat with Jake in the living room.

"Montaldo," she said.

"The Italian contractor. Working for the Vatican-connected organization."

"The FBI Miami field office has a team interested in her. She's in the country, which gives us more to work with than if she'd run. But she's operational — she'll be operating." Carla looked at her notebook. "Alejandro Reyes-Castillo. He's recoverable for the implicit threats on the recording. Whether we can connect him to the kidnapping is more complicated."

"He'll distance himself. The property in Hialeah will be clean."

"Probably. Which brings us to Magdalena Santiesteban, who called you, offered information, and positioned herself as the wronged party in this." Carla looked at Jake steadily. "She's trying to get ahead of the investigation."

"I know."

"By helping you find Arturo she created a connection that makes prosecuting her organization for his disappearance extremely complicated." A pause. "She's been doing this for sixty years. This is the family enterprise at the strategic level."

Jake said nothing.

"I'm not asking you to regret finding him," Carla said carefully. "I'm telling you what the legal landscape looks like." She closed her notebook. "Montaldo is the more actionable target. If I can establish jurisdiction and hand it to the FBI—"

"Arturo needs time," Jake said. "And so does the island situation."

Carla looked at him. "Define 'the island situation.'"

Jake looked out the window at the Coral Gables morning, which was doing its usual thing of being beautiful in a way that was largely indifferent to human complications.

"There's an archive on an island in the Windward Passage," he said. "Placed there in the fourteenth century. Arturo is the only person who can fully decode the location cipher and access instructions. There are at least three parties — the Santiesteban organization, the Vatican-connected operatives, and Arturo himself — who want to reach that archive. And a Keys treasure hunter who has been waiting thirty-one years." He paused. "The archive's historical significance is significant. The documents in it may include material that certain powerful institutions would prefer remained hidden."

Carla absorbed this. "You're planning to go to the island."

"If Arturo wants to. If Nacho can take us."

"If you go to the island — if you remove or disturb anything in those caverns — you're in legal territory that is probably international, probably complex, and certainly not yours to manage." She looked at him. "Which I'm sure you know."

"I know."

"So what do you want from me?"

Jake thought about this. "Cover. If Montaldo's people follow us — if the Santiesteban organization follows us — I want law enforcement at a level that can actually respond."

"Cayo Espiritu is south of Cuba," Carla said. "Which makes it either Cuban territorial waters or international waters, depending on the exact position. Either way, Miami-Dade Police Department has no jurisdiction."

"The FBI's maritime jurisdiction—"

"Is real but slow and procedurally intensive." She looked at him. "What you're describing is a private expedition into international waters to access an archaeological site that multiple criminal and quasi-governmental actors are also trying to access. That is not a law enforcement operation. That is a disaster waiting for a date and time."

"That's a fair description," Jake agreed.

They looked at each other.

"I'll make calls," Carla said finally. "To people above my pay grade. I'll describe the situation. I'll advocate for a coordinated approach." She paused. "While you do what you're going to do anyway."

"While I do what I'm going to do," Jake agreed.

"Malone," she said, with the tone of someone who has had this conversation before, not with him specifically but in the general pattern. "Be careful."

"You keep saying that."

"You keep making it necessary."

---

Elena came to find him when Carla had left and Arturo was sleeping in the guest bedroom with the specific totality of a man who has been awake at high alert for seven days and has now been given permission to stop.

She stood in the doorway of the living room and looked at him.

"He told me," she said.

Jake waited.

"About my biological parents. The adoption. The people in Havana." She came and sat in the chair across from him. "My biological mother's name was Rosa Castillo de Espinosa. She died in 1993 — she was thirty years old, which makes her about twenty years old when I was born. Arturo said she was from a family that had been connected, historically, to the Santiesteban family in Havana before the revolution."

Jake was very still.

"Connected how?" he said carefully.

"He said she was a distant relative of the Santiesteban family. A cousin of some kind — the extended family that stayed in Cuba when the main branch came to Miami." She looked at her hands. "He doesn't believe she knew about the historical connection — the island, the research. He thinks it was coincidental. He says—" She paused. "He says he has always been afraid of what it would mean if I found out and looked into it and discovered things that complicated my life. He was protecting me."

Jake looked at her. He thought about the connections in this case — the layers of them, the way everything kept connecting to everything else in the specific way that Miami cases connected, where the city's size and its history and its particular demographic density meant that the past kept finding its way into the present.

"Your biological family may have a connection to the Santiesteban organization," he said carefully. "That doesn't define you. Or complicate your involvement in this."

"I know that," she said directly. "I'm a scientist. I don't confuse lineage with causation." She paused. "I'm telling you because I think it's relevant to the case, not because I'm having an identity crisis." A brief pause. "I'm having an identity crisis, but that's a separate project that I'm putting aside for now."

Jake almost smiled again.

"Will you come to the island?" he asked.

She looked at him. "My father wants me to stay in Miami."

"I know."

"He thinks it's dangerous."

"It probably is."

"He's been keeping me safe from this for thirty years." She looked at the sleeping direction — toward the guest bedroom. "He's been keeping me safe from everything for thirty years. And I am thirty-four years old and I am a scientist and I am the daughter of a man who spent his life on this and I would like—" She stopped. "I would like to be there when it's opened."

Jake looked at her.

"Then come," he said.

She nodded. Once. The specific nod of someone who has made a decision and isn't going to revisit it.

"I'll need to pack," she said.

"We leave in two days," Jake said. "Drive to Big Pine Key. Take Nacho's boat south."

She nodded again. Then she went to check on her father, and Jake sat in the Coral Gables apartment and thought about the shape of the journey ahead.

The island. The cavern. The sound.

Seven hundred years of carefully preserved history, preserved in the specific way that history is preserved when the people who created it knew they were creating something for the future: with care, with precision, with the understanding that the act of preservation was itself a statement about what mattered.

Diego de Almeida had done this. A twenty-three-year-old courier who had walked south out of Paris in 1307 with horses coming behind him and had spent the rest of his life keeping faith with a trust placed in him by people who were being destroyed.

Jake thought: that's worth going to find.

He thought: let's not let anyone else get there first.

He thought: two days.
