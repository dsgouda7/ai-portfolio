APPENDIX I: DELETED SCENES AND ALTERNATE PASSAGES

Editor's Note: The following passages were written during the composition of The Everglades Cipher and later removed or substantially revised. They are included here as supplementary material documenting the novel's development and providing additional texture on characters and locations. Numbers refer to the chapters from which the passages were removed.

---

DELETED PASSAGE FROM CHAPTER THREE: JAKE'S CONVERSATION WITH DETECTIVE REINA MORALES

[Original position: following Jake's exit from the police station. This scene was removed because it duplicated exposition delivered more efficiently through the missing persons report Jake reads later. Kept here because the Jake-Morales relationship is only implied in the final text.]

He found Reina Morales in the break room, which was where Reina Morales always was when she was deciding something.

She was standing at the window looking at the parking lot. She had a coffee in one hand and she was not drinking it. The coffee was what she held when she was thinking, not what she drank.

"Jake," she said, without turning around.

"Reina."

"I assume you're about to ask me something I'm not supposed to tell you."

"I'm looking for information about the Vasquez-Cortez missing persons report."

She turned around. She had the specific quality of someone who had been a detective for twenty-two years and had developed, over those twenty-two years, the ability to communicate a very large amount of information with a very small change in expression.

"The report," she said.

"The one filed three days ago."

"Which we are treating as a voluntary absence."

"Which you are treating as a voluntary absence."

She looked at him. She drank her coffee. She turned back to the window.

"He's seventy-eight," she said.

"I know."

"And the daughter is a marine biologist at UM."

"I know that too."

"And she filed the report herself and came in three times since and sat in that waiting room for two hours each time."

"Yes."

She said nothing for a moment. She was watching something in the parking lot that was not, Jake was fairly certain, particularly interesting.

"The file," she said eventually, "is in the documentation room. In alphabetical order by complainant surname. As all public records are."

"V for Vasquez-Cortez."

"That would be correct."

"Thank you, Reina."

"I didn't do anything," she said. "I was on my break."

He went to the documentation room and found the file.

DELETED PASSAGE FROM CHAPTER SEVEN: ARTURO'S MEMORY OF PORTO

[Removed from the historical interlude structure and reintegrated as a brief reference in Chapter 19. Kept here because Arturo's Portuguese origins, while mentioned in the text, are rarely given this level of detail.]

He had not been back to Porto since 1968. He had been twenty-three and he had left with one bag and a case for the manuscript he was transporting to Lisbon for a Jesuit colleague, and he had not returned because returning had not been possible, and then it had been possible and he had not done it, and then enough time had passed that the decision had become part of who he was rather than something he was still deciding.

Porto as he remembered it: the terraced houses above the Douro, the azulejo tiles on the facades in blue and white geometric patterns, the wine-vault smell from the port houses along the river, the particular grey quality of Atlantic light through the clouds that came off the ocean from the west in October and November. The Jesuit library where he had worked for three years as a young man, cataloguing a collection that had arrived from the dissolution of the Brazilian missions. The specific smell of that library: old paper, the beeswax used on the wooden furniture, cold stone from the building's sixteenth-century walls.

He had been, in Porto, a very young man who believed that the most important thing a person could do was understand what had happened. He still believed this. The Porto version of him had not known yet how long understanding took, but the conviction had been correct.

He thought about Porto when he was working late in the back room of La Luz de los Libros and the city outside had gone quiet and the only sound was the air conditioner's hum and the occasional car on Calle Ocho. Those were the same conditions: late, quiet, the lamp, the page. He had translated the Porto conditions to Miami and they had worked. He did not know whether that said something about conditions or about him.

The Codex Almeida was on his worktable. He turned back to it. The modal passage was waiting.

DELETED PASSAGE FROM CHAPTER NINE: NACHO AND JAKE, EARLY MORNING IN BIG PINE KEY

[Removed for pacing. The final text renders Nacho efficiently; this scene's value is its portrait of their friendship as a complete thing, not a relationship defined by the plot.]

They sat on the dock at four-thirty in the morning because Nacho always got up at four-thirty, and Jake had learned, over forty years, that sitting on the dock at four-thirty with Nacho was what being Nacho's friend meant.

The water was very dark. The stars were still out. The eastern sky had a strip of lighter grey at the horizon that would, in about an hour, become the suggestion of dawn.

"You're looking for an island," Nacho said.

"I have coordinates," Jake said.

"Coordinates in the Caribbean."

"The Windward Passage. That area."

Nacho drank his coffee. He was looking at the dark water the way he looked at everything: with the unhurried attention of someone who had learned that the water would tell you things if you waited.

"Long way to go for a manuscript," he said.

"Not if it's the right manuscript."

"When is it ever not the right manuscript, with you."

Jake looked at him.

"That's not fair," Jake said. "I've had non-manuscript cases."

"The Brennan thing was non-manuscript."

"See."

"Although the Brennan thing turned into a case about property records, which is adjacent."

"Adjacent is not the same."

Nacho smiled. He had a smile that happened slowly, in stages, like watching someone make a decision. "You remember when we were twelve and you told me you were going to be a detective?"

"I did not say that."

"You said you were going to find out things."

"That's different."

"Not very."

The sky was getting lighter.

"My daughter Sofia," Nacho said. "She's been diving the Windward Passage. For three years. She keeps a dive log."

"I know."

"She's good."

"I know that too."

"She's going to want to know why."

"I'll tell her as much as she needs to know."

"She'll need to know all of it," Nacho said. "That's how she is."

Jake nodded. He looked at the water.

"Good," he said.

DELETED PASSAGE FROM CHAPTER FOURTEEN: ELENA AND THE BIOLOGICAL QUESTION

[Removed because the final text handles Elena's interiority with more restraint. This version is more explicit; it was felt to be too explanatory for the character.]

The thing about being an adopted child — and Elena had been thinking about this since she was approximately eleven years old, which was when the fact had become fully available to her — was that the question existed independently of the answer.

She did not know who her biological mother was. This was a fact. It had been a fact for forty-seven years. She had decided, at some point in her mid-twenties, that the absence of information was something she could live with, and she had lived with it, and the decision had worked in the sense that she had not been prevented from living her life by the not-knowing.

But she was a scientist. The not-knowing was a different kind of thing for a scientist. For a scientist, a question that could in principle be answered but had not been answered was not a resolved situation; it was an unresolved one. The resolution happened or it didn't. You couldn't decide to resolve it by accepting the irresolution. You could decide to stop looking, but that was a different decision.

She had not been looking. She had also not fully stopped. She had maintained, in some compartment of herself that she didn't examine too often, the awareness that the question was still open.

Arturo had her biological mother's name. He had had it since before Elena was born. She knew he had it — had known this since her mid-thirties, when she had found, in a box in the closet of his room, an envelope addressed to him in a handwriting she did not recognize, postmarked Havana, 1976. She had not opened it. She had closed the box. She had put the box back in the closet. She had not mentioned it to Arturo.

He had not mentioned it to her.

That was, she supposed, the central characteristic of their relationship: the things they both knew were in the boxes and didn't open.

DELETED PASSAGE FROM CHAPTER SEVENTEEN: THE NIGHT WATCH

[Removed for length. Replaced by a condensed version in the final Chapter 17. The full version is included here because the Diego-Jake convergence at night sea is one of the novel's structural motifs, and this version is more extended.]

Jake stood the second watch, three to six, and looked at the sea.

The sea at night on the open water south of the Straits of Florida was not what it was in daylight. In daylight it was blue and comprehensible. At night it was very large and not particularly interested in being comprehensible. The stars were overhead. The water was below. The Miss Biscayne moved through the space between them.

He thought about Diego de Almeida standing a night watch on a vessel somewhere in this same water, seven hundred years ago. Not this exact water — Diego's Caribbean voyage would have been in a different season, under different stars, in a different vessel entirely, a caravel or a nau, a ship that moved differently through the water and required different management. But the same physical reality: the water, the stars, the distance to be crossed.

He thought about what Diego had been carrying. Not just the archive — that was on a different vessel, the cargo ship following the caravel at a distance. Diego would have been carrying the knowledge of where the archive would go. He had the coordinates in his head before he embedded them in the Codex. He had crossed this water knowing the destination and knowing that he was the only person alive who held both pieces of information: the location of the archive and the system for encoding its location.

If Diego had died on that voyage, the archive would have been lost. Not destroyed — just lost. Sealed in a sea cave in the Windward Passage with no retrievable record of its location. The seven centuries of preservation would have been meaningless preservation.

The navigation had mattered. Getting back mattered. Diego had gotten back.

Jake thought: me too, eventually.

He looked at the compass. The heading was correct. The speed was steady. Nacho had trimmed the boat perfectly before going below; she was holding her course without much attention from the helm.

He thought about Elena asleep below, with her dive bags and her logbooks and her very recent discovery that the woman she came from was named Rosa Castillo and had lived in Havana and had died in 1981 and had left a surviving sister who was now seventy-four and lived in Vedado. He thought about how Elena had received this information from Arturo in the galley two days before they left Big Pine Key: sitting at the table, very still, with both hands flat on the table surface, listening.

He thought about Arturo, also asleep below, who had told Elena everything he knew in the galley that afternoon and had not, afterward, apologized, because what would he have apologized for? He had done what he knew how to do, for as long as he could, and had told the truth when it was time for the truth. That was what was available.

The sea moved under the hull. The stars moved overhead in the other direction. The Miss Biscayne moved between them, south and west.

He stood his watch.

DELETED PASSAGE FROM CHAPTER TWENTY-ONE: SOFIA'S FIRST DIVE

[Removed because the final text renders the cave discovery with more economy. The longer version is kept here because it contains more of Sofia's interior experience, which was felt to slow the chapter's momentum but has its own value.]

She had been diving for twenty-one years and she had developed, over those twenty-one years, the specific equilibrium of a diver who has been in enough places that novelty registers as information rather than as feeling.

She registered this cave as significant the moment she cleared the entrance passage and her light filled the space beyond.

The chamber was roughly oval, twelve to fifteen meters across, with the ceiling rising to approximately four meters above the water surface at the center. The far wall had the sea cave's characteristic textured limestone, but the walls of the chamber had been worked — she could see it clearly in her light's beam: the regular geometry of cut stone, the kind of modification that required sustained effort with metal tools. The cave had not always looked like this. Someone had shaped it.

The air pocket was in the left-hand section of the chamber, where the floor rose above the current tide level. The floor was dry limestone, dusted with the fine sediment that had blown or washed in over the centuries.

The containers were in the air pocket section.

She surfaced in the air pocket, pushed her mask up, took a breath of air that was cool and slightly stale and tasted of nothing in particular. She looked at the containers.

They were oak boxes, iron-bound, four of them visible from where she was floating, more behind them in the shadow. The iron was badly corroded but the boxes appeared structurally intact. They were not particularly dramatic-looking. They looked like boxes that had been there for a very long time and were still there.

She took out the waterproof camera.

She photographed everything systematically: the chamber, the walls, the floor of the air pocket, the containers, the lead sealing on the nearest container's lid, the stamp or mark she could see on one container's face (too corroded to read without cleaning).

She did the complete survey in forty minutes, then went back through the passage and up into the sun.

She surfaced ten meters from the Miss Biscayne's hull. She pushed her mask up. Jake was at the stern rail.

"There's a chamber," she said.

"Yes," Jake said.

"With boxes in it."

"Yes."

She looked at him.

"How old?" she said.

He looked back at her for a moment.

"Seven hundred years," he said. "Approximately."

She held onto the boat's swim ladder and looked up at him.

"Okay," she said. She had the specific tone of someone who had just had a new piece of information arrive and was updating their world model in real time, which is all that any of us can do when the world gives us something new. "Okay."

She climbed back aboard to give him the camera.

DELETED PASSAGE FROM CHAPTER TWENTY-THREE: THE PHILIP IV LETTERS

[Removed and condensed into the final novel's Chapter 23. The extended version provides more detail on the letters' content and historical implications.]

The first letter was dated to the first year of what Philip's cipher key identified as the eleventh year of his reign: 1296. The text, decoded by the conservation specialist's assistant using the cipher key that had been identified in the French royal archive a year earlier by a researcher who had not known what it unlocked, was a request for an intelligence summary.

'You will send to my household officer by the next courier this month's account of all agents in the Curia and all intelligence received from Avignon in the preceding six months. You will include the full text of any communications received from the Cardinal of Albano since the feast of the Assumption. You will include the names, functions, and current dispositions of all persons employed in the intelligence service of Boniface VIII known to you. You will have your agents obtain, by means to your judgment appropriate, the full text of the correspondence between the legate Flotte and the court of England that you have indicated in your previous communication you have access to.'

The Templar Grand Master who received this letter — not Jacques de Molay, who did not become Grand Master until 1298; this letter's recipient was Thibaud Gaudin — had replied, and the reply was not in the archive, but the nature of the reply was implicit in the second letter, which referenced 'the complete accounting you have provided in your response' and expressed satisfaction with its thoroughness.

Philip IV, in 1296, had been using the Templar intelligence network as his primary source of information about the papal Curia and the court of England. The Templar Order had been running agents in Avignon and had access to the correspondence between the papal legate and the English court. Philip knew this and was paying for it, in some coin not described in the letters but presumably in the continued goodwill of the French crown, which was the Templar Order's largest single source of institutional protection.

Arturo read the text of the first letter three times. He was thinking about the historiography.

The accepted account of the dissolution emphasized Philip IV's debts to the Templar Order and his general program of bringing ecclesiastical institutions under French royal authority. These were documented motivations. The dissolution fit a pattern.

But the letters described a different dynamic: not a king who resented his creditors but a king who had been in a working intelligence partnership with the Templar Order for eleven years, and who, at some point between 1296 and 1307, had a falling out. The letters did not document the falling out — they documented the partnership. But the falling out was implied by the dissolution itself: you do not destroy your intelligence partner unless the intelligence partner has become an intelligence risk.

What had happened? The letters did not say. The records of what had happened between Philip and the Templar leadership in the years immediately before the dissolution were, for obvious reasons, not available.

The archive might contain more. He would ask the excavation team.

He looked at the conservation specialist's assistant, who was looking at the letter with the expression of someone who had just deciphered something that required a great deal of careful thought before any public statement was made.

"We're going to need to be very careful about the sequence of announcements," the assistant said.

"We are," Arturo said.

AUTHOR'S NOTE ON THE DELETED PASSAGES

The passages collected here were removed from the final text for reasons of pacing, length, and structural efficiency. None of them were removed because they were wrong — all of them reflect the characters and world as understood during the novel's composition. They are included in this appendix because readers who want to spend more time with these characters may find them useful, and because the deletions themselves are part of the novel's development history.

The cave is real in the sense that the conditions described (limestone ridge, tidal sea cave, air pocket section with near-constant temperature and humidity) accurately describe a class of Caribbean sea cave that exists. Whether a specific cave of this description exists in the Windward Passage is a question the author has deliberately not answered by citing a specific location.

The Philip IV letters are fictional. The historical facts about Philip IV and the Templar dissolution are not.

All of the characters are fictional. All of the Miami neighborhoods, Keys geography, and Caribbean navigation are accurate to the extent that the author could make them accurate.

Arturo's answer at the press conference — "Adequate. The work was worth doing." — is the author's answer too.
