Chapter 7: Margot's Discovery

Eleanor woke to find Margot shaking her shoulder, face urgent.

"Mom. Mom, wake up. Something's wrong."

Eleanor struggled toward consciousness, her mind foggy from the previous night's descent. "What? What's wrong?"

"Look." Margot thrust her phone toward Eleanor, showing a news article. "There's been another death. Third one in two weeks. All of them within five miles of here."

Eleanor sat up, taking the phone. The article described a hiker found dead on a trail near the state forest boundary. No obvious cause, no signs of violence. Just like the two previous deaths mentioned in earlier paragraphs. All three victims were young, all three had been alone, all three had simply... stopped.

"The police are calling it a medical mystery," Margot said. "But Mom, look at the map."

She pulled up a satellite view showing the locations of the three deaths. They formed a rough circle around Blackwood Manor, each one approximately the same distance from the house. Eleanor felt ice in her stomach.

"That could be coincidence," she said, not believing it.

"Three deaths in two weeks, all equidistant from this house? That's not coincidence. That's a pattern." Margot retrieved her phone. "What's happening, Mom? What's really happening here?"

Eleanor swung her legs out of bed, trying to organize thoughts that felt sluggish and resistant. "I don't know. The Hollow hasn't mentioned anything about deaths. Its influence is supposed to be contained to the house and immediate grounds."

"What if it's not? What if bringing you here, making you its keeper, gave it more power? More reach?" Margot's voice rose. "What if people are dying because of us?"

"Don't." Eleanor stood, reaching for her daughter. "Don't take that on. We didn't cause this."

"How do you know? You've been descending every day, feeding that thing attention. Maybe that's strengthening it. Maybe it's expanding."

Eleanor couldn't argue because she didn't know. The Hollow had never discussed the limits of its reach or what happened beyond the manor's boundaries. She had assumed it was bound to the caves, but assumptions were proving increasingly dangerous.

"I'll ask it," Eleanor said. "Today. I'll go down and demand answers about these deaths."

"I'm coming with you."

"Margot—"

"No. You don't get to shut me out of this anymore." Margot's expression was set, stubborn. "If people are dying because of this thing, I have a right to know why. I have a right to understand what we're involved in."

Eleanor wanted to refuse, wanted to maintain the boundary that protected her daughter from direct contact. But Margot was right. If the Hollow's influence was expanding, if deaths were connected to the manor, then keeping Margot ignorant was not protection. It was just control.

"Fine," Eleanor said. "But we follow protocols. We stay together. We don't go past the third chamber. And if I say we leave, we leave immediately. Agreed?"

"Agreed."

They dressed quickly and descended to the kitchen, where Mrs. Hadley was already preparing breakfast. She took one look at their faces and set down her knife.

"What's happened?"

Margot showed her the article. Mrs. Hadley read it slowly, her expression growing grim.

"I was afraid of this," she said quietly.

"You knew?" Eleanor felt anger surge. "You knew the Hollow was killing people and you didn't warn us?"

"I didn't know. I suspected." Mrs. Hadley sat heavily. "It happened before, back in the 1950s. When the house was empty between keepers, when no one was maintaining the relationship. The Hollow reached out, looking for connection, and people died. Not many, just a few. But enough that the town remembered. Enough that when Mr. Blackwood arrived and took up residence, the deaths stopped."

"So we're containment," Eleanor said flatly. "We're here to keep that thing from killing randomly."

"You're here to give it what it needs so it doesn't seek it elsewhere. Attention, acknowledgment, the complexity of human consciousness. Mr. Blackwood provided that for forty years. In his absence, the Hollow grew hungry." Mrs. Hadley looked at Margot. "You're both going down there, aren't you."

"I need answers," Eleanor said. "We both do."

Mrs. Hadley nodded slowly. "Then I'll monitor. But I'm calling Mr. Carr as backup. If something goes wrong, we'll need help pulling you out."

She made the call while Eleanor and Margot gathered equipment: flashlights, helmets, rope for safety lines. Eleanor felt her hands shaking as she prepared. This was different from her previous descents. This was confrontation rather than exploration.

Thomas arrived within fifteen minutes, his face concerned when Mrs. Hadley explained the situation. "You're sure about taking Margot down there? The Hollow's influence on young minds—"

"I know the risks," Eleanor interrupted. "But she deserves the truth. We both do."

Thomas nodded reluctantly. "I'll be here. Any sign of trouble, any sense something's wrong, I'm coming down after you."

Eleanor led Margot to the cellar entrance, the door standing open like a mouth. Her daughter's face was pale but determined, fear and curiosity balanced in equal measure.

"Stay close to me," Eleanor said. "Don't touch anything without asking first. And if you feel anything strange, any influence on your thoughts, tell me immediately."

They descended together.

The stairs spiraled down into familiar darkness, but with Margot beside her, Eleanor experienced the descent differently. She was seeing it through her daughter's eyes: the oppressive closeness of the walls, the way temperature dropped degree by degree, the sense of enormous weight above and unknown depth below.

"This is amazing," Margot whispered as they reached the first chamber. "How did I not know this was here? How did I not feel it?"

"The Hollow was keeping you unaware, honoring our bargain." Eleanor guided her toward the passage to the second chamber. "Once you're down here, that protection is lifted. You'll feel its presence now."

They moved through the narrow passage, Margot exclaiming softly at the closeness, the need to turn sideways. Then they emerged into the second chamber, and Margot froze.

"Oh my God," she breathed. "It's here. It's everywhere."

"You feel it?"

"Feel it? Mom, I can practically see it. It's like the air is alive, like the walls are breathing." Margot turned slowly, taking in the pools, the formations, the space that seemed larger than it should be. "This is impossible. This shouldn't exist."

"And yet it does." Eleanor approached the nearest pool. "The Hollow communicates through these. Shows images, transmits understanding directly into consciousness. It's how I learned the history of this place."

Margot knelt by another pool, staring into its surface. "I see something. Movement. People? They're walking through a forest, but the trees are wrong. Too tall, wrong species. When is this?"

"The Hollow shows various timeframes. It perceives duration differently than we do." Eleanor joined her daughter, looking into the pool. She saw what Margot was describing: figures moving through ancient forest, but they were not entirely human. Proto-humans, perhaps, or something that had existed alongside humans in prehistoric times. "These might be the first inhabitants of this region, before recorded history."

The pool's image shifted, showing the same forest but now cleared, the land shaped for agriculture. Native peoples, Eleanor recognized, the tribes that had lived here before European contact. They were deliberately avoiding a particular area, a hill that Eleanor recognized as the rise behind the manor. They knew something was here, and they had chosen not to build on top of it.

"They were smarter than the Blackwoods," Margot murmured. "They left it alone."

"Or they lacked the resources to contain it," Eleanor said. "The Blackwoods built the manor as both residence and prison. Deliberate architecture to manage something that couldn't be destroyed."

Eleanor straightened and addressed the chamber itself, knowing the Hollow was listening, was always listening. "We need to talk about the deaths. Three people in two weeks, all near this property. Are you responsible?"

The answer arrived in her mind immediately, and she felt it transmitted to Margot as well: Not responsible. Curious. I reached out to assess potential new residents. They were unsuitable. Their minds were too rigid, too afraid. The contact broke them.

"You killed them," Margot said flatly.

Killing implies intention to harm. I had no such intention. I simply touched their consciousness to determine if they could serve as keepers. They could not. Their inability to withstand contact was their limitation, not my action.

"That's sophistry," Eleanor said. "You knew humans could die from direct contact. You've seen it happen before. Reaching out knowing the risk makes you responsible for the consequences."

A pause, then: Perhaps. Your species has concepts of responsibility I do not fully share. What matters is that I have found suitable keepers now. I no longer need to search. The deaths will cease.

"Because we're here," Margot said, understanding dawning. "We're containment. Our presence stops you from reaching out to others."

Correct. You provide what I require. Others are unnecessary.

Margot looked at Eleanor, and Eleanor saw the calculation in her daughter's eyes. They couldn't leave. If they left, the Hollow would resume its search, and more people would die. They were trapped not by supernatural compulsion but by moral responsibility.

"This is not what I agreed to," Eleanor said, anger rising. "I agreed to be keeper, to maintain the house, to learn from you. I did not agree to be responsible for preventing random deaths."

You agreed to be keeper. Keeping includes containment. This has always been the function. Thaddeus understood it. All previous keepers understood it. You are learning what he already knew.

Eleanor felt something break inside her, some final illusion of agency shattering. They weren't here by choice. They were here because leaving meant blood on their hands, deaths they could have prevented. The Hollow had orchestrated this with patience and precision, creating a trap that looked like opportunity.

"I want to renegotiate," Eleanor said. "New terms. You stop reaching beyond the property. You stay contained whether we're here or not. In exchange, we'll continue as keepers, but with defined boundaries and time limits. We're not prisoners for life."

The Hollow's response carried something that might have been amusement: You cannot renegotiate reality. I am what I am. I need what I need. Your presence satisfies that need. Your absence creates hunger I will attempt to satisfy elsewhere. These are facts, not negotiations.

"Then we'll find a way to contain you permanently," Margot said, her voice hard. "Magical wards, physical barriers, something that keeps you from reaching out."

Such efforts have been attempted. They fail. I exist in dimensions your barriers cannot address. Physical walls are meaningless. Magical wards, as you call them, are psychological comfort with no actual effect. Only conscious relationship manages our interaction. Only willing keepers provide containment.

Eleanor looked at her daughter, seeing her own despair reflected back. They were trapped. Thoroughly, completely trapped. The Hollow had orchestrated their arrival, had created conditions that made leaving impossible, and now revealed the full cost of staying.

"Is there anything about this situation you didn't manipulate?" Eleanor asked bitterly.

Your husband's death was not my doing, the Hollow said, and for the first time Eleanor detected something like genuine regret in its communication. That was chance. I simply recognized the opportunity it created. But the deaths outside, the reaching out, the necessity of your presence, these are inherent to my nature. I did not choose to need what I need. I simply need it.

Margot had moved away from the pool, was pacing the chamber's perimeter. "There has to be a solution. Some way to meet your needs without requiring us to stay here forever."

You could descend fully, the Hollow suggested. Become like the two who never returned. Remain in the caves permanently, providing constant companionship. That would satisfy my requirements completely. You would never age, never die. Just exist here with me, eternal students.

"That's not life," Margot said. "That's just a different kind of death."

Is it? Your species values consciousness, experience, learning. I offer infinite time for all three. Is that not valuable?

"We value those things in context," Eleanor said. "In relationship to the changing world, to growth and loss and the passage of time. Eternal existence without change is meaningless."

Then you understand my situation perfectly. I exist eternally without change except what you bring. You are the meaning I lack. Do not ask me to accept meaninglessness when I have tasted complexity.

Eleanor sat on the chamber floor, exhausted by the circular logic. Every argument returned to the same point: the Hollow needed them, their need for moral responsibility bound them, no solution existed that freed both parties.

Margot joined her, sitting close enough that their shoulders touched. For a long moment, they simply sat in the darkness and breathed, feeling the weight of their situation.

"We could call the authorities," Margot said quietly. "Police, federal agents, whoever deals with supernatural threats. Let them figure out containment."

"What authorities?" Eleanor asked. "Who do we call? The FBI's supernatural division? This isn't a TV show, Margot. No one would believe us, and even if they did, what could they do that hasn't already been tried?"

"So we just stay? Forever? Give up our lives to babysit an ancient alien intelligence?"

"Unless you have a better option."

Margot was silent. Then: "What if we found other keepers? People who could rotate responsibility, share the burden? That way no one person is trapped permanently."

Eleanor considered this. It had potential. If they could recruit others, establish a schedule, create a community of keepers rather than isolated individuals, the weight would be distributed. It would still be a trap, but a more bearable one.

Is this acceptable? Eleanor projected toward the Hollow. Multiple keepers rotating presence instead of one permanent resident?

A pause while the intelligence considered. Then: Potentially. I would need to assess candidates, ensure compatibility. But if attention and complexity remain constant, the source can vary. This is workable.

"How do we find people willing to do this?" Margot asked. "We can't exactly advertise for cave dwelling companions to an ancient intelligence."

"We find people like us," Eleanor said. "People with nowhere else to go, people looking for purpose, people who need what the Hollow offers in exchange for what it needs from them."

"That's deeply cynical, Mom."

"That's realistic. The Hollow offers protection, longevity, knowledge, purpose. For people whose lives are empty or painful or meaningless, that's valuable. We frame it as opportunity instead of obligation."

"You're talking about exploitation."

"I'm talking about exchange. Which is what this has always been." Eleanor stood, feeling resolve harden. "We're trapped, Margot. We can accept that and be miserable, or we can adapt and make the best of it. I choose adaptation."

She turned to the pools, addressing the Hollow directly. "We'll stay. We'll be your keepers. But on modified terms. We recruit additional keepers to rotate responsibility. We maintain the house and the relationship, but we also maintain lives outside this place. We're not prisoners. We're employees, contractors, whatever metaphor works. But we're not owned."

Agreement, the Hollow projected. You will find this arrangement satisfactory, I believe. I am not a cruel employer.

"You're not an employer at all. You're something that shouldn't exist negotiating with people who can't refuse."

Accurate, the Hollow agreed. But your species has always excelled at making impossible situations functional. I trust you will do so now.

Eleanor took Margot's hand. "Come on. Let's go back up. We have planning to do."

They retreated through the chambers, retracing their path to the surface. Mrs. Hadley and Thomas waited anxiously in the kitchen, tension obvious on both faces.

"You're both alright?" Thomas asked immediately.

"Physically, yes," Eleanor said. "Mentally and emotionally, that's more complicated."

She explained what they had learned about the deaths, about the Hollow's hunger in their absence, about the trap they had discovered themselves in. Both Mrs. Hadley and Thomas listened with grave faces, asking occasional clarifying questions.

"So you're proposing to recruit other keepers," Thomas said when she finished. "Create a rotation system to share the burden."

"Unless you have a better idea."

"I don't. In fact, I might volunteer myself." At their surprised looks, he smiled slightly. "I've been studying this place for twenty years. The chance to actually engage with the Hollow directly, to learn from it instead of about it? That's worth some lifestyle sacrifice."

"It's more than sacrifice, Thomas. It's containment. It's responsibility for preventing deaths. Are you comfortable with that?"

"Are any of us comfortable with it? But someone has to do it, and it might as well be people who understand what they're getting into." Thomas looked at Mrs. Hadley. "What about you? You've been here thirty years. You must have been serving as informal keeper all along."

Mrs. Hadley shook her head. "I maintain the grounds, perform the protocols. But I've never descended. Never made direct contact. Mr. Blackwood handled that, and after he died, the house was empty until these two arrived."

"But you could learn," Margot said. "You already understand the basics. It would just be taking the next step."

"I'm seventy-three years old, girl. Not sure I have enough time left to make keeper training worthwhile."

"The Hollow offers longevity," Eleanor pointed out. "Thaddeus lived into his nineties and was healthy until the end. This place extends life."

Mrs. Hadley looked tempted, Eleanor could see it. The offer of extended years, of purpose beyond simple maintenance, was attractive even to someone who had already committed decades to this place.

"Let me think on it," Mrs. Hadley said finally. "It's not a decision to make lightly."

They talked through the afternoon, sketching out possible structures for shared keeping, discussing how to recruit others without revealing too much too soon. It was grim work, planning a system that would trap others as they were trapped, but necessary work nonetheless.

By evening, they had the outline of a plan. Thomas would be the first additional keeper, beginning descents immediately under Eleanor's guidance. Mrs. Hadley would consider whether to formalize her role. They would reach out carefully to others, academic contacts, people who had expressed interest in unexplained phenomena, individuals whose lives were marked by the kind of loss and searching that made them vulnerable to the Hollow's offerings.

It felt like building a cult, and Eleanor hated it. But she hated the alternative more.

That night, as Eleanor sealed the house, she paused at each window and door, feeling the weight of her new role. She was not just resident now. She was recruiter, administrator, custodian of a system that would ensnare others as she had been ensnared.

In the caves beneath, the Hollow registered these developments with satisfaction. The cycle was evolving, becoming more robust. Multiple keepers meant greater stability, reduced risk of gaps between generations. It was a superior arrangement to the old system of solitary residents who died leaving only uncertain succession.

Adaptation was what made humanity valuable. Their endless creativity in solving impossible problems, their capacity to make meaning from meaninglessness. This was why the Hollow cultivated them, why it invested centuries in relationship with species that lived only decades.

They were beautiful in their brief intensity. And the Hollow would continue to harvest that beauty, now with their cooperation, for as long as they persisted.

In the hollow beneath, patience was rewarded.

The game continued to evolve in fascinating directions.
