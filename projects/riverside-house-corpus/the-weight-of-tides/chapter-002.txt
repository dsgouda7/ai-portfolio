Chapter 2: The Blue Folder

Morning light found Claire still at her father's desk, the blue folder's contents spread before her like evidence at a crime scene. Forty years of documentation: water temperature readings, unusual catches, strange sonar returns, photographs that showed nothing and everything. And at the center of it all, July 12, 1975, circled in red ink on a dozen different pages.

She heard Maya's footsteps on the stairs, the coffee maker gurgling to life in the kitchen. Normal sounds in an increasingly abnormal situation. Claire rubbed her eyes, trying to reconcile what she had read with the world she thought she knew.

Her father had been twenty-five that summer, newly married to Dottie, fishing with a small crew of men who had known these waters all their lives. They had gone out at dusk, as lobstermen sometimes did in those days, checking traps set in the deep channels off Whitehead Island. The sea had been calm, the air warm. Then, around midnight, everything had changed.

The official Coast Guard report was brief and skeptical. The crew had radioed in about unusual sonar readings, possibly a submarine or large whale. They reported seeing lights in the water, bioluminescence possibly, though unusually bright and persistent. No distress call. No request for assistance. Just observations, filed and forgotten.

But Thomas Merritt's personal log told a different story. He described an object, massive, moving beneath their boat. Lights that weren't bioluminescence but something mechanical, patterned, intelligent. The water temperature dropping fifteen degrees in minutes. Every lobster trap they pulled empty, the bait untouched but the traps showing signs of intense pressure, metal bent inward. And then, just before dawn, the thing rising.

Claire stared at the photograph. Dark water, caught in harsh deck lights. Something breaking the surface, something huge and curved and covered in what looked like barnacles but wasn't. A portion of something larger. Her father had written coordinates on the back, depths, water temperature readings. He had documented it with the precision of a man who knew he wouldn't be believed.

"What are you reading?"

Maya stood in the doorway, holding two mugs of coffee. She crossed the study and set one beside Claire.

"Grandpa's notes. Research he did on local marine life."

Maya looked at the photographs. "That doesn't look like marine life."

"No," Claire admitted. "It doesn't."

Her daughter picked up one of the photos, examining it closely. Maya had inherited Claire's scientific curiosity, her need to understand rather than simply observe. "What did he think it was?"

"I don't know. He never wrote a conclusion, just kept documenting. For forty years."

"Did anyone else see it?"

Claire shuffled through the papers. "Three other men were on the boat that night. Two are dead now. The third, Vernon Shaw, still lives in town. Jake mentioned him yesterday, actually. He runs the marine supply shop on Harbor Street."

"Are you going to talk to him?"

"I don't know what I'm going to do."

Maya sat on the desk edge, sipping her coffee. She had pulled her dark hair into a messy bun, and without makeup she looked younger, more vulnerable. "Grandpa wanted you to know about this. He kept all of it. That means something."

"It means he was obsessed."

"Or that it mattered. That it was real and important and connected to everything else he cared about." Maya gestured at the piles of documents. "All this stuff about water temperature, changing migration patterns, the lobsters moving north. What if it's all connected? What if whatever he saw that night is part of why the ecosystem is changing?"

Claire looked at her daughter with new appreciation. "That's actually a reasonable scientific hypothesis."

"I pay attention in biology class."

"Apparently."

They sat in comfortable silence, drinking coffee as sunlight filled the study. Claire felt the exhaustion of the sleepless night weighing on her, but beneath it something else was stirring. Curiosity. The same drive that had made her a scientist, that had pulled her into studying marine ecosystems in the first place.

"I should go back to Boston," she said. "Talk to my supervisor, see if I can take a leave of absence. If I'm going to be here settling the estate, I should do it officially."

"Or," Maya said carefully, "you could look at this as research. You're always saying you need new data sources. Well, Grandpa left you forty years of meticulous observations. That's worth something, scientifically."

"It's not peer-reviewed. It's one man's anecdotal—"

"Mom." Maya's voice was gentle but firm. "You've been looking for a reason to come home. Maybe not consciously, but I've heard you on the phone with Grandma, the calls you thought I didn't notice. The guilt in your voice. You've been waiting for permission to reconnect with this place. Well, here it is. Grandpa's research. A mystery worth solving. Permission granted."

Claire felt tears prick her eyes. When had her daughter become so wise?

"The bonfire tonight," she said. "You can go."

Maya grinned. "Yeah?"

"Yeah. But text me every hour, and be home by eleven."

"Midnight."

"Eleven thirty."

"Deal." Maya jumped up, energy restored by victory. "I'm going to shower and then walk into town. There's a library, right? I want to look up local history, see if there are other stories about weird ocean stuff."

"Check the newspaper archives. The Willowport Courier. They've been publishing since the 1800s."

After Maya left, Claire gathered the documents carefully, replacing them in the blue folder. Her father's handwriting covered every page: neat, methodical, the script of a man who believed in the power of evidence. She thought about the last time she had seen him, five years ago at a cousin's wedding. They had exchanged pleasantries, careful and distant. She had told him about a promotion at work. He had mentioned the lobster population decline. Neither had said what they meant: I miss you. I'm sorry. Come home.

Now he was gone, and she was home, and the conversation would never happen. But perhaps there was another way to connect with Thomas Merritt. Through his work. Through his questions. Through the mystery that had obsessed him for forty years.

Claire stood, stretching muscles stiff from a night in the chair. She needed sleep, but first she needed to talk to Vernon Shaw.

Harbor Street was busier than she expected. Tourists mixed with locals, spring visitors drawn by the town's marketing campaign: "Authentic Coastal Maine Experience." Claire wondered if they knew how much that authenticity was under threat, how the working waterfront was being squeezed out by gentrification and climate change and the relentless pressure of money.

Shaw's Marine Supply occupied a weathered building at the end of the street, its windows cluttered with rope and buoys and the equipment of a dying trade. A bell chimed as Claire entered.

The interior smelled of engine oil and salt and rope. Vernon Shaw stood behind the counter, older than she remembered but unmistakable: tall, weathered, eyes the color of winter sea. He looked up from an invoice and smiled.

"Claire Merritt. Heard you were back."

"Mr. Shaw. It's been a long time."

"That it has. I was sorry to hear about your father. Tom was a good man."

"Thank you." Claire approached the counter, gathering courage. "I wanted to ask you about something. Something that happened a long time ago. July 12, 1975."

Vernon's expression didn't change, but something flickered in his eyes. Recognition. Memory. Maybe fear.

"Your father talked to you, then."

"He left me his notes. His logs. I've been reading them."

Vernon set down his pen carefully. "I wondered if he would. He swore me to silence while he lived, but I suppose death releases those kinds of promises." He moved to the door, flipped the sign to "Closed," and locked it. "Come on back. This isn't a conversation for open doors."

He led her to a small office cluttered with decades of marine supply records. Vernon cleared papers from a chair and gestured for Claire to sit. He remained standing, looking out a grimy window toward the harbor.

"We saw something that night," he said quietly. "All four of us. Tom, myself, Eddie Garrett, and Paul Chen. We swore we'd never talk about it. People would think we were crazy, or drunk, or making up stories for attention. But we all saw it. And it changed things."

"Changed how?"

"The water changed. The temperature, the chemistry, something. We all noticed it over the years. The lobsters moving, the fish populations shifting. It's been gradual, easy to blame on climate change. And maybe that's all it is. But Tom didn't think so. He thought whatever we saw that night was still down there, still affecting things."

"What was it?"

Vernon turned to face her. "I don't know. Something big. Something that didn't belong in these waters, or maybe something that belonged here before we did and we'd forgotten about it. Tom had theories. He thought maybe it was some kind of research vessel, military or scientific. Or something older. Something that lived in the deep trenches and only came up rarely. He spent years trying to find it again, looking for patterns in the sonar readings, tracking temperature anomalies. He never did find it. But he never stopped looking."

"Did you believe him? That it was still there?"

"I believe Tom saw what he saw. I believe I saw it too. Beyond that?" Vernon shrugged. "I'm a practical man. I sell marine supplies. I watch the boats come in with smaller catches every year. I see the young people leave because there's no future in fishing anymore. Whether there's something strange in the water or it's just regular climate change doesn't much matter. The end result is the same. This way of life is dying."

Claire thought about her daughter's hypothesis, about connections and causes. "But what if the cause matters? What if understanding what's happening could help us prevent it, or at least slow it down?"

"That's what Tom thought. That's why he documented everything. He said someday someone with the right expertise would look at his data and see the pattern he couldn't. Someone who understood marine ecosystems and climate science. Someone like you, I'm guessing."

The weight of expectation settled on Claire's shoulders. Her father had been preparing for her return, had been waiting for her to come home and continue his work. She felt manipulated and honored in equal measure.

"I'm not sure I'm the right person."

"You're the only person. You're Tom's daughter. You're a scientist. And whether you admit it or not, this place is in your blood. You can run from it, like you did, but it doesn't let go. The ocean doesn't let go."

Vernon unlocked the office door, their conversation clearly finished. "There's one more thing. Tom kept his personal research separate from his business records. The really sensitive stuff, the things he didn't want anyone stumbling across. He kept those on his boat."

"The Anna Marie?"

"That's the one. It's still moored at the town dock, slip fourteen. Jake has the keys. Tom wanted you to have the boat, Claire. And everything on it."

Claire thanked him and left the shop, stepping back into spring sunshine that felt surreal after the dim interior and heavy conversation. She stood on the sidewalk, watching tourists photograph the picturesque harbor, unaware of the mysteries beneath the surface, the changes creeping through the ecosystem, the way of life slipping away.

Her phone buzzed. A text from Maya: "Library is amazing. Found newspaper articles about 'mysterious lights' off Whitehead Island. Not just 1975. Goes back to the 1800s. Coming home for lunch?"

Claire texted back: "Meet me at the harbor. We're going out on a boat."

She walked toward the town dock, past the tourists and boutique shops, past the diner and the hardware store, past the tourist information center with its cheerful maps and brochures. At the dock, she found Jake directing a boat crew, his harbormaster uniform giving him an official air that sat oddly on someone she remembered as a perpetual rebel.

"Claire. Twice in two days. People will talk."

"Let them. I need the keys to my father's boat."

Jake studied her. "You've been reading the files."

"How much do you know?"

"Enough. Tom trusted me. Not with everything, but with enough." He pulled a keyring from his pocket, removed one key, and handed it over. "She's fueled and ready. Tom kept her maintained even when he couldn't afford to. The Anna Marie was his pride."

"Named after my grandmother."

"Who died when Tom was twelve. He never got over it, people said. Never really accepted being motherless. Maybe that's why he was so fascinated with the ocean. Looking for something he lost."

Claire closed her fingers around the key. "That's pretty philosophical for a harbormaster."

"I read, Claire. And I think. You don't have a monopoly on depth just because you left."

The words stung because they were fair. "I'm sorry. That was condescending."

"Yeah, it was." But Jake smiled. "Go on. The boat's waiting. Just be back before dark. Weather's supposed to turn tonight."

Claire found the Anna Marie in slip fourteen, a working lobster boat, thirty-two feet of scarred fiberglass and practical design. She was beautiful in the way functional things can be, every inch of her designed for purpose. Claire climbed aboard, feeling the boat rock beneath her feet, and for the first time since arriving in Willowport, she felt something like peace.

Maya appeared, running down the dock with a backpack full of library printouts. "Are we really going out?"

"We're really going out. But you need to wear a life jacket."

"Mom, I can swim."

"Life jacket. Non-negotiable."

They motored out of the harbor, the Anna Marie's engine settling into a steady rhythm. Claire hadn't piloted a boat in years, but the skills came back: reading the water, feeling the currents, understanding the relationship between vessel and sea. This was what her father had taught her, during the years before everything went wrong. This was the language they had shared.

Past the harbor mouth, the water deepened and darkened. Claire aimed for Whitehead Island, following the coordinates from her father's notes. The island rose from the water like a broken tooth, abandoned lighthouse standing sentinel over empty beaches and wind-twisted trees.

"This is where it happened," she told Maya. "Where Grandpa and his crew saw whatever they saw."

"Are we looking for it?"

"I don't know what we're looking for. Evidence maybe. Understanding. Connection to something I thought I'd lost."

They drifted in the deep water off the island, engine idling. Claire pulled out her father's notes, comparing the old coordinates to her phone's GPS. They were in the right place, more or less. Forty-five years later, but the right place.

The water was opaque, revealing nothing. Claire lowered a thermometer, checked the reading. Twelve degrees Celsius. Warmer than her father's notes from forty years ago by several degrees. The Gulf of Maine was one of the fastest-warming bodies of water on Earth. Everything was changing.

"There's something down there, isn't there?" Maya said. "Something that matters."

"There's always something down there. The question is whether we can understand it, and whether understanding it makes any difference."

"Grandpa thought it made a difference."

"Grandpa was obsessed."

"Maybe obsession is just another word for caring enough to not give up."

Claire looked at her daughter, this fierce and certain young woman. "When did you get so smart?"

"I've always been this smart. You've been too busy to notice."

It was said without malice, just observation, but it cut deep. Claire had been busy. Too busy for phone calls, for visits, for noticing that her father was dying and her daughter was growing up. Too busy for everything except work that now felt distant and unimportant.

"I'm sorry," she said. "For being absent. For dragging you here. For all of it."

Maya smiled. "I'm not. This is the most interesting thing that's happened to me in years. A mystery, a crusade, a dying town that needs saving. It's better than AP Bio."

"Your AP exam—"

"Is in six weeks. I'll study. But first, let's figure out what Grandpa was trying to tell you. Let's find out what's in the water."

They stayed until the light began to fail, taking measurements, reviewing documents, feeling the rhythm of the sea. As they motored back to harbor, Claire felt something shift inside her. A door opening. A decision forming.

She was going to stay. Not forever, perhaps, but long enough to understand. Long enough to honor her father's obsession and maybe discover her own. Long enough to save the Anna Marie and the logbooks and the mystery that had consumed Thomas Merritt for forty years.

The weight of tides had pulled her home. Now she would see where the current carried her.
