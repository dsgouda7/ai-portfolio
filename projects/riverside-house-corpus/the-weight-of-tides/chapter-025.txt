Chapter 25: What the Ocean Remembers

Thomas III had been keeping a research journal since he was sixteen, and he had learned, by now, the difference between the entries he wrote immediately after a water session — impressionistic, image-heavy, reaching for equivalents in a language that didn't always have them — and the entries he wrote two days later, when the sediment had settled and he could see what the experience had deposited. He kept both, because the immediate ones were truer to the feeling and the delayed ones were truer to the meaning, and what he was doing required both.

Today was an immediate day. He sat on the research dock in the August morning, wet from the dive, a towel around his shoulders and his journal on his knees, writing with the particular speed of someone trying to get something down before it dissolved. His grandmother would have recognized the impulse; it was the same one that had driven Thomas Merritt the first to write in the dark in his fishing boat, the necessity of recording something before the doubt could arrive and rename it.

Thomas III was twenty years old. He had been, since January, officially enrolled in the Institute's graduate research program, which made him the first person in the program's history to hold the dual status of researcher and — in the program's careful, deeply considered language — "community participant." He was, in other words, both studying the deep-dweller civilization and belonging to part of it, which created the kind of methodological complexities that his mother found professionally fascinating and his academic committee found professionally alarming. He found it simply accurate.

He had been working for seven months on what his committee called his "initial mapping project" and what Observer called, in the deep-dweller utterance Thomas III translated as something between "the listening work" and "the remembering together," a joint archival effort that had no precedent.

The deep-dwellers kept their history differently.

This was the first thing he had written in his journal, back in January, underlined twice: they keep their history differently. It had taken him six months to begin to understand what differently actually meant.

Human history lived in documents and objects and the narratives told about both — in the records created with the explicit intention of preservation, and in the architecture of power that determined which records were kept and which were allowed to dissolve. Deep-dweller history lived in the environment itself. It lived in the structure of the communities they had built and rebuilt over millennia, in the chemical record encoded in the mineral formations of the deep-ocean floor, in the songs that were not art but archive — sequences of tone and rhythm and bioluminescent pattern that could be performed from memory by any adult member of the community and that contained, embedded in their structure like code, a compressed account of what the community had experienced and believed and survived.

Observer was teaching him to read all three.

The dive this morning had been a reading session. That was what Thomas III had begun calling them to himself, and what Observer had adopted with a kind of amiable precision — they were reading the record. Observer would lead him to a specific formation or a specific location in the deep-city structures, would produce the associated song, and Thomas III would listen and map and ask questions, and Observer would correct his misreadings and confirm his accurate ones, and gradually, painstakingly, an account was emerging of something Thomas III found increasingly difficult to describe in either of his languages without feeling that both of them were inadequate to the scale of it.

The deep-dwellers had records of human presence in these waters going back twelve thousand years.

He had confirmed this six weeks ago, and had called his mother from the dock immediately after, sitting dripping on the research ladder with his phone pressed against his ear, trying to explain what he had understood. She had listened in silence, then asked three precise questions, then said quietly: "Get back in the water and ask Observer to take you to the beginning."

He had done this. He was still doing this. The beginning — in the deep-dweller sense, in the sense of the earliest record that Observer could guide him to directly — was a formation on the seafloor approximately forty kilometers offshore, in water just under two hundred meters, at the edge of the continental shelf. The formation was not a building. It was not quite a monument. It was something the deep-dwellers would have called, Thomas III had decided after considerable translation effort, a witness marker: a physical structure built deliberately over a long period of time, encoding in its specific arrangement of sediment-encrusted mineral and carved stone a summary of what had been observed in a particular region over a particular span of time.

The region was the Maine coast. The span of time was the Holocene: the eleven thousand years since the last glacial maximum, when the coastline that was now Willowport Bay had been a ridge above the retreating ice.

He had lain beside the witness marker in the cold deep water, Observer's bioluminescence his primary light source at that depth, and had listened to the associated song and tried to understand what twelve thousand years of human presence looked like from below.

The oldest layer of the encoding had taken him four dives to begin to read. It was not that the individual symbols were especially difficult — he had enough deep-dweller linguistic competency to parse the basic vocabulary of the song — but that the grammar of deep historical narration was different from any conversational or contemporary form he knew. It was highly compressed, structured around what the deep-dwellers called the "weight markers" — the elements that indicated not just what had happened but how consequential the occurrence was in the deep-dweller assessment. High-weight events were encoded with greater phonological complexity and longer bioluminescent duration; low-weight events were compressed to near-zero, retained only as context for the surrounding high-weight material.

The humans on the ancient coastline were high-weight from the beginning.

This had surprised Thomas III, and he had sat with it for two weeks before he felt ready to talk about it. He had expected the early human records to be low-weight — marginal, peripheral, the occasional notation of surface-world activity that had some interaction with deep-dweller territory. What he found instead was that the first appearance of humans in the deep-dweller record, twelve thousand years ago, had been marked with the full complexity of significant occurrence. They had been paying attention from the very start.

Observer had explained, in the way Observer explained things that required careful translation — in layers, returning to the same point multiple times from slightly different angles until Thomas III could feel the three-dimensional shape of the concept — that the reason was simple: humans were novel. In twelve thousand years of deep-dweller observation before the human records began, there had been nothing quite like the pattern of activity that the coastal humans represented. Small, vulnerable, slow, entirely surface-bound — and yet deliberate. Purposeful. Doing things for reasons that could be inferred but were not obvious. The deep-dwellers had found this interesting in the way that scientists find things interesting: the way that observing something you don't have a category for produces the particular intellectual alertness of genuine discovery.

"They were doing science," Thomas III had told his mother on the phone, the night after the fourth dive. "From the beginning. They were observing us the way we observe systems we don't understand. With the same quality of attention."

"Did that change over time?" Maya had asked.

"It changed in complexity. In the early records they're describing behavior patterns — where humans camped, what they ate, how they moved seasonally. In the middle period — roughly the last five thousand years — they're describing culture. Trade routes. Language diversity — they could apparently hear the acoustic difference between different coastal cultures. The patterns of warfare and peace. And in the last two hundred years—" He had paused. "In the last two hundred years the records get very dense. The density increase corresponds to the industrialization."

He had gone back in the water the following day and had asked Observer to take him to the densest section of the record. Observer had done so, and Thomas III had spent three hours in forty-meter water listening to the accounting of what the past two centuries had looked like from below.

He had come back up and sat on the dock for a long time without writing anything.

The ice age records were at the other end of the emotional register: vast, patient, and in a strange way peaceful. He returned to them when the density of the modern records became heavy. The ice age humans were not yet the world-altering species they would become; they were clever, adaptive, resourceful creatures who lived in close relationship with the rhythms of the coast and who were, from the deep-dweller perspective, primarily interesting because of what they might become. The deep-dwellers of twelve thousand years ago had watched the post-glacial humans with the interest of someone reading the first chapters of a very long book.

Thomas III found, in the ice age records, a quality he needed when the modern records became too much to hold: the perspective of beginning. The knowledge that there had been a time before the damage, before the industrial acceleration, before the mourning ceremonies for extinct species — a time when the observation was fresh and the book was open and the ending was unknown. That time had existed. It was in the record. He could go back to it when he needed to.

He called Maya after the fourth ice age dive. She was in a meeting but she picked up when she saw it was him — she always picked up.

"Tell me," she said.

He told her about the song structure, the weight markers, the twelve-thousand-year arc. He told her about the early records and the ice age humans and the quality of observation that had been sustained across all that time. He told her about the modern section and what it cost to listen to. He told her about Observer guiding him through it with the specific kind of patience that deep-dwellers brought to difficult material — not rushing, not lightening, not translating the grief into something more bearable, but staying with it at full weight and trusting that full weight was what the material required.

Maya was quiet for a long time after he finished.

"This is going to take years," she said. "To do properly."

"Yes."

"Are you all right?"

He thought about this. "I'm—" He looked at the bay. "I feel like I've been given something that's too large to hold and that I have to hold anyway. Like—" He paused. "Like inheriting something. Something that didn't belong to me personally but that I'm now responsible for."

Maya said: "Yes. That's exactly what it is. That's what Grandma felt. That's what I felt when I understood what the biological exchange really meant." A pause. "Welcome to the family business."

He had laughed, which surprised him, and it had been a releasing kind of laughter, the kind that happens when something large is acknowledged directly instead of being carried in silence.

"She would have wanted to know all of it," he said, meaning Claire.

"She would have asked every question you haven't thought of yet," Maya said. "Write them down and ask them for her."

He was doing that. He had a separate journal now just for the questions he was asking on behalf of the people who could no longer ask them directly. It was, he had noticed, getting full.

He thought: twelve thousand years. They were watching us twelve thousand years ago. The ocean level was lower by a hundred feet; where Willowport now stood, there had been a tidal estuary on a broad coastal plain, and the people who had lived there had been small bands of hunter-gatherers following the coastline, camping in rock shelters along what was now submerged, fishing the cold water in skin boats.

The deep-dwellers had been watching even then.

What they had observed — or rather, what the song preserved as the community's understanding of what their ancestors had observed — was people in relationship with the water. Not the relationship of industrial exploitation that would come much later. The relationship of dependence and respect and the very specific fear that comes from knowing you need something that can kill you. The people on that ancient coast had loved the sea and feared it and taken from it and given back to it — offerings, Thomas III understood from Observer's careful commentary, that the deep-dwellers had found meaningful. Not because the offerings had any practical value. Because they indicated that the people on the shore understood that the ocean was not merely a resource. That it had a nature of its own.

That understanding had persisted, the record showed, through the changes of the Holocene. Through the rising sea levels that had drowned the coastal plain and created the Bay. Through the succession of cultures that had replaced one another on the Maine coast, each one leaving its trace in the changing patterns of activity that the deep-dweller record tracked: the characteristic sounds of different types of fishing, the changing chemistry signatures of different kinds of human habitation, the acoustic record of boats that varied from log canoe to birch bark to wooden plank to fiber and steel.

The deep-dwellers had been watching all of it.

Thomas III had tried to explain the scale of this to his academic committee and had found, as he often found, that the English concepts available to him were not quite equal to what he was trying to convey. It was not merely that the deep-dwellers had records spanning twelve thousand years, though the records themselves were remarkable enough. It was the quality of the attention that the records represented. The consistency of it. The deep-dwellers had not occasionally observed the humans on the coast above them; they had attended to the humans continuously, across every generation of their own civilization, accumulating a perspective on human development that made every human historical account look, by comparison, like a photograph from a single afternoon.

"Observer," he had asked, the previous week, floating at thirty meters while Observer moved in slow circles around him in the way that meant sustained, comprehensive communication was coming. "When deep-dwellers look at human history from this perspective — all of it, from the people on the coastal plain to now — what is the most important thing you see?"

Observer had taken a long time to respond. The response, when it came, had used three communication channels simultaneously and had required Thomas III to sit in the water for nearly a minute before he attempted translation.

What he eventually wrote in his journal was: "They see a species that keeps forgetting it knows something and then remembering it again. And each time the remembering happens, it happens at a higher level. The forgetting is not failure. It is—" He had crossed out several attempts. "The forgetting is like the tide going out. The remembering is like it coming back. Further each time. Incorporating more."

He was sitting with that still.

He thought about his great-great-grandfather, who had spent forty years on this water listening to something he couldn't translate. He thought about his grandmother, who had spent thirty years building the institutional framework for a conversation that was just now beginning to approach the depth it required. He thought about his mother, making the case in The Hague for the recognition that Observer's civilization deserved. He thought about the ice-age people in their skin boats on the estuary beach, making offerings to the water they feared and loved, twelve thousand years ago, observed by the same civilization that was observing him now.

He thought about the witness marker on the seafloor, the sediment-encrusted archive of twelve millennia of attention.

Something the size of that was difficult to hold in a human mind — in any single human mind — and perhaps that was the point. It wasn't supposed to fit in one mind. The Merritt Center for Interspecies Relations existed because the understanding required was too large for any single generation or discipline or perspective. It was distributed knowledge, knowledge that existed in the network of people and institutions and relationships rather than in any single repository.

Which was, Thomas III thought, structurally similar to how the deep-dwellers kept their history.

He wrote this in his journal. Then he wrote: "I think Observer knows this. I think this is why Observer agreed to work with me directly. Not because I am exceptional but because I am distributed. I carry the Merritt history — four generations of attention to this water — and I carry the deep-dweller community's history — Thomas III's years of relationship — and I carry my own bilingual access. I'm not a single researcher. I'm a connection between several archives."

He capped his pen and looked at the water.

His grandmother would be on the Institute porch at this hour, in the warmth that her seventy-five-year-old joints required, reading the morning's correspondence. He would go tell her what he had found. He would try to find the right words. She would listen with the quality of attention she had always brought — the deep patience, the willingness to hold complexity without forcing it to resolution — and she would probably ask two or three questions that reframed everything.

He thought: I have been educated by the most extraordinary teacher available to any human being, and her name is Claire Merritt, and the education I received from her was mostly: how to pay attention.

He thought: and she received that education from a man who sat in a boat in the dark, alone, and wrote down what he heard.

He thought: twelve thousand years. They were watching us twelve thousand years ago.

He stood, stretching the joints that had been compressed at depth, and rolled his shoulders, and looked out at the bay that had been a coastal plain when the first deep-dweller had bent its attention toward the figures on the shore.

He would go tell his grandmother. She would want to know.

She had always wanted to know. That was the thing about Claire Merritt — not her courage, not her institutional intelligence, not her skill in negotiation or her scientific rigor, though she had all of those. The thing about her was the wanting to know. The appetite for the truth of things, even when the truth was large and uncomfortable and required everything you had to look at directly.

Twelve thousand years.

He walked up the dock toward the Institute building, the morning sun on his back and the salt water drying on his skin, carrying what he had been given to carry.

His grandmother was on the Institute porch, as he had expected. She was in the canvas chair, in the warmth, with the morning's correspondence on her lap and her reading glasses pushed up on her forehead in the way that meant she had been reading and had stopped to think. She looked up when she heard his footsteps on the dock boards.

"How deep?" she asked.

"Forty-two meters. The witness marker section."

She took off her glasses entirely, which meant she wanted to give him her full attention. "Tell me."

He sat on the porch steps and told her. He told her about the oldest layer of the encoding and the ice age people on the coastal plain and what the deep-dwellers had made of the first humans they observed. He told her about the weight markers and the way the historical density increased in the records leading up to the industrial period. He told her about the mourning ceremonies. He told her about what Observer had said about hope without a timeline.

She listened with her hands in her lap and her eyes on the bay and did not interrupt until he finished.

"Your great-great-grandfather," she said. "His phrase. The weight of tides."

"It's in their record. Or something so close to it that the distance doesn't matter."

She was quiet for a long time. The bay was doing its midmorning things: a few lobster boats at the north end, the research vessel coming in from an overnight trip, the ordinary life of a harbor that had been doing this for three hundred years and that was now, also, the center of something unprecedented.

"He would have wanted to know," she said. "He spent forty years wondering if he was right. He was right about more than he knew."

"I'm going to include it in the archive project," Thomas III said. "A note about the correspondence. The two records of the same phrase."

"Good." She put her glasses back on and looked at him with the expression he had known all his life — the complete attention, the quality of care that asked nothing of him but his presence. "How are you?"

"I'm — I keep thinking about the scale of it. Twelve thousand years. They've been watching us for twelve thousand years and they still decided we were worth waiting for."

"Yes," she said. "Hold onto that. That's the most important thing in the archive."

She handed him a cup of coffee from the table beside her chair — she had apparently brought two, knowing he would come — and he sat on the porch steps in the August sun and drank it, and they were quiet together in the way they had been quiet together since he was small, and the bay did its work around them, and the twelve thousand years of record below the surface continued accumulating its next entry.
