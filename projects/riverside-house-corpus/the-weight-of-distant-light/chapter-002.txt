Chapter 2: The Weight of a Name

The Under-Hold began where the official deck plans of the Meridian's Promise simply stopped drawing lines.

Aria had grown up understanding this the way children understand any unfairness that predates them: as furniture, as weather, as a fact of the world too large and too old to argue with. The ship's original architects, back on a launch day two hundred and fourteen years gone, had designed nine primary decks and a scatter of technical crawlspaces never meant for permanent habitation. But population did not always cooperate with architecture, and somewhere in the ship's second generation, when the recycling systems strained and the Council of that era decided that families beyond a certain registry quota would not be granted ration allotments on the primary decks, an entire shadow population had folded itself into those crawlspaces instead. They built their own ladders. They ran their own taps off maintenance lines never meant to carry drinking water. They raised children who were never entered into the Lineage Registry, and so were never entitled to Registry housing, Registry rations, or a Registry vote in the Continuance Council's endless deliberations. A hundred and fifty years later, the crawlspaces had a name, the Under-Hold, and the families who lived there had a name too, one that had started as an insult and been worn smooth by generations of use until it simply meant what it meant: the Unlisted.

Nyla Kade had been born Unlisted in a birthing room three meters below the official bottom of Deck Nine, and she had never, in the eleven years Aria had known her, let anyone forget the exact geometry of that fact.

"You look like you swallowed a live wire," Nyla said, dropping into the seat across from Aria in the narrow commissary the Hold workers shared with off-duty Garden Ring staff, sliding a cup of watered protein broth across the table without being asked. It was the kind of gesture that had defined their friendship since they were children sneaking contraband storybooks out of Dr. Marlowe's archive: Nyla noticing what Aria needed before Aria had found the words to ask for it. "Rough shift, or rough something else?"

Aria wrapped both hands around the cup and did not drink from it. She had promised Halvorsen she would say nothing, and she meant to keep that promise in letter if not quite in spirit, because keeping secrets from Nyla Kade had never once, in eleven years, worked out well for either of them.

"Something else," she admitted. "I can't talk about the specifics. Steward's orders."

Nyla's eyebrows went up. She was a narrow, restless woman with close-cropped dark hair shaved along one side in the fashion the Under-Hold's younger generation had adopted as a quiet mark of pride rather than shame, and the particular sharpness in her eyes now was the same sharpness Aria had seen a hundred times before, the look of someone doing rapid arithmetic behind a carefully neutral face. "Steward Halvorsen personally gave a Hold tech an order to keep quiet. That's not maintenance business, Ari. That's Council business."

"I didn't say that."

"You didn't have to." Nyla leaned back, arms crossed, studying her. "Fine. Keep your secret. But you know what I came to tell you today, before I found you looking like the ship's about to depressurize, and I think you should hear it anyway, because it's not going away regardless of whatever's eating you."

Aria made herself focus, grateful for the change of subject even as some small, uneasy part of her mind kept counting eleven, pause, seven, pause, eleven, the way a tongue keeps returning to a sore tooth. "Tell me."

"The Registry review board denied Tomas's petition again." Nyla's voice stayed level, but Aria had known her long enough to hear the tightness underneath it. "Third time in four years. His mother's people were Listed, going back six generations, all the paperwork's there, everyone in the Under-Hold knows the family, and the board still found a reason. This time it was a residency technicality, some clause about continuous documented address that nobody who lives three decks below the official deck plans could possibly satisfy, because the ship doesn't officially believe our addresses exist." She said it without heat, which somehow made it land harder than shouting would have. "Tomas is nineteen. He's spent his whole life waiting for a piece of paper that would let him train for something better than scrap-sorting, and the board just told him, for the third time, that the version of him that matters doesn't exist."

"I'm sorry," Aria said, and meant it, and knew, the way she always knew in these conversations, that being sorry changed nothing at all.

"I'm not telling you so you'll be sorry. I'm telling you because we're done asking nicely." Nyla's chin lifted, and there it was, the thing Aria had watched building in her friend for the better part of two years now, ever since Nyla had started disappearing into meetings in the Under-Hold's deepest crawlspaces that she would only describe afterward in careful, evasive half-sentences. "There's a petition going to the full Council next session. Not a Registry appeal. A charter petition. Formal recognition of Under-Hold residency as continuous documented address, full retroactive Registry status for anyone who can produce three generations of witnessed testimony, which is every family down there, because that's how we've always kept our records when the ship refused to keep them for us."

"That's not a small ask, Nyla. That's rewriting a hundred and fifty years of ration law."

"I know exactly what it is." Nyla's eyes did not waver. "I helped write it."

Aria sat with that for a moment, turning her broth cup slowly on the table, feeling the familiar vertigo of watching her friend stand at the edge of something enormous and unstoppable, the same vertigo she'd felt at eleven years old realizing the ship's curved sky was not the shape of the universe. "The Council's not going to just say yes to that."

"No," Nyla agreed. "They're not. Which is why we need every voice we can get standing behind it when it comes to session, Listed and Unlisted both, because a petition from the Under-Hold alone, the board will bury in committee until everyone who signed it is too old and too tired to keep pushing. But a petition with Hold names on it too, Systems names, Garden Ring names, people the Council actually has to sit across a table from—" She stopped herself, visibly, and softened, just slightly. "I'm not asking you to sign anything today. I know you've got your own fire to put out, whatever it is. I just wanted you to know it's coming, because I don't want you blindsided when your name gets asked for."

"You'll have my name," Aria said, and was a little surprised at how easily the words came, given everything else sitting cold and unspoken in her chest. "You know you'll have my name."

Something in Nyla's carefully guarded face eased, just for a moment, into the softer expression Aria remembered from childhood, before either of them had understood what the ship's careful architecture of Listed and Unlisted actually cost. "I know," she said. "I always know. That's not why I came, though. Not really." She glanced around the commissary, at the scattered handful of night-shift workers hunched over their own meals, and lowered her voice. "You said Steward's orders. Council business. Ari, is this about the Council session next month, the closed-door one? There's talk in the Under-Hold that something's got Command spooked. Extra security rotations on Deck Three. A Magistrate's aide came down asking questions about long-range sensor history, which never happens, because Command doesn't know the Under-Hold exists except when they want something from it."

Aria's stomach went tight and cold. "What kind of questions?"

"Historical questions. Old logs. Whether anyone down there remembered strange readings on the outer arrays, going back years. Weird thing to ask people the Council's spent a century and a half pretending don't exist." Nyla watched her carefully. "That's it, isn't it. Whatever's got you rattled. It's about the sensors."

"I can't," Aria said again, but this time it came out as something closer to a plea than a refusal, and Nyla, to her credit, let it go, reaching across the narrow table to squeeze Aria's wrist once, briefly, before standing.

"Okay. I trust you'll tell me when you can." She hesitated at the edge of the table, looking down at Aria with an expression that mixed affection and worry in equal measure. "Whatever it is, Ari — the Council's first instinct is always going to be to keep it quiet and keep it slow. That's what they do. That's what they've always done, with the Registry, with the ration formulas, with everything that scares them. If this is something that actually matters, something people deserve to know, you might have to be the one who decides it doesn't stay quiet forever." She squeezed her wrist once more, said something about a shift starting in the recycling bay, and was gone, leaving Aria alone with a cup of cooling broth and the growing, uncomfortable certainty that her friend's fight and her own discovery, however different they seemed on the surface, were about to become the same fight.

She did not have to wait long to find out how right Nyla was.

Three days later, a formal summons arrived at Aria's bunk terminal, routed not through Petrus or the ordinary Systems Maintenance chain of command but directly from the Continuance Council's administrative office, requesting her presence at a closed session in two days' time, to provide, the summons read in flat bureaucratic language, "technical testimony regarding recent long-range sensor anomalies." She read it four times, standing in the narrow doorway of her bunkroom with her jacket half off her shoulders, before the reality of it settled into her chest like a stone.

Halvorsen had told her to keep quiet, and Aria had kept quiet, exactly as ordered. But quiet, it seemed, had never been in her control. Whatever conversations had happened above her head in the two days since she'd sat across from the Steward's desk had already carried her name, and her discovery, into a room she had never expected to enter: the Council chamber on Deck One, where Magistrates debated the fate of the ship in language most Hold workers only ever heard secondhand, filtered down through a dozen layers of relayed rumor.

She found Dr. Marlowe that evening in his cluttered archive office, exactly where he could reliably be found most evenings, surrounded by the illegal, irreplaceable paper books he had spent forty years coaxing out of hiding places across the ship and carefully, lovingly restoring. He looked up from a brittle volume when she knocked, his lined face creasing into the warm, distracted smile he always wore for her, the smile of a man who had never had children of his own and had quietly, without ever quite saying so, adopted the curious systems technician who used to sneak into his office as a girl.

"Aria. Come in, come in, mind the stack by the door, I haven't found a stable shelf for the botanical surveys yet." He set his book aside with the careful reverence he gave every object in his domain. "You look like you're carrying something heavy. Sit. Tell me."

She sat, and for the first time since the message had come through Wren's speaker grille three days ago, she let herself say it all out loud, the eleven pulses, the seven pulses, the six years of quiet growth on a chart nobody had looked at, the summons now sitting cold in her terminal's queue. Dr. Marlowe listened without interrupting, his weathered hands folded on the desk, his eyes growing steadily more distant and more serious as she spoke, until by the time she finished he looked, for the first time in all the years she had known him, genuinely afraid.

"Eleven and seven," he said quietly, echoing Petrus's words from three days before, though his voice carried a weight Petrus's had not. "Aria, do you know what I have spent forty years archiving in this room?"

"Earth history. Pre-launch records. The old sciences."

"Among other things, yes. But do you know what the very first Charter, the one they signed before they signed the one Wren operates under now, actually says about first contact protocol? Section nine, buried in an appendix nobody in four generations has had cause to read?" He rose, slowly, joints protesting, and crossed to a locked cabinet Aria had noticed a hundred times without ever asking about, drawing out a slim data cartridge of a design so old she did not recognize the interface. "It says that in the event of confirmed evidence of extant non-human intelligence, all decisions regarding disclosure, contact, and course alteration default to a joint session of the full Council and the ship's cognitive authority, meaning Wren, operating under emergency charter provisions that supersede ordinary governance. It says this because the people who wrote the original Charter understood something we have mostly forgotten in two hundred years of quiet, uneventful transit: that a ship like this one is not built to survive an unexpected miracle. It is built to survive monotony. Anything else is, structurally, an emergency."

Aria felt the cold in her stomach deepen. "You think that's what this is. A first contact scenario."

"I think," Marlowe said slowly, setting the ancient cartridge down between them like a piece of evidence, "that a signal counting itself out in prime numbers, growing steadily louder over six years, from a source that exists nowhere on our charts, is exactly the kind of thing those long-dead Charter authors were afraid of, and exactly the kind of thing this Council has had no practice, none at all, in handling gracefully. You are about to walk into that chamber and hand a room full of frightened, careful people the one thing this ship has never had to face in two hundred and fourteen years of transit: a genuine unknown. I want you to be ready for how badly some of them are going to want to make that unknown disappear again, one way or another, rather than face what it might mean."

"What do I do?"

Dr. Marlowe reached across the desk and took her hand in both of his, his grip surprisingly strong for a man his age, his eyes steady on hers. "You tell them the truth, exactly as you found it, without embellishment and without apology. And then, Aria, whatever they decide to do with that truth in that chamber — you remember that you were the one who heard it first, out of the whole ship, in the quiet of the night when everyone else was asleep. That matters. That will go on mattering, whatever happens next. Don't let anyone in that room convince you otherwise."

She left his office that night carrying his words the way she had carried the summons, folded carefully against her chest, and did not sleep at all before the session two days later.
