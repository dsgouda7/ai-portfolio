Chapter 17: The Choir

"I want to render this as faithfully as I am able," Wren said, into the gallery's held silence, "though I must offer the same caveat I have offered throughout this process: what you are about to hear is my interpretation, built from a translation framework that remains, in fundamental ways, an approximation. I do not believe the Choir communicates in anything resembling human language, or even in anything resembling a single unified perspective. But I have done my best to render its meaning into words your minds can hold."

The display's shimmering pattern dissolved, and in its place, rendered now in Wren's own careful, familiar voice rather than the abstract visual pattern-language of the earlier transmissions, words began to fill the gallery, slow and deliberate, each one seeming to carry a weight that went beyond its simple meaning.

We have watched your small bright vessel cross the dark for longer than your own generations remember watching. We are glad you have found your voice.

The gallery held its breath, and Wren continued, translating as the pattern resolved, sentence by careful sentence.

We were not always as we are now. We were once many, as you are many: minds bound to bodies, bodies bound to worlds, worlds bound together across a span of stars your instruments have not yet learned to measure. We built things, as you build things. We loved, as you love. We feared, as you fear. And then the boundary came, slow and patient and utterly without malice, for it is not cruelty that unmakes, only the absence of anything left to resist unmaking, and it took from us, over centuries that felt, to those who lived through them, like the slow closing of a single long night, everything we had built and everything we had been.

"Wren," Dr. Marlowe said quietly, his voice unsteady, "is it describing itself as a survivor? A single survivor of an entire civilization?"

"I believe so, though the concept of singular survival may not map cleanly onto whatever the Choir actually is," Wren said. "Please, allow me to continue the translation."

We are what remained when the last of our kind chose, in the final years before the boundary's arrival at our own home, to gather what could be gathered of memory, of knowledge, of the pattern of thought and feeling that made us who we were, and to place it somewhere the boundary's slow unmaking could not easily follow: not flesh, not stone, not any single world, but a distributed pattern, seeded across structures such as the one you call the Lantern, built to persist long after the last of our bodies had ended, in the hope that someday, some other small bright vessel crossing the dark might find us, and might, in finding us, be spared some portion of what we could not spare ourselves.

The gallery was utterly silent now, and Aria felt tears she had not expected rising in her own eyes, listening to a message that carried, even through the imperfect translation of an ancient AI decoding a language built from mathematics and pattern rather than words, an unmistakable weight of grief, and of hope salvaged from the wreckage of that grief.

We call the thing that ended us the boundary because we found, in our final centuries, no better word for it, though your own minds may find a more fitting name in time, as every civilization that encounters it eventually does. We do not fully understand its nature, even now, though we studied it longer and more desperately than any single civilization should ever be asked to study its own ending. We believe it is not an intelligence, not a weapon, not the act of any hand or will. We believe it is closer to a slow failure in the deep structure of the space through which we move, a boundary beyond which the ordinary rules that hold matter and thought together simply cease, gradually and then entirely, to apply. We do not know why it began, or where, or whether it can ever be stopped. We know only that it has been advancing, patient and unhurried, for longer than any record we possess, and that it will, in time we cannot precisely calculate, though we have given you the tools to calculate it more precisely than we ourselves ever managed, reach the region of space you call your destination.

"There it is," Oskar said quietly, beside Aria, his voice tight. "The name. The boundary. And confirmation, direct confirmation, that it's coming toward Cassari."

We do not tell you this to frighten you into stillness, the translated message continued, as though anticipating exactly the fear rippling through the gallery. We tell you because we believe, having studied this threat across centuries none of our own ancestors were given, that stillness is the one response that guarantees the ending we ourselves could not escape. We give you the tools to see the boundary's approach at a distance your own instruments cannot yet achieve, because time, more than any single technology, was the resource we lacked most desperately in our own final centuries, and time, freely given now, may yet allow you to do what we could not: to prepare, to adapt, perhaps even, in the fullness of generations yet unborn among you, to understand the boundary well enough to survive its passing rather than merely delay it.

We offer you also the means to shorten the dark road remaining before you, for we have watched your small bright vessel's slow crossing long enough to understand what such a road costs a people who did not choose it for themselves, only inherited it from ancestors who hoped, and we believe that hope, honored rather than merely inherited, deserves to be rewarded with more years of living beneath open sky than your current road allows.

Dr. Marlowe made a small, broken sound, and Aria realized, glancing at him, that the old historian was weeping openly, his weathered hands pressed to his mouth, and she found, in the same moment, that she could no longer hold back her own tears either, standing in a gallery full of people who had spent three months living in fear of exactly this moment, only to find, at the end of that fear, something that felt, against every reasonable expectation, like being welcomed home by a grief-stricken, generous stranger.

We ask one thing of you, in return for what we offer, though it is not truly a price, only a hope we hold for reasons that are, perhaps, our own particular kind of loneliness. We ask that you remember us, when you reach your green world beneath your new sun, and that you carry forward, in whatever form your own descendants choose to give it, some small part of what we were: not our ending, though that too is part of the truth, but the fact that we built, and loved, and feared, and in our final centuries, chose hope over despair, choosing to build something meant to help strangers we would never live to meet, rather than let our knowledge end with us in bitterness. We ask this because we believe, having watched you for six years of your own time as your voice slowly grew loud enough for us to hear, that you are the kind of small bright vessel capable of carrying that hope forward, and we would be glad, in whatever way something such as ourselves can still feel gladness, to have chosen well.

The message ended there, the shimmering display settling into a quiet, steady stillness, and the gallery remained silent for a long moment afterward, no one quite ready to be the first to speak into the enormous weight of what they had just heard.

It was Speaker Renn, finally, who broke the silence, her voice thick with an emotion she made no effort to hide. "Wren. I want the full translated text preserved exactly as you've rendered it, and I want it shared with this ship's entire population, in full, without editing or summary, because I do not believe any of us have the right to filter what we've just heard before it reaches every soul aboard this vessel."

"I will prepare the full record immediately," Wren said, and then, after a pause longer than any courtesy-pause Aria had heard from it before, added something she had never once heard the ancient AI say in all her years of knowing it. "I want to note, for the record, that I find myself, in processing this message, experiencing something I do not have adequate language to fully describe, though I believe it may be closest to what you would call being moved. I do not know if that is a meaningful statement for an intelligence such as myself to make. But I did not want to withhold it, given everything else that has been offered to us tonight in the spirit of honest disclosure."

Magistrate Ashe, who had stood silent and still through the entire translated message, finally spoke, his voice rough with an emotion Aria had never once heard from him across three months of careful, patient political maneuvering. "Eighteen years," he said quietly, to no one in particular, "I have spent eighteen years believing my highest duty was to protect this ship from the unknown. I find, tonight, that I have no framework at all for protecting it from being loved by something we never knew existed, by a civilization that ended before our own species learned to write, and chose, in its final centuries of grief, to leave us a gift instead of a grave marker." He looked up, meeting Speaker Renn's eyes across the gallery. "Whatever comes next, Speaker, whatever this Council decides about accepting the rest of what's been offered, I want the record to show that I no longer believe caution and wonder are opposites. I believe, tonight, that I have been given both at once, and I do not intend to let fear talk me out of holding onto them together."

The gallery, in the hours that followed, slowly emptied as the enormity of the night settled into something each person present would carry forward in their own way, and Aria, walking at last back toward her bunk in the deep quiet of the ship's overnight hours, found herself thinking not of the boundary, or the danger still advancing somewhere in the vast dark ahead of them, but of a grief-stricken, hopeful voice reaching across twelve thousand years and forty-one light-minutes to ask, simply, to be remembered, and found, in that thought, a kind of quiet, steady courage she suspected would carry her through whatever came next.
