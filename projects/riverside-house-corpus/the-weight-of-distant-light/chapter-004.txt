Chapter 4: Passive Investigation

The Council reconvened the following afternoon, and by the time Speaker Renn called the chamber to order, word of the closed session had already leaked, in the maddening, unstoppable way that word always leaked aboard a ship where nine thousand people lived within shouting distance of each other. Aria had woken to find Petrus waiting for her outside her bunkroom, his broad face drawn tight with worry, asking in a low voice whether it was true that Command had found something out past the debris shell, and by the time she reached the Systems Maintenance bay for her shift, three separate colleagues had already asked her some version of the same question, each one citing a slightly different, slightly more distorted version of the story, until by midday the rumor had transformed, through nine thousand mouths, into everything from a derelict Earth-era colony ship to an invading fleet.

She said nothing to confirm or deny any of it, as Halvorsen had instructed, and found the silence harder to keep with every passing hour.

The second session was shorter than the first, and far more procedural. Speaker Renn laid out both proposals as promised: Magistrate Ashe's course correction, costed at six to nine additional years of transit and a hard reduction in life support redundancy that Wren estimated would increase mortality risk for the ship's eldest and youngest residents by a measurable margin; and a second proposal, drafted overnight by Renn's own office in consultation with Wren, for continued passive investigation at current heading, with a defined escalation protocol that would trigger an emergency session and immediate course reassessment if the signal's character changed in any of several specified ways.

The vote, when it finally came, was closer than Aria expected, seventeen to fourteen in favor of continued passive investigation, with two abstentions. Magistrate Ashe's bloc had held together almost entirely, a fact that told Aria, watching from the same small witness floor she had stood on the day before, that whatever fight was beginning here in this domed chamber was going to be a long one, fought seat by seat, vote by vote, for as long as the mystery ahead of them remained unresolved.

What she had not expected, walking out of the chamber that afternoon, was to be intercepted by Speaker Renn herself, moving quickly to catch her before she reached the corridor doors, her Council robes traded already for the simpler practical coat she seemed to prefer outside formal session.

"Technician Voss. A moment, if you'll give me one."

Aria stopped, startled. "Of course, Speaker."

Renn glanced once around the emptying chamber before lowering her voice. "The Council has authorized continued passive investigation, which means, practically speaking, someone needs to actually conduct that investigation, and I want it conducted by people I trust to be thorough and honest with what they find, rather than people who will shade their findings toward whichever outcome makes their own position easier. I'm forming a small working group, reporting directly to my office rather than through the ordinary Steward hierarchy, to manage the technical side of this. I want you on it."

Aria blinked. "Speaker, I'm a maintenance technician. I fix relay nodes. I don't have the training for—"

"You have the training to have noticed a pattern that six years of Steward review cycles missed entirely," Renn said, cutting her off gently but firmly. "You have the instinct, and I watched you demonstrate the honesty, yesterday, to tell this Council you didn't know something rather than performing false certainty in either direction. That is worth more to me right now than a formal xenology credential nobody aboard this ship actually holds, because we have never needed one before. I'm also bringing in Dr. Marlowe from the archive, since apparently he's already been consulted informally, and Officer Oskar Delane from Deck Nine security, who has the clearance and the discretion to keep this working group's activities out of the rumor mill as much as that's still possible. I would like you to lead the technical sensor analysis. Will you do it?"

Aria thought of Dr. Marlowe's warning, two nights before, about the weight of being the one who heard it first, and found, standing in the emptying chamber with the Speaker's steady gaze on her, that she did not actually have to think very hard about her answer. "Yes, Speaker. I'll do it."

"Good," Renn said, and for the first time since Aria had met her, something like real warmth broke through the careful composure of her office. "Then let's get to work. I don't think we have as much time as Magistrate Ashe would like to believe we do."

The working group's first formal meeting convened two days later, in a cramped technical office on Deck Two that had until recently served as overflow storage for outdated navigational hardware, hastily cleared and repurposed at Renn's direct order. Dr. Marlowe arrived first, characteristically laden with a satchel of paper notes he insisted on cross-referencing against Wren's digital archive despite Wren's gentle, repeated observation that its own records were both more complete and more easily searched. Officer Oskar Delane arrived next, a lean, quiet young man perhaps two years older than Aria, with the watchful stillness of someone who had spent his career learning to read a room before speaking in it, and who greeted Aria with a brief, genuine nod that suggested he, at least, held no illusions about the informal hierarchy of credentials the Speaker had waved away.

"I've read your report twice," he said, settling into a chair at the room's single cluttered table. "You found something in six years of ignored variance data that an entire Steward review cycle missed. I want to know how, because whatever instinct let you do that is probably going to matter a great deal more before this is over."

"I wasn't looking for it," Aria admitted. "I was just doing a routine diagnostic and it took longer than it should have because I got curious about a pattern. That's not exactly a methodology."

"It's more of one than most people ever develop," Delane said, and left it there, which Aria found she appreciated more than she expected to.

Wren's voice filled the small office from its single ceiling grille, and over the following hours, the four of them, three humans and an ancient distributed intelligence, began the slow, careful work of trying to understand what lay ahead of them in the dark.

"I want to begin," Wren said, "by sharing what I have determined since yesterday's session regarding the encoded content beneath the prime-count pulse layer. I want to be direct about the limits of this analysis, because I am aware that false certainty in either direction would be a disservice to this group's purpose."

"Go ahead," Renn said, though she was not present in person, joining instead by a live audio link from her office, the pressures of Council business apparently too great even for a discovery this significant to pull her fully away.

"The prime-count pulses, eleven and seven, function as what I now believe is an attention header, a deliberately simple, mathematically universal pattern designed to be recognizable by any technological civilization capable of detecting radio-frequency signals at all, regardless of that civilization's language, culture, or cognitive architecture. It is, in essence, a knock on a door, phrased in the one language its creators could reasonably assume any listener would share: the language of countable, indivisible numbers. Beneath that header lies a second layer, far denser, which I have begun to partially decode using a comparative framework borrowed from historical human efforts, cataloged in Dr. Marlowe's archive, to design similar universal first-contact messages during the twentieth and twenty-first centuries on Earth."

Dr. Marlowe's eyes had lit up at this, in the particular way Aria had come to recognize as the historian glimpsing, however faintly, a link between his beloved archive and the living present. "The old interstellar plaques," he murmured. "The ones sent out on the first unmanned probes, before crewed vessels like this one were ever conceived. Pictographic star maps, elemental diagrams, mathematical constants rendered as universal reference points."

"Precisely," Wren said. "The encoded layer beneath the prime-count header appears to follow a similar logic, though vastly more sophisticated in execution: a nested sequence of mathematical and physical constants, rendered in a format that assumes a listener capable of counting, capable of basic arithmetic, and capable of recognizing the ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter as a meaningful, universal constant. I believe this sequence functions as a kind of primer, a shared vocabulary being established before any more complex content is transmitted. I have not yet decoded anything beyond this primer layer. I want to be clear that I do not know what lies beneath it, only that something does, because the primer's structure clearly anticipates further content that has not yet been transmitted, or that we have not yet successfully received."

"Not yet transmitted," Delane repeated slowly. "You mean it's waiting for something."

"That is one interpretation consistent with the data," Wren said. "Another is that the deeper content is being transmitted continuously, but at a signal strength currently below our detection threshold, in which case it may become receivable as our approach continues and the signal strengthens, as it has been strengthening steadily for six years. A third interpretation, which I raise only because intellectual honesty requires it, is that the primer layer is designed to establish a shared vocabulary specifically so that the deeper content can only be meaningfully transmitted once we have demonstrated, through some action or transmission of our own, that we have successfully received and understood the primer. In which case, the object may be waiting for a response before it says anything more."

The room was quiet for a long moment, the weight of that last possibility settling over all four of them.

"A conversation," Aria said finally. "It's not just broadcasting. It wants us to answer."

"I cannot confirm that interpretation with confidence," Wren said. "But I consider it plausible enough that this working group should begin preparing for the possibility that a decision regarding response, not merely observation, may be required of this ship sooner than Magistrate Ashe's faction would prefer."

Officer Delane leaned back in his chair, his watchful stillness taking on a harder edge. "Which is going to make Ashe's position a great deal more difficult to hold, politically, the moment word of this gets out. Passive investigation was a vote he could live with, because passive investigation implies we're just watching, just gathering information, not committing to anything. The moment this working group's findings suggest the object is waiting for a reply, the whole framing of the debate changes. It stops being should we look, and starts being should we answer, and I don't think there's a version of that debate Ashe's bloc wins cleanly, because half this ship, the moment they understand there might be another mind out there patient enough to wait six years for us to notice it exists, is going to want to say hello."

"And the other half," Renn's voice added quietly through the audio link, "is going to be more afraid than they have ever been in their entire lives, because for two hundred and fourteen years, this ship's whole identity has rested on being alone in the dark, self-sufficient, answerable to no one and nothing beyond its own Charter and its own careful continuity. I need this working group to move quickly, and I need you to move quietly, because the moment this leaves this room in anything other than a fully considered, carefully framed form, I lose control of how this ship's population processes it, and a frightened population of nine thousand people with nowhere to run is not a problem any of us want to discover the shape of."

Aria thought, then, of Nyla's warning in the commissary three days before, of the Preservationist aide asking questions in the Under-Hold about old sensor readings, of Magistrate Ashe's careful, patient authority in the Council chamber, and understood, with a growing unease she could not quite name, that the quiet Renn was asking for was already, in small and uncontrollable ways, beginning to slip.

She did not know yet how right she was to be uneasy. She would not learn, for another eleven days, that a copy of her original sensor report, the one she had handed to Steward Halvorsen in good faith and confidence, had already found its way, through channels she would never fully trace, into the hands of people who had no intention of waiting quietly for a working group's careful, considered conclusions.
