Chapter 17: Following the Money

Priya Mehta arrived in Ashmont Bay on a Monday morning with two duffel bags and a laptop that had never been connected to a home network since March. She had driven herself from Portland in a car she had borrowed from Devika Rao, and she called Elena from a gas station on the south side of the city to confirm she had arrived and to ask for directions to the safe location Marcus had arranged.

The location was an apartment above a hardware store in the working-class residential neighborhood on the bay's eastern ridge, owned by a retired police officer named Clarence Webb who rented it occasionally to people Marcus trusted. It was unremarkable in every useful way — ordinary building, ordinary block, no connection to Elena's office or to the Harbor Development Project, with a view of the back alley and a front window that saw the quiet residential street. Priya looked it over carefully when she arrived and said nothing about it, which Elena took as a sign that it met whatever standard she'd developed during eight months of self-protective solitude.

They sat at the kitchen table with Priya's laptop and a printed copy of the case structure Elena had built from the documents and interviews assembled so far. Marcus arrived thirty minutes later with food from the diner two blocks away, ate standing at the counter, and listened without interrupting while Priya walked them through Santos' documentation.

David Santos had been a methodical builder of cases. His backup files were organized with the care of someone who understood that the documentation might need to be read by people who hadn't been present for the investigation — not just as notes to himself but as a comprehensible record for others. He had dated every entry, labeled every source, and written clear analytical memos connecting the evidence to the conclusions it supported.

The financial audit trail was the foundation. Santos had begun the Harbor Development Project audit with the routine scope of his assignment: reviewing Pacific Harbor Corp's preliminary expenditure records for the eighteen months of pre-construction work, examining invoices and contracts and subcontractor payments for legitimacy. What he found within the first three weeks was systematic. The preliminary phase overruns were not the result of genuine cost escalation — unexpected soil conditions, revised engineering specifications, permitting delays. They were the result of payments to a set of subcontractors who had no operational presence and whose services could not be verified by any physical evidence.

He had documented seven subcontractors in this category. Their names were unremarkable — generic geographic names that could plausibly belong to legitimate coastal construction firms. But when Santos requested certificates of insurance, bonding records, and operational permits from each of them, all seven had provided documentation from the same insurance broker, the same bonding agent, and with registration addresses in the same industrial park in Tacoma. He had then contacted the industrial park's property management company and confirmed that none of the seven companies were or had ever been tenants.

Seven phantom subcontractors, collectively receiving fifty-eight million dollars in payments from Pacific Harbor Corp's state-funded preliminary phase expenditures. The money entered Pacific Harbor Corp, was authorized through the company's internal approval process, and paid out to entities that existed only on paper.

Where did the money go from the phantom subcontractors? Santos had traced it. Each company maintained a bank account at one of three regional banks. The accounts received the construction payments and within seventy-two hours transferred the funds to a single receiving account at a private bank in the Cayman Islands. The Cayman account belonged to a trust entity called Pacific Coastal Heritage Trust.

"Pacific Coastal Heritage Trust," Elena said. "Not Pacific Harbor Corp."

"A separate entity," Priya said. "And this is where the structure becomes important." She pulled up a document that Santos had labeled Corporate Structure Analysis — Final. "Pacific Harbor Corp is the surface layer. It's the operating company that holds the state contract, employs the staff, and receives the public infrastructure funding. Beneath it is a complex of subsidiary entities that are linked by overlapping ownership structures. Pacific Harbor Development LLC is the primary subsidiary. But Pacific Harbor Corp itself is majority owned by Pacific Coastal Holdings LP, a limited partnership registered in Delaware."

She walked them through the ownership chart Santos had built. The chart was a diagram with boxes and arrows, and as Elena studied it, the visual logic became clear. Pacific Harbor Corp sat at the surface. Below it, Pacific Coastal Holdings LP held the controlling stake. And Pacific Coastal Holdings LP's general partner — the entity that controlled the partnership's management — was Pacific Coastal Heritage Trust.

The trust that was receiving the money from the phantom subcontractors was the entity that controlled the partnership that owned the operating company that received the state contract.

"They're paying themselves," Marcus said from the counter. "The state pays Pacific Harbor Corp. Pacific Harbor Corp pays phantom subcontractors. The phantom subcontractors pay the trust that controls Pacific Harbor Corp."

"Circular financial flow," Elena said. "The money leaves the project as apparent construction costs and re-enters it as trust capital, but it never actually gets spent on construction. It's a siphon."

"More than that," Priya said. "The trust capital is being invested. The Pacific Coastal Heritage Trust's filings show investment allocations across a portfolio that includes commercial real estate, mineral rights in three western states, and what's listed as 'coastal resource development reserves.' The extracted money is being deployed to build a separate asset base that will eventually be worth far more than the construction contracts."

Santos had estimated the total extraction through the phantom subcontractor mechanism at approximately eighty million dollars over the preliminary phase, with the mechanisms in place to extract three to four times that amount once full construction funding was released. But the real value was not the extracted construction payments — those were significant but finite. The real value was what the trust was positioned to own once the Harbor Development Project was complete.

"The submerged lands claim," Elena said.

"The submerged lands claim," Priya confirmed. "If the project completes and the facilities are certified as compliant and operational, Pacific Coastal Heritage Trust — through Pacific Harbor Corp — will be positioned to apply for extraction rights to the rare earth deposit. That application, under the 2018 precedent, has a strong probability of success. The deposit's estimated value is several billion dollars."

"Who controls the trust? Santos identified the beneficial owners as Winters, Blackwell, and Crane. I found a third name — Patricia Crane — but not the full trust structure."

Priya pulled up another document. "Santos worked the trust structure further than the surface disclosures. The trust's formation documents, filed in the Cayman Islands, list three co-settlors: Harlan Winters IV, Robert Blackwell III, and a woman named Eleanor Harrington. Eleanor Harrington's maiden name is Eleanor Crane — she is Patricia Crane's mother. The beneficial interest flows through multiple generations of the same families."

Elena studied the document. The founding families, three of the six, moving money through a structure that was sophisticated enough to require deliberate design and enough political infrastructure to suppress investigation. The same families that had falsified surveys in 1879 to steal the city's founding lands were falsifying surveys in the present to steal what lay beneath the harbor's floor.

"The fourth family," she said. "Is there a fourth? Santos said three. I found three. But six original families."

"Three of the six founding family lines have descendants still active in the city who are clearly not part of this structure — the Thorne family is essentially gone, the Kahale family is an active victim rather than a perpetrator, and the Mordecai inheritance was resolved through the previous case. That leaves the Ashmont line, whose principal representatives are either incarcerated or discredited. So the three founding families with active descendants and sufficient resources to participate are exactly the three we've identified." She paused. "Though I want to verify that the Ashmont line is truly excluded. James Ashmont is incarcerated. But he has cousins."

Elena made a note. Verify Ashmont family connection to Pacific Coastal Heritage Trust. It was a thread worth pulling.

Marcus finished the last of his food and moved to the table, sitting across from Priya and pulling the corporate structure diagram toward him. He studied it for a long time with the focused attention of someone identifying what would matter in court.

"The phantom subcontractor payments," he said. "Seven companies, fifty-eight million dollars, all routed to the same Cayman account. That's wire fraud, tax fraud, and conspiracy to commit government contract fraud at minimum. The state contract funding that went through Pacific Harbor Corp includes federal matching funds, which adds federal jurisdiction."

"Santos was building toward a federal referral," Priya said. "He was in contact with a contact at the FBI's white-collar crime unit. He called her on February fifteenth. Two days before he died."

"Do you know who?"

"A special agent named Lori Figueroa, Seattle field office. Santos had a phone note in his files with her name and a reminder to call about the Harbor Project referral. I looked her up. She's real, she's there, and she's been there for nine years."

Marcus and Elena looked at each other. A federal agent Santos had been planning to contact before his death. A contact who might or might not know what Santos had found, might or might not have been briefed by him in that February fifteenth call, and who was either a resource or a potential liability depending on whether the political insulation around the Harbor Project extended into the federal system.

"We need to know if Santos briefed Figueroa before he died," Elena said.

"I'll reach out through official channels," Marcus said. "I have a standing FBI liaison relationship from a previous case. I can contact Figueroa as a professional inquiry without immediately signaling what we're building." He paused. "But carefully. If she's been sitting on Santos' information for eight months, I want to know why before we hand her more of it."

Elena nodded. The caution was correct. The investigation had moved from the harbor to the financial structure and was now approaching the boundary where it would need institutional support to proceed to prosecution. That boundary was the most dangerous passage — the moment when powerful people would know that the threat to them had become concrete enough to require action beyond surveillance.

"There's something else in Santos' files," Priya said. She had been working toward this for the past hour, Elena realized, saving it for the right moment. "Something I haven't shown anyone. I want you both to understand the context before I show you."

She opened a separate folder on the laptop. Inside it was a single document, titled: Personal Memo — February 16 — D. Santos.

It was dated the day before he died.

The memo was short, written in the compressed language of someone organizing their own thoughts quickly. It read: Confirmed today through FW coastal hazard assessment report: pipeline easement on north shore recorded in 1931 intersects with proposed Pier 7 foundation footprint. Easement language does not expire. Pacific Harbor Corp's feasibility study omits this conflict. Either their engineers missed it or the company knows and chose not to disclose. If the latter: they cannot build Pier 7 as designed. The pier is the critical facility for establishing the adjacency claim under the 2018 precedent. Without Pier 7, the submerged lands application fails. This needs to go to state environmental review immediately. Calling Figueroa tomorrow first, then state.

He had called Figueroa the next day, February fifteenth. He had died on February seventeenth.

The weight of the memo settled over the table like weather.

"He found the structural flaw," Elena said. "The pier they need to establish the legal claim can't be built where they've planned to build it. Without that pier, the entire mechanism fails."

"And he was going to report it to the FBI and then to state environmental review," Marcus said. He looked at the memo for another moment. "Which means the people behind this project learned on February fifteenth or sixteenth that he was about to destroy it. And he was dead on February seventeenth."

"Did Figueroa receive Santos' call?" Elena asked Priya.

"I don't know. His phone records don't show the outcome of the call. Only that it was made."

Marcus pulled out his phone and stood. "I'm calling my FBI liaison contact now. This is no longer something we can build quietly in private." He looked at Elena. "We need to get this to the right people quickly. The corporate security company that was following you — they were watching. They may not know exactly what you have. But they know you're building something."

"Whitmore hasn't been approached again?"

"Not that he's mentioned. But I want you to tell him to be careful." Marcus walked toward the apartment's other room to make his call.

Elena turned back to the table and looked at Priya across Santos' documentation spread between them. The young woman was tired. She had been carrying this alone for eight months — the files, the knowledge, the specific loneliness of someone who knows something important and cannot safely tell anyone. She had kept herself together through discipline and purpose, and now the purpose had found its final stage.

"Santos gave you access to the backup files two weeks before he died," Elena said. "Did he say anything to you about what he thought would happen?"

Priya was quiet for a moment. "He said he thought the project principals would try to protect themselves once his report was submitted. He said he hoped the formal submission to the state and federal channels would be enough to get the investigation outside of anyone's reach. He seemed optimistic. He was a state employee — he believed in the process." She paused. "I think he underestimated how far the protection extended. Or how quickly they would act."

"He was right about the process," Elena said. "He just couldn't get there fast enough alone."

"Do you think we can?" Priya asked. Not with doubt in her voice — the question was genuine, a request for an honest assessment.

Elena thought about the documentation on the table. The corporate structure, the phantom subcontractors, the trust, the falsified surveys, the geological assessment, the legal mechanism, the February fifteenth phone call and the February seventeenth death. She thought about Marcus in the other room making contact with federal law enforcement. She thought about James Whitmore and Dale Ferrara and Rosa Medina, people who had seen the thing from the outside and had kept their observations despite the pressure to stay quiet.

"We can," Elena said. "But we need to move faster than they do."

Marcus returned from the other room. "Figueroa confirms she received Santos' call on the fifteenth. He gave her a preliminary briefing. She opened an internal inquiry on February eighteenth — the day after Santos' body was found. Three weeks later, the inquiry was suspended pending additional evidence. She was told by her section chief that the evidence threshold for a financial crimes investigation of a state-contracted development company was high and that Santos' preliminary information was insufficient." His expression was carefully neutral. "She didn't agree with the suspension. She has been waiting for a reason to reopen it."

"She's an ally," Elena said.

"She wants to see what we have. I told her we'd meet with her in two days. She'll come here." He looked at Priya. "That gives you tomorrow to complete the document package. Everything organized, everything source-attributed, everything in a form that can stand up to a federal evidentiary standard."

Priya nodded once, with the focused calm of someone returning to work they have been waiting to complete. "I'll have it ready."

Elena gathered her notes and the document copies she'd made and stood. Outside the apartment window, the October afternoon was moving toward evening, the sky over the eastern ridge going the color of old brass. The harbor was visible from the apartment's north window as a thin stripe of dark water between buildings — patient, cold, full of what it knew.

"One more thing," Elena said. "Santos' memo about the Pier 7 pipeline easement conflict. If that easement record is in the public files, I want to locate the original documentation before anyone else does. If they know Santos found it, they may try to remove it."

"County engineer's office would have the 1931 easement records," Marcus said.

"I'll go first thing tomorrow." Elena picked up her bag. "Get some sleep, Priya. Tomorrow we build the case that gets this to trial."

Priya looked at her across the table, across eight months of careful isolation and all the documentation David Santos had assembled before someone took his life. "He would have liked you," she said. "He was the kind of person who believed that careful, rigorous work eventually wins. That if you build the record well enough, justice catches up."

"It did," Elena said. "He just didn't live to see it."

After Marcus left to make his call, Elena sat with Priya at the kitchen table and looked at the structure Santos had built, spread across the table in printouts and annotated pages and the clean, readable diagrams of someone who had understood that his work might eventually need to be followed by people who hadn't shared his months of immersion in it.

"He was very careful about the legal mechanism," Priya said. "He kept a separate research file on the Submerged Lands Act and the 2018 precedent. He annotated the circuit court opinion in detail — highlighted the language about operational adjacency, wrote margin notes about how that language could be applied to harbor facility development. He was building toward connecting the legal analysis to the financial fraud to make clear that the fraud was not incidental but structural. The fraud existed in order to fund the legal mechanism."

"Which means the principals understood the mechanism from the beginning," Elena said. "This wasn't discovered opportunistically. When Pacific Harbor Corp was incorporated three years ago, Winters and Blackwell and the Crane descendant already knew what they were building toward. The harbor development was designed from inception to generate the submerged land claim."

"Yes. And that means the falsified environmental assessment, the phantom subcontractors, the replacement auditor — none of those were improvisations. They were planned. The fraud is the infrastructure for the bigger crime, not the crime itself."

Elena thought about the distinction Priya was drawing. In the founding conspiracy cases, the present-day criminals had been maintaining a historical fraud — protecting an inherited structure. What Pacific Harbor Corp represented was something constructed freshly, deliberately, from the beginning. Three founding-family descendants, presumably having inherited wealth that bore the marks of the original 1879 crimes, now designing a new fraud from scratch using the tools available to them in the present: shell corporations, Delaware registrations, Cayman trusts, favorable regulatory relationships, and the physical infrastructure of a state contract.

"Do you know how they found out about the rare earth deposit?" Elena asked. "The geological assessment was published twenty-two months ago. Pacific Harbor Corp was incorporated twenty-four months ago. The timeline suggests they may have had advance knowledge."

Priya was quiet for a moment. "Santos thought about that too. He has a note in the file suggesting that the Pacific Northwest Coastal Resources Assessment was shared in draft form with state economic development offices before its public publication. If the trust principals had relationships with state economic development officials — and given their founding-family histories and business connections, that's plausible — they could have seen the draft survey data before the report was public." She paused. "That's a thread I never finished. Whether there was a leak from the state economic development office, and whether it was paid for."

"One more thread for Figueroa," Elena said. "The federal case can include an inquiry into how Pacific Harbor Corp knew to move so quickly after the deposit was identified."

Marcus returned from the other room. "Figueroa is set for Tuesday at four. She wants to review Santos' files herself before the formal presentation, so she can anticipate the defense challenges."

"Tuesday gives us the weekend to complete the document package," Elena said. "What about the Ashmont family connection — the cousin search?"

"I ran the Ashmont family tree against the Pacific Harbor Corp registration documents. Nothing connects. Whatever James Ashmont's cousins are doing, they're not part of this particular enterprise." He paused. "Or they're doing it in a way that isn't visible yet."

"Note it as unresolved," Elena said. She looked at the document spread across the table one final time, impressing its structure into memory. The thing Santos had built was almost complete. What remained was authentication, presentation, and the moment when the record moved from assembled to official — from an investigation to a case.

She left the apartment and drove home through the darkening city, through the streets that ran past the harbor and the construction staging area and the blue and white Pacific Harbor Corp signage promising a gleaming future built on top of a buried lie.
