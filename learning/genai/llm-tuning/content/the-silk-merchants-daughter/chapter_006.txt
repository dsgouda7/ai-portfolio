Chapter 6: The Desert Crossing

The desert between Jiayuguan and Dunhuang was called the Gobi, though that name encompassed many different terrains: gravel plains, sand dunes, rocky badlands, and salt flats. The delegation left the fortress at dawn, following a route marked by cairns and the bones of animals that had not survived previous crossings.

Wei Lian had thought she understood what the desert would be like, but the reality exceeded her imagination. The heat during the day was crushing, the sun reflecting off rocks and sand with blinding intensity. At night, temperatures plummeted, and she woke shivering despite multiple blankets. Water, carefully rationed, became the most precious commodity. Every member of the delegation carried their own supply, supplemented by the water carts that Lord Sun had insisted on bringing despite their weight.

"Three days to the first oasis," Kara announced on the morning of their departure. "If we maintain good pace and the weather holds."

"And if the weather does not hold?" Master Chen asked nervously. The elderly official was not adapting well to the harsh conditions.

"Then we dig in and wait. The desert demands patience."

They traveled in the early morning and late afternoon, resting during the brutal midday heat beneath improvised shelters. Wei Lian learned to wrap her face against the wind-blown sand, to walk her horse when the footing was uncertain, to conserve her energy for the long stretches of travel.

On the second day, they encountered a merchant caravan coming from the west, a long train of camels laden with goods from the western regions. The caravan leader was a Khotanese merchant named Vijaya, a Buddhist who wore robes of saffron beneath his traveling cloak.

Lord Sun called for a halt, and the two parties mingled, exchanging news and information as was customary among Silk Road travelers. Vijaya's caravan had left Dunhuang ten days earlier, he reported, and the city was peaceful. However, they had encountered signs of bandit activity two days west of the city.

"Fifteen men, maybe twenty," Vijaya said, speaking in accented but fluent Chinese. "They watched us from the rocks but did not attack. Our caravan was too large and too well-guarded. But a smaller party might not be so fortunate."

"Did you recognize any tribal affiliation?" Captain Wei asked, his professional interest evident.

"Uighurs, I think. Or perhaps Tibetans. Hard to say. They keep their distance, assess their prey, strike only when advantage is theirs."

After the merchant caravan departed eastward, Lord Sun called a meeting. "We must assume we are being watched. Captain Wei, increase the guard rotation. From now on, we travel in tight formation with scouts ahead and to the flanks. No one strays from the main group."

Wei Lian felt the atmosphere of the delegation shift from careful caution to genuine alertness. The soldiers checked their weapons more frequently. The officials spoke in quieter tones. Even the horses seemed to sense the tension, their ears swiveling constantly to catch unfamiliar sounds.

That night, camped at a dry riverbed that offered some shelter from the wind, Wei Lian found herself unable to sleep. She lay beside Zhang Ming in their small tent, listening to the night sounds: the stamp of horses, the murmur of guards, the wind sighing across the empty land.

"Are you afraid?" Zhang Ming's voice was barely a whisper.

"Yes. And you?"

"Yes. But Captain Wei knows his business. And Lord Sun has dealt with bandits before. We should be safe enough."

"Should be is not the same as will be."

Zhang Ming found her hand in the darkness. "No. But it is all we have. In the capital, we think we control our fates through proper behavior and careful planning. Out here, we remember that fate has its own plans."

"Very philosophical for the middle of the night."

"The desert makes philosophers of us all, apparently."

Wei Lian squeezed his hand, drawing comfort from the contact. "If something happens, if we are attacked... Zhang Ming, I want you to know that these months have been the happiest of my life. Whatever comes, I do not regret this journey."

"Nor do I. Sleep now, if you can. Tomorrow will be a long day."

But sleep would not come. Wei Lian lay awake, thinking of her family in Chang'an, of Lady Zhang waiting for letters that might never arrive, of her father who had traveled this same route decades ago and returned to tell his stories. She thought of the jade pendant around her neck and the compass in her pack, talismans connecting her to the past and guiding her toward an uncertain future.

When dawn finally came, gray and cold, the delegation broke camp quickly and resumed their westward march. The scouts ranged ahead, reporting back on the terrain and watching for any signs of ambush. The day passed without incident, but the tension remained.

Late in the afternoon, they reached the first oasis, a small settlement clustered around a spring-fed pool of surprisingly clear water. The villagers were accustomed to caravans and had simple accommodations for travelers: a caravansary with rooms for rent, a stable for animals, and a small market selling supplies.

Lord Sun negotiated for rooms and storage for their goods, while the soldiers established a perimeter and set up guard rotations. Wei Lian, grateful for the chance to wash the desert dust from her face and hands, took advantage of the women's bathing area at the caravansary.

Lady Chen was already there, luxuriating in a basin of heated water. "Civilization," she sighed. "Or as close as we are likely to find in this desolate country."

Wei Lian smiled and began washing. "The desert has its own beauty."

"You sound like my husband. He keeps saying this journey is broadening our perspectives. I say it is merely making us dirty and uncomfortable."

"Perhaps both are true."

Lady Chen studied her with shrewd eyes. "You are handling this better than I expected. When I heard that Zhang Ming was bringing his bride on this expedition, I thought you would be a pampered girl who would complain constantly. But you ride as well as the men, you speak sensibly in the planning meetings, and I have yet to hear you utter a single complaint."

"Would complaining improve our situation?"

"No, but most women would complain anyway. I certainly have, though perhaps not in your hearing." Lady Chen's expression turned more serious. "You have earned respect among the delegation. Even the soldiers speak well of you, and soldiers are not easily impressed by officials' wives."

"I am grateful for your good opinion."

"Keep it in mind if we encounter trouble. The men will protect Lord Sun and the senior officials first. The wives are secondary considerations. You should have a plan for protecting yourself."

Wei Lian felt a chill despite the warm water. "What kind of plan?"

"A weapon you can actually use. A place to hide or run. The knowledge that if it comes to violence, you cannot rely entirely on others for your survival." Lady Chen pulled herself from the basin and began drying off. "I am not trying to frighten you, only to be realistic. We are women in a dangerous place. Prudence is our best defense."

That evening, Wei Lian sought out Captain Wei. The guard commander was inspecting the perimeter defenses, checking sight lines and posting his soldiers at strategic points around the caravansary.

"Captain, may I speak with you?"

He turned, surprise evident on his weathered face. "Of course, Madam Zhang."

"Lady Chen suggested I should learn to defend myself, in case of attack. Is that advice you would support?"

Captain Wei considered her seriously. "It is sensible advice. Can you use a weapon?"

"I have never tried."

"Then a sword would be of no use to you. In the heat of battle, an untrained person with a sword is more likely to hurt themselves than an enemy." He thought for a moment. "But a dagger is different. Close quarters, simple techniques. I could teach you enough to give you a chance."

And so, in the relative safety of the oasis, Wei Lian received her first lesson in violence. Captain Wei showed her how to hold a dagger, where to strike for maximum effect, how to use an attacker's momentum against them. The lesson was brief and practical, focused on survival rather than artistry.

"The key is to be unexpected," Captain Wei told her. "Most attackers will not expect resistance from a woman, especially not serious resistance. Use that surprise to your advantage. Strike hard, strike fast, and then run."

Wei Lian practiced the movements until her arm ached, committing them to muscle memory. It felt strange and transgressive to be learning violence, something so far removed from the educated young woman she had been in Chang'an. But it also felt necessary, even empowering.

Zhang Ming found her as she was finishing the lesson, her practice dagger still in hand. "I saw you training with Captain Wei."

"Lady Chen suggested I learn to protect myself."

"And what do you think?"

"I think she is right. I think I have been naive about the dangers we face." Wei Lian slipped the dagger into her belt, feeling its unfamiliar weight. "I do not wish to use this. But I am glad to know that I could, if I had to."

Zhang Ming nodded slowly. "The world is more complex than the books suggest. More dangerous, but also more surprising. Every day on this journey teaches me something new."

They stood together in the gathering dusk, watching the soldiers complete their preparations for the night. Above them, the first stars were appearing in the darkening sky, bright and clear in the desert air.

The next morning, they departed the oasis before dawn, following Kara's guidance deeper into the desert. The landscape grew harsher, more barren. Even the hardy desert plants became scarce, leaving only bare rock and sand dunes that shifted and changed with the wind.

On the third day after leaving the oasis, the attack came.

It happened in the late afternoon, as the delegation was threading through a narrow passage between towering rock formations. The scouts had just reported the way ahead was clear when arrows began to fall from the rocks above.

Captain Wei's response was immediate. "Ambush! Close formation! Shields up!"

The soldiers snapped into defensive positions, shields raised to deflect the arrows. Lord Sun was pulled from his horse and hustled into the protective circle of guards. Chaos erupted as people shouted, horses screamed, and more arrows fell like deadly rain.

Wei Lian found herself yanked from her horse by Zhang Ming, both of them crouching behind the supply cart as arrows thudded into the wood above their heads. Her heart hammered with terror, but her hands were steady as she drew the dagger Captain Wei had given her.

"Stay low," Zhang Ming ordered, his scholar's calm demeanor replaced by intense focus.

The bandits came down from the rocks, perhaps thirty men armed with swords and spears, their faces wrapped against recognition. They were counting on overwhelming the delegation through surprise and numbers. But they had underestimated both Captain Wei's preparations and his soldiers' training.

The guards met the attack with disciplined violence, their formation holding as they fought off the bandits. The clash of metal on metal rang through the narrow defile. Men shouted in Chinese and other languages. Blood began to stain the sandy ground.

Wei Lian watched from behind the cart, her dagger gripped tightly, as the battle raged. A bandit broke through the defensive line, running toward the officials huddled near the supply carts. He raised his sword, murder in his eyes.

Without thinking, Wei Lian moved. She remembered Captain Wei's instruction: use surprise, strike fast, run. She lunged forward, driving her dagger toward the man's side, beneath his ribs where the captain had told her the vital organs were vulnerable.

The blade sank into flesh. The bandit screamed and turned toward her, shock evident even through his face wrapping. Wei Lian pulled the dagger free and scrambled backward, her hands now shaking violently. The bandit took one step toward her before collapsing, blood spreading across his robes.

Zhang Ming grabbed her arm and pulled her back behind the cart. "Are you hurt?"

"No. I... I killed him."

"You saved lives. That man was going for Master Chen."

Wei Lian stared at her hands, at the blood on her dagger, unable to fully process what had just happened. She had killed a man. The knowledge sat in her stomach like a stone.

The battle ended as suddenly as it had begun. The bandits, realizing they were not facing an easy target, began to retreat, melting back into the rocks from which they had come. Captain Wei did not pursue, keeping his forces in defensive formation until he was certain the threat had passed.

The casualties were tallied: two soldiers killed, five wounded, three horses dead. Among the officials and their families, there were minor injuries from fallen arrows but no fatalities. And one bandit dead by Wei Lian's hand.

Lord Sun surveyed the damage with grim satisfaction. "Captain Wei, your preparation saved us. Well done." He turned to the rest of the delegation. "We move immediately, before they regroup. This position is indefensible if they return with reinforcements."

The delegation packed rapidly, abandoning anything that might slow them down. The wounded were placed in carts, the dead were wrapped for later burial. Within an hour, they were moving again, leaving the blood-stained defile behind.

Wei Lian rode in numb silence, barely aware of the landscape passing by. The adrenaline of the fight had drained away, leaving only exhaustion and a profound sense of dislocation. She had killed a man. That fact repeated itself in her mind like a mantra, impossible to ignore or dismiss.

That night, when they finally made camp at another, more defensible location, Lady Chen sought her out. The older woman's face was bruised from where she had fallen during the attack, but her eyes were sympathetic.

"I heard what you did. Saved Master Chen's life, and possibly others."

"I killed someone."

"Yes. And you will have to live with that. It is not easy, taking a life. Even when it is justified, even when there was no choice." Lady Chen sat beside her. "I killed a man once, many years ago. A thief who broke into our home in Luoyang. I struck him with a bronze incense burner, and he never woke up. For months afterward, I had nightmares. I kept thinking about his family, whether he had children, what had driven him to theft."

"How did you bear it?"

"By accepting that the world is imperfect and that sometimes we must do difficult things to protect what we love. You did not seek violence. It came to you, and you responded with courage. That is something to be respected, not mourned."

Wei Lian looked at the older woman with new understanding. "Thank you."

"Get some rest. Tomorrow will be hard in different ways."

Wei Lian returned to the tent she shared with Zhang Ming, where he was waiting with a basin of water and clean cloths. Without speaking, he helped her wash the blood from her hands and arms, his touch gentle and careful.

"I am sorry you had to do that," he said quietly.

"So am I. But I would do it again, if I had to. To protect you, or Master Chen, or anyone in this delegation."

Zhang Ming pulled her close, and she rested her head against his shoulder, feeling the solidity of him, the comfort of his presence. "You are braver than I knew."

"I am more terrified than you know."

"Those are not mutually exclusive."

They held each other in the darkness of the tent, listening to the sounds of the camp settling for the night. Guards patrolled the perimeter. Horses shifted in their tethers. Wounded men groaned softly.

Wei Lian thought of the man she had killed, the life she had ended. She would carry that with her, she knew, for the rest of her days. But she would also carry the knowledge that when violence had come, she had not frozen or fled. She had acted, and in acting, she had saved lives.

The desert had taught her many things: endurance, patience, the value of water and shade. Now it had taught her something else, darker and more difficult. The world was not kind. It did not care about propriety or gentleness or the sheltered dreams of merchant's daughters. It demanded strength, decisiveness, the willingness to do hard things when hard things were necessary.

Wei Lian closed her eyes and tried to sleep, knowing that dawn would bring new challenges, new tests. The Silk Road stretched ahead, and they had only just begun their journey into the western regions. More dangers awaited, more trials to face.

But she had survived today. She had been tested and had not broken. And that knowledge, hard-won and blood-stained as it was, would have to be enough to carry her forward into whatever came next.
