Chapter 2: The Scholar's Visit

Three days after the matchmaker's visit, word came that the Zhang family wished to meet Wei Lian. This was expected, the next step in the elaborate dance of courtship that characterized marriages among respectable families. Zhang Ming himself would visit the Wei compound, accompanied by his mother and a male relative, to take tea and assess the potential bride.

Lady Wei threw herself into preparations with the intensity of a general planning a military campaign. Every surface in the reception hall was polished until it gleamed. Fresh flowers were arranged and rearranged. The kitchen staff was instructed to prepare delicacies that would demonstrate the household's sophistication without appearing ostentatious. Wei Lian's robes were selected with agonizing care, settling finally on a silk the color of morning mist, embroidered with bamboo leaves in silver thread.

"Bamboo suggests scholarly cultivation," Lady Wei explained as Plum Blossom arranged Wei Lian's hair. "It is humble yet refined. Exactly the right impression for a scholar's wife."

Wei Lian submitted to these preparations with growing nervousness. This would be her first real glimpse of the man who might become her husband. Until now, he had been merely an abstract concept, a name attached to credentials and family connections. Soon he would become a living, breathing person, with his own thoughts and expectations.

"What if he does not like me?" she asked quietly.

Her mother paused in her inspection of the reception hall. "You are beautiful, educated, and from a good family. What is there not to like?"

"Perhaps he wants a different kind of wife. More traditional. Less opinionated."

Lady Wei smiled, a rare expression of understanding passing across her usually stern features. "If Zhang Ming is as intelligent as his examination results suggest, he will appreciate a wife who can hold a conversation. But remember, daughter, there is a difference between being educated and being argumentative. A wise woman knows when to speak and when to remain silent."

The Zhang party arrived precisely at the appointed hour, a testament to their regard for punctuality and propriety. Wei Chen greeted them at the compound gates, as custom required, and escorted them to the reception hall. Wei Lian, following protocol, waited in the women's quarters with her mother until summoned.

When the call finally came, Wei Lian's heart was beating so rapidly she feared others might hear it. She followed her mother through the covered corridors to the reception hall, her silk slippers whispering against the wooden floors.

Zhang Ming rose as they entered, and Wei Lian's first impression was of height and a certain scholarly awkwardness. He was tall and thin, with the pale complexion of someone who spent more time with books than in the sun. His face was pleasant rather than handsome, with intelligent eyes and a mouth that suggested he smiled more readily than frowned. His robes were the dark blue of a scholar-official, well-made but not luxurious, with minimal embroidery.

Beside him stood his mother, Lady Zhang, a formidable woman in her fifties with iron-gray hair and a posture that suggested military discipline. The male relative was an uncle, Master Zhang Hao, a minor official in the Ministry of Revenue with the perpetually distracted air of someone mentally calculating tax figures.

The initial greetings were formal and choreographed. Wei Lian bowed at the appropriate depth, kept her eyes downcast at the appropriate moments, and responded to questions with the appropriate brevity. Tea was served in the finest porcelain cups, accompanied by crystallized fruit and small honey cakes.

"Your daughter plays the guqin, I understand," Lady Zhang said, her sharp gaze fixed on Wei Lian.

"Modestly," Wei Lian replied, as modesty required, though she knew her skill was well above average.

"Would you honor us with a performance?"

This too was expected. Wei Lian moved to where the instrument had been placed and settled herself in the proper position. She chose a piece from the classical repertoire, neither too simple nor too complex, and played it with careful precision. Her fingers moved across the strings with practiced grace, and the notes filled the hall with crystalline clarity.

As she played, she was acutely aware of being observed, evaluated. Every note was a test, every phrase a judgment. She kept her expression serene, her posture perfect, even as her mind raced with anxiety.

When she finished, there was a moment of silence before Lady Zhang nodded approvingly. "Very accomplished. My son also plays, though his skill is perhaps less refined."

"Mother exaggerates," Zhang Ming said, speaking for the first time. His voice was pleasant, cultured, with the slight southern accent of someone from the Jiangnan region. "I am an indifferent musician at best."

"My son is too modest," Lady Zhang countered. "He is accomplished in many arts, though his true talent lies in calligraphy and poetry."

The conversation continued in this vein, a careful exchange of compliments and self-deprecations designed to demonstrate both families' refinement and humility. Wei Chen spoke of his silk business with pride but not arrogance, emphasizing the family's long tradition of honest dealing. Lady Wei detailed Wei Lian's education and household skills without appearing boastful. Lady Zhang described her son's scholarly achievements while insisting they were merely the result of diligent study rather than exceptional talent.

Through it all, Wei Lian remained largely silent, as was proper. But she listened carefully, trying to form an impression of Zhang Ming from the fragments of conversation. He seemed intelligent, as advertised, with a dry wit that occasionally surfaced in his comments. He was respectful to his mother but not servile, suggesting a man comfortable with his own judgment. He asked thoughtful questions about the silk trade, displaying genuine curiosity rather than mere politeness.

Finally, as the afternoon drew on, Lady Zhang rose to depart. "This has been a most pleasant visit. Your family does you credit, Master Wei."

"The honor is ours," Wei Chen replied.

As the guests prepared to leave, Zhang Ming turned to Wei Lian and bowed slightly. "I hope we may meet again, Miss Wei."

Wei Lian returned the bow, her heart unexpectedly light. There had been something in his tone, a warmth that transcended the formality of the occasion. For the first time, she allowed herself to consider that this marriage might not be entirely unwelcome.

After the Zhang family departed, Lady Wei embraced her daughter. "They were very pleased. I could tell. Lady Zhang is not an easy woman to impress, but your performance on the guqin won her over."

That evening, the family gathered to discuss the visit. Wei Chen was optimistic, Lady Wei was triumphant, Wei Bo was bored by the entire subject, and Wei Mei was full of romantic notions about her sister's impending wedding.

Wei Lian excused herself early and returned to her room. She sat at her writing desk, brush in hand, but found herself unable to write in her journal. Instead, she replayed the afternoon in her mind, examining each moment, each word, searching for clues about the man who might become her husband.

Zhang Ming had seemed kind. That was the word that kept returning to her. Not passionate or exciting, but kind. His eyes had held no cruelty, no arrogance, only a mild curiosity and, perhaps, a hint of nervousness that matched her own.

Was that enough? Could kindness and intelligence form the foundation of a life together?

She had no answers, only questions. And the knowledge that the decision, ultimately, would not be hers to make.

The next morning brought unexpected news. A messenger arrived from the Zhang household with a letter for Wei Lian, delivered through the proper channels of her father's hands. Such direct communication was unusual but not improper, particularly since the two families were now in formal negotiations.

Wei Chen called his daughter to his study and handed her the sealed letter. "Zhang Ming has written to you. This is... unconventional but not inappropriate. You may read it here."

Wei Lian's hands trembled slightly as she broke the seal and unfolded the rice paper. The calligraphy was excellent, each character precisely formed with elegant brushstrokes.

"Honored Miss Wei," the letter began. "I hope you will forgive the presumption of this communication. Yesterday's meeting, though constrained by the necessary formalities, left me with many questions I did not have the opportunity to ask. If our families proceed with the marriage negotiations, as seems likely, we will spend our lives together. It seems only prudent that we should know something of each other's thoughts and preferences.

Therefore, I propose a correspondence, with your father's permission. I would ask you questions, and you would answer honestly, and I would endeavor to do the same. Nothing improper, merely an exchange of ideas between two people considering a shared future.

If this proposal offends, please disregard it, and I will not mention it again. But if you are willing, I would welcome the chance to know your mind as well as your family's reputation."

The letter was signed simply "Zhang Ming."

Wei Lian looked up at her father, who was watching her with an unreadable expression. "Father, what should I do?"

Wei Chen was silent for a moment, considering. "This is highly unusual. Your mother would probably disapprove. But I see the logic in it. If you are to marry this man, it would be better to know something of his character beforehand. I will allow it, on two conditions. First, I will review each letter before it is sent, to ensure nothing inappropriate is written. Second, you will keep your correspondence from your mother. She would insist on composing your replies herself, and that would defeat the purpose."

Wei Lian felt a surge of affection for her unconventional father. "Thank you, Father."

"Do not thank me yet. This correspondence may reveal things you do not wish to know. Zhang Ming may turn out to be tedious or pedantic. But at least you will know before the marriage, not after."

Wei Lian spent the rest of the morning composing her reply. It took numerous drafts before she achieved the right tone: respectful but not servile, intelligent but not overly learned, open but not improper.

"Honored Master Zhang," she wrote at last. "Your letter was unexpected but not unwelcome. I agree that practical understanding is more valuable than romantic mystification, particularly in matters of lifelong commitment. I accept your proposal of correspondence, with the understanding that our letters remain appropriate for two people in our situation.

Perhaps we might begin with simple questions. What books do you favor? What are your views on education? What do you hope for in a marriage partnership?

I will answer the same questions for myself. I favor poetry above all other literature, particularly Wang Wei and Li Bai. I believe education is valuable for both men and women, as it cultivates wisdom and virtue. In a marriage, I hope for mutual respect, honest communication, and a household where learning is valued."

She paused, wondering if she had revealed too much, but finally signed it "Wei Lian" and brought it to her father for review.

Wei Chen read it carefully, made one small suggestion for a more formal phrasing in the opening, and then nodded his approval. "This is well written. Neither too forward nor too submissive. Send it."

Thus began a correspondence that would last for the next two months, as the formal marriage negotiations proceeded through their elaborate stages. Zhang Ming wrote of his admiration for Zhuangzi, his belief that education should cultivate moral character rather than mere technical skill, and his hope for a marriage of intellectual companionship as well as domestic harmony.

Wei Lian found herself looking forward to his letters with surprising eagerness. Zhang Ming's writing revealed a man of genuine thoughtfulness, with a subtle humor and a refreshing lack of pretension. He asked her opinions on matters great and small, from philosophical questions about the nature of virtue to practical concerns about household management. And, remarkably, he seemed to genuinely value her responses.

"Do you believe," he wrote in one letter, "that women are inherently less capable of scholarly achievement than men, or is the difference merely one of education and opportunity?"

Wei Lian considered carefully before responding. This was dangerous ground, where she might easily offend conventional sensibilities. But Zhang Ming had asked for honesty, and she decided to give it.

"I believe," she wrote, "that individuals vary in their capabilities regardless of sex. I have known stupid men and brilliant women, and the reverse as well. That women have fewer opportunities for formal education is undeniable. Whether this represents an inherent difference or merely social custom is a question each must answer according to their own observations."

His reply came swiftly. "Your answer is diplomatic and wise. I will be more direct. I believe talent is distributed without regard to sex, and that our empire wastes potential by restricting women's education. My mother is the most intelligent person I know, man or woman. She could have been a magistrate or a minister had she been born male. Instead, her talents are confined to household management. It seems a profound waste."

Wei Lian read this letter three times, hardly believing what she was seeing. Such views were not unheard of, but they were rare, especially among men of Zhang Ming's class and education. She felt something shift in her chest, a loosening of the tightness that had gripped her since the matchmaker's first visit.

Perhaps this marriage would not be a cage after all. Perhaps it might even be something approaching a partnership.

The formal negotiations proceeded smoothly. Dowries were discussed, wedding dates were proposed, horoscopes were consulted. The Zhang family proved generous in their terms, and the Wei family responded with equal generosity. Both families stood to gain from the alliance: the Zhangs would receive a substantial dowry and connections to the prosperous silk trade, while the Weis would gain the prestige of marrying their daughter to a promising scholar-official.

On the evening when the final terms were settled, Wei Chen summoned his daughter to his study once more. "The wedding will be in three months," he told her. "The astrologers agree that it is an auspicious date. Are you content with this match?"

Wei Lian thought of the letters hidden in her room, the careful words exchanged between her and Zhang Ming, the glimpse of a life that might hold both security and intellectual companionship. "Yes, Father. I am content."

Wei Chen studied her face, as if searching for hidden doubts. "You understand that marriage is not always easy, even with a kind husband. There will be duties, expectations, the demands of his family as well as your own. And if he receives a government posting, you may be far from Chang'an, far from us."

"I understand."

"But I believe you will manage well. You have intelligence and strength, though you hide both behind proper modesty. Zhang Ming is fortunate, though he may not realize it yet."

Wei Lian felt tears prick her eyes at her father's unexpected praise. "Thank you, Father."

"One more thing." Wei Chen reached into his desk and withdrew a small wooden box. "This was your grandmother's. She gave it to me before she died and told me to give it to you when you married."

Wei Lian opened the box to find a jade pendant carved in the shape of a phoenix, the legendary bird that represented the empress and, by extension, womanhood itself. The jade was a deep, translucent green, cool to the touch.

"Your grandmother was an unusual woman," Wei Chen said. "She ran this business for fifteen years after my father died, navigating wars and famines and political upheavals. She wanted you to remember that women, too, can be strong. Keep this as a reminder."

Wei Lian closed her fingers around the pendant, feeling the weight of it, the connection to a grandmother she barely remembered but whose legacy had shaped her life. "I will."

As she left her father's study, the jade pendant warm in her hand, Wei Lian felt something she had not expected to feel about her marriage: hope. Not the naive hope of romantic stories, but something more solid and more real. The hope that she had found a partner who might respect her mind as well as value her household skills, who might allow her some measure of the intellectual freedom she craved.

The next three months would be a whirlwind of preparations, fittings, rituals, and ceremonies. But for now, in the quiet of her father's study, she allowed herself a moment of cautious optimism.

Perhaps her life would not be entirely decided by others after all. Perhaps, within the constraints of her role as a wife, she might find unexpected freedoms. Perhaps the door she had feared was closing might, instead, be opening onto something new.

Only time would reveal which path she truly walked. But tonight, with her grandmother's jade pendant in her hand and Zhang Ming's latest letter waiting to be read, Wei Lian felt ready to take the first steps into her unknown future.
