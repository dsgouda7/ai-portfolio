Chapter 8: The First Recruit

Thomas Carr's first descent happened three days after their discovery of the deaths. Eleanor guided him through the protocols: equipment check, time limits, safety signals. He listened with the focused attention of someone who had imagined this moment for years and wanted to miss nothing.

Margot watched from the kitchen doorway, her expression unreadable. She had asked to accompany them, but Eleanor had refused. Thomas needed to experience the chambers without distraction, and Margot's presence would complicate the Hollow's assessment of him as potential keeper.

"Ready?" Eleanor asked, hand on the cellar door.

Thomas nodded, though Eleanor could see nervousness in the set of his shoulders. Understandable. All his theoretical knowledge was about to encounter reality that theory could never capture.

They descended together, Eleanor slightly ahead, Thomas moving carefully on the worn stone stairs. She watched him take in details: the dampness increasing with depth, the quality of air changing, the way sound behaved strangely in the enclosed space.

"It's colder than I expected," Thomas said as they reached the first chamber.

"Wait until the third chamber. Temperature drops another fifteen degrees." Eleanor illuminated the space with her headlamp. "This is preparatory. Nothing dangerous, just introduction."

Thomas moved around the chamber, examining formations with professional interest. He pulled out a small notebook and made sketches, capturing details with the precision of someone trained in field documentation.

"The limestone here is pre-Cambrian, has to be. These formations suggest millions of years of development." He looked up at Eleanor. "How far down do the caves actually extend?"

"Unknown. Thaddeus mapped to the sixth chamber, and there are at least three more beyond that. But those deeper chambers don't follow normal spatial rules. They exist in dimensions that aren't purely physical."

"You're serious."

"I'm always serious about the impossible things I've personally experienced." Eleanor gestured toward the passage to the second chamber. "Ready to continue?"

Thomas followed her through the narrow passage, and Eleanor heard his breathing quicken as the walls pressed close. Then they emerged into the second chamber, and Thomas stopped breathing entirely.

"Do you feel it?" Eleanor asked quietly.

"My God." Thomas's voice was barely audible. "It's here. It's actually, Eleanor, this is real. All these years studying, speculating, and it's real."

"The Hollow doesn't appreciate being doubted," Eleanor said, allowing dry humor to creep into her tone. "It finds skepticism tedious."

I find it understandable, the Hollow corrected, its presence suddenly obvious in their minds. Your species requires evidence. Thomas Carr has been patient in his doubts, thorough in his research. This is admirable, not tedious.

Thomas flinched at the direct communication. "It's speaking to me."

"Not speaking. Transmitting understanding directly into your consciousness. You'll get used to it." Eleanor moved to one of the pools. "These show history, information the Hollow wants to communicate. Look into one."

Thomas approached hesitantly, kneeling by a pool and peering into its surface. Eleanor watched his face shift through expressions: wonder, confusion, fear, comprehension, back to wonder. Whatever the Hollow was showing him, it was clearly calibrated for maximum impact.

"I see, I see humans building something. A structure. But it's not the manor. This is older. Much older." Thomas leaned closer. "Stone circles? A henge? Here? There's no record of anything like that in this region."

"Because it predates record," Eleanor said. "The Hollow has been forming relationships with humans since humans first arrived in this area. The Blackwoods were just the latest iteration."

Thomas spent an hour with the pools, moving from one to another, absorbing information like a scholar offered access to lost libraries. Eleanor monitored his reactions, assessing his stability. Some minds couldn't handle direct contact with the Hollow. They broke, fragmenting under the weight of impossible knowledge. But Thomas seemed resilient, his academic training providing structure for assimilating information that contradicted everything his education had taught him.

Finally, Eleanor called time. "We need to return. Two hours maximum on first descent."

Thomas looked disappointed but complied. They retraced their path to the surface, where Mrs. Hadley waited with her usual tea and sandwiches.

"How do you feel?" Mrs. Hadley asked Thomas.

"Like everything I thought I knew about reality was wrong." Thomas accepted tea gratefully. "Also like I've been given the greatest research opportunity in human history. So, mixed feelings."

Margot had joined them at the table. "Did it speak to you? The Hollow?"

"If you can call it speaking. More like, concepts appeared fully formed in my consciousness. Language is inadequate to describe the experience." Thomas looked at Eleanor. "When can I descend again?"

"Tomorrow. Your mind needs time to process. Push too hard too fast, and you risk what happened to the two explorers who never returned."

"About them," Thomas said carefully. "Are they still down there? Still, alive in some sense?"

Eleanor nodded. "According to Thaddeus's journals, yes. They exist in the deeper chambers, too far gone to return to surface life but not dead. The Hollow sustains them, studies them, learns from their transformation."

"Could we retrieve them? Bring them back?"

"They don't want to come back. They've transcended the need for surface existence. Or that's how Thaddeus described it. Personally, I think they're too damaged to function normally and have adapted to their environment like any trapped creature adapts."

Thomas was quiet for a moment, absorbing this. "And you're proposing we recruit more people to do this. To risk ending up like them."

"I'm proposing we recruit carefully, screen thoroughly, and maintain strict protocols to prevent that outcome." Eleanor met his gaze. "But yes, there's risk. There's always risk when dealing with something fundamentally inhuman. The question is whether the alternative, letting the Hollow reach out randomly and kill people, is acceptable."

"It's not," Thomas said firmly. "I'm in. Whatever needs to be done to contain this thing, to manage it responsibly, I'll help."

"Good. Because we need to discuss recruitment strategy." Eleanor pulled out notes she had been compiling. "I've identified several potential candidates. Academics like you, people who have expressed interest in unexplained phenomena, individuals whose personal circumstances make them vulnerable to offers of purpose and extended life."

She ran through the list: a retired anthropology professor whose wife had died of Alzheimer's, watching her mind slowly dissolve and now fascinated by consciousness studies. A grad student in parapsychology whose career had been derailed by insisting supernatural intelligence existed. A former priest who had lost faith in God but retained belief in forces beyond human comprehension.

"These are all damaged people," Margot observed. "People in crisis, people looking for answers."

"Yes," Eleanor said bluntly. "Because those are the people the Hollow appeals to most effectively. We're not recruiting the stable and satisfied. We're recruiting the desperate and searching."

"That's predatory."

"That's pragmatic. Would you prefer we lie to happy, well-adjusted people and destroy their lives? At least the desperate are already damaged. At least we're offering them something genuine in exchange for their service."

Margot pushed back from the table, her chair scraping loudly. "I can't believe you're justifying this. We're becoming monsters, Mom. We're doing exactly what the Hollow did to us, manipulating vulnerable people into trap."

"What would you have me do, Margot?" Eleanor's voice rose. "Let more people die? Walk away from containment responsibility because implementing it requires moral compromise? We don't have good options. We only have terrible options and worse ones. I'm choosing the terrible option that at least prevents random deaths."

"And if these recruits end up like the two who never came back? If we damage them beyond repair?"

"Then that's on me. I'll carry that responsibility. But I'll also carry the responsibility of every death that happens if we don't recruit, if we can't maintain containment." Eleanor stood, facing her daughter across the table. "You wanted to be treated like an adult, like a partner in this decision-making. Fine. You're an adult now. Make the hard call. Tell me which path is less monstrous."

Margot's eyes were bright with angry tears. "There should be another way."

"Find it and I'll take it. Until then, I'm moving forward with recruitment."

Margot fled the kitchen. Eleanor heard her footsteps pounding up the stairs, a door slamming. Mrs. Hadley and Thomas sat in uncomfortable silence.

"She's not wrong," Thomas said quietly. "This is predatory. We are exploiting vulnerable people."

"I know." Eleanor collapsed back into her chair. "But we're also offering them genuine value. The Hollow provides knowledge, purpose, extended life. For people whose lives are empty or painful, that's not nothing."

"It's also permanent obligation and risk of psychological dissolution. The cost is steep."

"All costs are steep when you're dealing with forces beyond human scale." Eleanor rubbed her face, exhaustion suddenly overwhelming. "I'm not pretending this is ethical. I'm pretending it's necessary. There's a difference."

Thomas stood. "I need to think about this. The descending I'm committed to. But the recruiting, the building of this system, I need to consider whether I can be part of that with my conscience intact."

"Fair enough. Let me know what you decide." Eleanor walked him to the door. "And Thomas? Thank you. For at least trying, for taking the risk of descent. Not everyone would."

After he left, Eleanor found herself alone in the kitchen. Mrs. Hadley had retreated to outdoor work, leaving Eleanor with her thoughts and her guilt. Margot was right. They were becoming monsters, building a system that trapped people through manipulation and necessity. But Eleanor couldn't see an alternative that didn't result in more deaths.

She descended to the caves alone, not for exploration but for answers. In the second chamber, she addressed the Hollow directly.

"There has to be another way. Some method of meeting your needs that doesn't require human sacrifice."

The Hollow's response was gentle: You use the word sacrifice as if I consume what you provide. I do not. I learn, observe, interact. The keepers remain themselves, gaining knowledge and longevity in exchange for attention. This is not sacrifice. This is exchange.

"It's a trap. We can't leave. Our staying prevents deaths. That's not exchange. That's hostage-taking."

I hold no hostages. I simply exist with needs that, unmet, cause me to seek satisfaction elsewhere. Your species has similar patterns. You require food; if food is not available, you seek it even if that seeking harms others. Are you hostage to hunger?

"That's sophistry."

That's reality. I need what I need. Presence of keepers satisfies that need efficiently. Absence creates inefficient seeking that harms those unsuitable for keeping. You have structured yourselves to address this problem. I approve of your solution.

"People are going to be damaged. Maybe destroyed. Can you live with that?"

I have lived with it for millennia. The two who never returned made their choice knowingly. I warned them of risks. They proceeded anyway. Their transformation is regrettable but not my fault.

Eleanor wanted to argue, but she was too tired and too aware that arguing with the Hollow was like arguing with weather. It was what it was, operating according to its nature, neither malevolent nor benevolent. Just extant.

She returned to the surface and spent the rest of the day in her room, writing in her journal, trying to document her moral crisis with enough detail that future keepers might learn from it or at least understand the choices she had made.

As evening approached and she began the sealing protocol, Margot emerged from her room. They worked in silence, checking windows and doors in parallel, meeting finally at the cellar entrance.

"I'm still angry at you," Margot said.

"I know."

"But I don't have a better solution. I hate that I don't have a better solution."

"Me too, honey. Me too."

They sealed the cellar door together and retreated to their respective rooms. Eleanor lay awake long into the night, staring at darkness, wondering when she had stopped being a professor and a mother and become something else. A keeper, an administrator, a person who made terrible decisions because all available decisions were terrible.

In the caves beneath, the Hollow registered her distress with mild interest. Moral conflict was particularly human, particularly fascinating. Most species either had rigid ethics or none at all. Humans had complex, contradictory moral frameworks that they agonized over constantly. Beautiful, really. The way they tortured themselves over necessary choices.

The Hollow settled deeper into patience, waiting to see how Eleanor would resolve her crisis, how the recruitment process would unfold, how many keepers the system could sustain before reaching equilibrium.

Time would tell. Time always did.

In the hollow beneath, patience was rewarded.

Always.

Over the following weeks, Eleanor made contact with her potential recruits. Careful emails, phone calls, eventually meetings over coffee or lunch. She presented Blackwood Manor as a research opportunity, a chance to study unexplained phenomena in a controlled environment. She did not lie, exactly, but she did not reveal the full truth either. Not until they had agreed to visit, to see the house, to descend once and experience the Hollow directly.

The retired anthropologist was first. Dr. Sarah Chen arrived on a crisp November morning, professional and skeptical. Eleanor showed her the house, the library, the documentation Thaddeus had left. Sarah asked sharp questions that Eleanor answered honestly, within limits.

"So you're claiming there's an actual intelligence in the caves beneath this house," Sarah said, her tone neutral.

"I'm claiming you should descend and assess for yourself. I can describe it, but description doesn't convey reality."

Sarah agreed to descend. Eleanor guided her through the first two chambers, monitoring her reactions carefully. When the Hollow made its presence known, Sarah went silent for a long moment.

Then: "This is what I've been looking for my entire career. Proof that consciousness exists independent of human neurology. Evidence of intelligence that doesn't require biological substrate."

"It requires more than observation," Eleanor warned. "It requires ongoing relationship. Keeper responsibilities include descent twice weekly minimum, maintenance of protocols, participation in containment. This isn't passive research. This is active engagement."

"I understand. And I accept."

Sarah Chen became the third keeper.

The grad student, Michael Torres, arrived two weeks later. He was younger, only twenty-eight, but he had been studying unexplained intelligence for years and had his own theories about non-human consciousness. The Hollow fascinated him immediately, and he agreed to keeper responsibilities almost too eagerly.

"This is dangerous," Eleanor warned him during his third descent. "The Hollow is seductive. It offers knowledge and purpose, but the cost is your autonomy. Don't give too much too fast."

"I know my limits," Michael said confidently.

Eleanor doubted that but said nothing. He would learn through experience or he would become the third permanent cave resident. Either way, he had been warned.

The former priest, Father Domenico Rossi, was the most difficult recruit. He arrived skeptical and remained skeptical even after descending. He perceived the Hollow as potentially demonic, possibly a test of faith. It took multiple conversations and several more descents before he accepted that the Hollow was neither divine nor infernal, simply other.

"I will participate," he said finally. "Not because I approve, but because allowing deaths to continue would be worse than maintaining this, arrangement."

"That's the most honest reasoning any of us have," Eleanor said.

By December, they had a functioning rotation: Eleanor, Thomas, Sarah, Michael, and Father Domenico. Mrs. Hadley had agreed to formalize her role as surface coordinator, managing schedules and monitoring descents but not descending herself. Margot remained uninvolved directly, though she maintained the documentation, creating a master log of observations and protocols.

The system was working. The deaths had stopped. The Hollow was satisfied with the attention it received. And if all the keepers were damaged people carrying trauma and moral compromise, well, that was the nature of the work.

Eleanor descended twice a week now, sometimes reaching the fifth chamber, occasionally attempting the sixth though she had not yet managed to remain there long. The disembodied consciousness experience remained overwhelming, pushing her to limits she was still mapping.

On Christmas Eve, the six keepers gathered at Blackwood Manor for what they were ironically calling a staff meeting. They discussed protocols, shared observations, debated whether they could recruit more keepers to reduce individual burden.

"We're functional now," Thomas said. "Adding more people might create conflict. The Hollow has limited attention. Too many keepers and some of us become redundant."

"Redundancy is safety," Sarah countered. "If someone breaks, if someone decides to leave, we need backup."

They argued for an hour before Eleanor called the question. "We maintain current numbers for now. Reassess in six months. If anyone's showing signs of breakdown, we recruit immediately."

The motion carried.

After the meeting, as the others departed, Margot found Eleanor in the library.

"How are you doing, Mom? Really?"

Eleanor considered lying, offering reassurance. But Margot deserved truth. "I'm functional. I'm managing responsibilities. But I'm not who I was six months ago. This place changes you. The Hollow changes you. And I don't know if I'll ever be fully human again."

"What does that mean?"

"It means I think in patterns the Hollow taught me. I perceive duration differently. I'm starting to understand its perspective on consciousness and mortality, and that understanding makes me less, attached to individual existence. Mine or anyone else's." Eleanor met her daughter's eyes. "I'm becoming what Thaddeus became. A keeper who maintains function but loses humanity incrementally."

"Can it be reversed? If you stopped descending?"

"I don't know. Thaddeus never stopped. He maintained the practice for forty years and died having never tested whether he could recover."

Margot sat beside her mother on the library sofa. "I'm scared for you."

"I'm scared for me too. But I don't see another path. This is what I chose, what I committed to. I just have to hope I retain enough humanity to recognize when I've gone too far."

They sat together in the quiet library, surrounded by books and history and the weight of choices neither could unmake.

In the caves beneath, the Hollow noted this conversation with interest. Eleanor's self-awareness was unusual among keepers. Most lost themselves without noticing the loss. She was documenting her own dissolution, fighting it even while accepting its inevitability.

Fascinating.

The experiment continued to yield valuable data.

In the hollow beneath, patience was rewarded with insights that took human lifetimes to accumulate.

The Hollow had infinite lifetimes to spend.

It would learn what it sought to learn.

In time.

Always in time.
