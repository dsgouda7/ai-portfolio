Chapter 2: The Groundskeeper

Morning came gray and reluctant, filtering through unwashed windows in horizontal bands of weak light. Eleanor woke to the sound of crows, their calls harsh and territorial, arguing over something in the trees beyond her window. For a disoriented moment, she forgot where she was, expected to see the bedroom in Ann Arbor, expected Marcus to be padding downstairs to make coffee.

Then memory returned, layered and heavy. The inheritance. The drive. The house.

The dream about Marcus.

She pushed herself upright, muscles protesting from tension she had carried through the night. Her phone showed six-fifteen, earlier than she usually woke, but sleep felt impossible now. She dressed in the clothes she had worn yesterday, wrinkling her nose at her own rumpled appearance, and ventured into the hallway.

Margot's door was closed. Eleanor paused outside it, listening, hearing nothing. She remembered her daughter at five, at ten, at every age when Eleanor could still protect her from the world's casual cruelties. Now Margot was nearly grown, and the grief they both carried was too large for Eleanor to shield her from. Maybe that was why Margot stayed angry. Anger was better than drowning.

Eleanor descended to the kitchen, hoping yesterday's observation about modernity meant there might be running water. She turned the tap, and after a moment of groaning protest from pipes, water gushed out, rust-brown at first, then clearing to something approaching clean. Small victories.

She explored the cabinets and found them stocked with canned goods, their labels faded but dates suggesting they were only a few years old. Soup, vegetables, coffee. The coffee especially felt like salvation. She found a percolator in another cabinet, old-fashioned but functional, and set it on the gas stove. When she turned the burner knob, gas hissed, then caught with a blue flame. Apparently some utilities were still connected.

While the coffee brewed, Eleanor examined the kitchen more carefully. The bones of the room were old, probably original to the house, but someone had updated it competently. The sink was modern stainless steel. The refrigerator, though unplugged, looked relatively new. Cabinets had been refaced, countertops replaced.

Someone had lived here. Someone had cared for this place.

But the attorney's letter had mentioned the manor standing empty for months, awaiting estate settlement. Who had been here? When had they left? And why did it feel like they might return at any moment?

The coffee finished percolating, and Eleanor poured a cup, black because there was no milk. She sipped it, grimacing at the bitterness, and carried it to the back door. Through its window, she could see the grounds extending into mist.

Gardens, once formal, now gone wild. A greenhouse with most of its glass intact. Outbuildings that might have been carriage houses, converted to garages. And beyond everything, the forest, dark and dense, rising up the slope of a hill that blocked the horizon.

Eleanor turned the doorknob, expecting it to be locked. It opened smoothly.

The morning air hit her face, cold and damp, carrying scents of decay and growth intermingled. October in the North, where things died and fed the soil for spring's renewal. She stepped onto a stone patio, noting how the stones had settled unevenly over decades, creating subtle traps for unwary feet.

"You'll want to watch your step there."

Eleanor spun, coffee sloshing dangerously close to the cup's rim. A woman stood at the garden's edge, perhaps seventy, dressed in practical clothes that had been mended more than once. Her face was weathered in a way that suggested decades of outdoor work, but her eyes were sharp and assessing.

"Mrs. Hadley," the woman said, not offering a hand. "I keep the grounds. Or kept them, more accurately. Wasn't sure if you'd be wanting services continued."

Eleanor's heart was still recovering from the startle. "I didn't realize anyone was still employed here. The attorney didn't mention—"

"The attorney doesn't know half of what goes on at Blackwood Manor," Mrs. Hadley interrupted. "Never has. The family has always preferred certain arrangements to remain informal." She looked Eleanor up and down with an expression that revealed nothing. "You're the daughter of Thaddeus's sister's son's wife, I'm told. Quite a stretch of relation."

"I didn't know I had family here."

"Most don't, until the house calls them."

Eleanor felt a chill that had nothing to do with the morning air. "I'm sorry?"

"Needs someone living in it, doesn't it? Big house like this. Gets lonely, sitting empty." Mrs. Hadley's tone suggested she was discussing something as mundane as gardening. "Thaddeus understood that. Stayed here until the end, even when his body was failing. Said the house needed him."

"When did he die?" Eleanor asked, trying to ground the conversation in facts.

"March," Mrs. Hadley said, and Eleanor felt the word like a blow. March. The same month Marcus had died. The same month their lives had fractured.

"March seventeenth," Mrs. Hadley continued, watching Eleanor's face. "Went to sleep in the master suite on the third floor, didn't wake up. Peaceful, they said. Though I've worked here thirty years and know better than to trust what's said about Blackwood Manor."

Eleanor wanted to ask what she meant, but Mrs. Hadley had already turned toward the greenhouse. "I'll be in if you need me. House will want breakfast prepared soon. It's particular about routines."

She walked away before Eleanor could respond, leaving Eleanor standing alone on the uneven patio, coffee cooling in her hands, wondering if she had imagined the strangeness of that conversation or if it was exactly as unsettling as it seemed.

Back inside, she found Margot had emerged, drawn by the smell of coffee. Her daughter looked pale, dark circles under her eyes suggesting she had slept as poorly as Eleanor.

"There's a groundskeeper," Eleanor said. "Mrs. Hadley. She was just here."

"Did she come out of the walls?" Margot asked, pouring coffee with hands that trembled slightly. "Because this place feels like people could just emerge from the woodwork."

"She came from the garden. She says she's worked here thirty years."

Margot raised an eyebrow. "And?"

"And she talks about the house like it's alive. Like it has preferences and needs." Eleanor heard how that sounded, tried to moderate. "I'm sure it's just an expression. People who work for old families, they get attached to properties, develop certain ways of speaking."

"Mom." Margot set down her coffee cup with deliberate care. "It's okay to admit this place is weird. You don't have to rationalize everything."

It was the most direct thing Margot had said to her in months. Eleanor felt something shift between them, a small adjustment in their careful dance of grief and blame.

"It's weird," Eleanor conceded. "The house is weird. Mrs. Hadley is weird. All of this is deeply, profoundly weird."

Margot almost smiled. "Was that so hard?"

"Terrifying, actually."

This time Margot did smile, brief but genuine. "Come on. Let's explore before we both lose our nerve."

They spent the morning going through rooms they had only glimpsed the night before, this time with daylight revealing details that darkness had obscured. The library fascinated Eleanor, its collection spanning centuries, from leather-bound volumes that might have been genuinely rare to more recent editions that suggested someone had continued acquiring books well into the twenty-first century.

She pulled out a volume at random, a collection of New England ghost stories published in 1847. The frontispiece bore an inscription in faded ink: "To Nathaniel Blackwood, may these tales remind you of home. J.H." She carefully replaced it, fingers lingering on the spine, wondering how many hands had touched this book, how many Blackwoods had read these stories by firelight.

Margot had discovered a music room, the grand piano they had glimpsed yesterday only one of several instruments. A violin rested in an open case, looking playable. A cello stood in the corner. Sheet music was stacked on the piano in neat piles, organized by composer.

"Someone was musical," Margot said, running a finger across the piano keys. The notes rang out, perfectly in tune, which should have been impossible for an instrument left unattended for months.

"Don't," Eleanor said sharply, then immediately regretted her tone when Margot's face closed. "I just mean, we don't know what condition these instruments are in. We should have them looked at before—"

"Before what? Before they contaminate us with their wrongness?" Margot's voice dripped sarcasm. "It's a piano, Mom. It's not cursed."

But even as she said it, neither of them believed it. The piano's perfect tuning felt like evidence of something neither wanted to name.

They moved on to the third floor, which Eleanor had avoided the night before. The staircase narrowed as it ascended, the walls pressing closer, the air growing stale. At the top, a hallway extended in both directions, doors lining each side.

"This floor feels different," Margot said quietly.

Eleanor felt it too. The second floor had been impersonal, rooms dressed for guests who never came. This floor felt inhabited. Not currently, perhaps, but recently enough that presence lingered like perfume.

She opened the first door and found a study. Desk, bookshelves, filing cabinets. Papers stacked with military precision. A typewriter, electric model from perhaps the 1980s. A computer monitor, old enough to be curved glass but modern enough to suggest use within the last decade.

"This was his office," Eleanor said. "Thaddeus's workspace."

She approached the desk, some part of her feeling like an intruder. The papers were correspondence, letters written in precise cursive, discussing estate management, investments, charitable donations. One letter, dated February, was addressed to the Portland law firm and referenced "arrangements for succession." Thaddeus had known he was dying, had been preparing for it.

Another letter, this one unsent, began with "My dear relation," and Eleanor realized with a jolt it was addressed to her. Her maiden name, Vance, appeared in the second line, though she had never mentioned it in any of their family stories because there had never been family stories to tell about the Blackwood side.

She picked up the letter with trembling hands.

"My dear relation, If you are reading this, then the house has called you, and you have answered. Do not think this was chance. Blackwood Manor chooses its inhabitants as carefully as any living thing selects its symbiotes. You are here because you are needed, and because you need what the house can provide."

The letter continued, but Eleanor couldn't process the words. They swam in her vision, mixing with the morning's coffee and the previous night's unease. The house has called you. Chosen its inhabitants. Symbiotes.

"Mom?" Margot's voice came from down the hall. "You should see this."

Eleanor set the letter down carefully, marking her place with a paperweight, and followed her daughter's voice. Margot stood in the doorway of a room at the hall's end, a room larger than the others, with windows on two walls providing views of the forest and the drive.

The master suite. Where Thaddeus had died.

The room was clean, the bed made with hospital corners. On the nightstand sat a water glass, empty, and a book, bookmark still in place. Eleanor approached and checked the title: "The House of the Seven Gables," Hawthorne's meditation on inherited guilt and cursed dwellings.

"Of course," Eleanor murmured.

"What?"

"Nothing. Just, Hawthorne would be appropriate reading material for someone living in a place like this."

Margot had moved to the windows. "Mom, look at this."

Eleanor joined her and followed her gaze. From this height, they could see over the immediate grounds to the forest beyond. But it wasn't really a forest in the traditional sense. The trees were clustered oddly, creating patterns that looked almost deliberate. And in several places, the ground had collapsed into sinkholes, dark mouths leading down into earth.

"Caves," Eleanor said. "This area must be limestone, karst topography. The ground is literally hollow."

"The hollow beneath," Margot said, her voice strange.

"What?"

"Nothing. Just something that popped into my head." Margot pulled away from the window abruptly. "I'm going back downstairs. This room creeps me out."

She left quickly, footsteps loud on the wooden floor. Eleanor remained, studying the sinkholes, counting at least seven visible from this vantage point. The hollow beneath. The phrase resonated unpleasantly.

She returned to Thaddeus's study and picked up the letter again, forcing herself to read the rest.

"The house requires certain things. Acknowledgment. Respect. Occupancy. In return, it provides shelter of a kind you may not yet understand you need. But it is not without cost. Nothing that protects is ever free."

"The foundations extend deep, Eleanor. Deeper than any blueprint shows. What lies beneath predates the house by millennia. I have spent forty years learning to coexist with it, learning its rhythms, respecting its boundaries. You must do the same."

"Keep the routines. Morning inspection of the grounds. Evening sealing of the doors. Never open the cellar entrance after dark. These are not superstitions. They are protocols developed through hard experience."

"If you find this unbearable, if you choose to leave, I will understand. But know that once the house has called you, leaving becomes complicated. It remembers. It waits."

"Welcome to Blackwood Manor. May you find what you need, and may the cost be bearable."

"Your uncle in blood if not affection, Thaddeus Blackwood."

Eleanor read the letter three times, her rational mind cataloguing reasons to dismiss it as the ramblings of an elderly man in declining health. But her irrational mind, the part that had felt the house breathing in the night, that had recognized its attention, knew the letter was not madness.

It was instruction. Warning. Contract.

She folded the letter and slipped it into her pocket. Margot didn't need to see it. Margot was already too fascinated by this place, already listening for messages Eleanor couldn't hear.

Downstairs, she found Mrs. Hadley had returned and was preparing lunch, moving through the kitchen with practiced efficiency.

"Mrs. Hadley," Eleanor said carefully. "Did you know Thaddeus well?"

The older woman paused in her chopping. "Well as anyone could know him. He was private, like all the Blackwoods. But fair. Paid on time, never asked for more than was reasonable."

"Did he ever talk about the house? About its, about—" Eleanor struggled for words that wouldn't sound insane. "About its particular needs?"

Mrs. Hadley's knife resumed its rhythm. "He understood the house. Respected it. You'd do well to do the same, Doctor Vance."

"How did you know I was a doctor?"

"House mentioned it." Mrs. Hadley said this as casually as if she had said she read it in a letter.

Eleanor felt her scalp prickle. "The house mentioned it."

"House knows what it needs to know." Mrs. Hadley swept the chopped vegetables into a pot. "Lunch will be ready at noon. The house prefers punctuality."

She said nothing more, and Eleanor didn't push. Some conversations, she was learning, were meant to end abruptly.

She found Margot in the library, curled in a leather chair with a book. For a moment, Eleanor saw her daughter as a child again, always reading, always lost in stories. The sight ached with nostalgia and loss.

"Did you find anything useful?" Margot asked without looking up.

"Depends on your definition of useful." Eleanor sank into the chair opposite. "Margot, I need to ask you something. Last night, did you feel anything strange about this house?"

Margot finally looked up, her expression guarded. "Strange how?"

"Like it was aware of us."

Her daughter held her gaze for a long moment. "Yeah," she said finally. "I felt that. Still feel it, actually. Like we're being observed, but not in a threatening way. More like we're being considered."

Eleanor nodded slowly. "That's what I felt too."

"Are we losing our minds?" Margot asked, and beneath the question Eleanor heard hope. Hope that they shared this madness, that they were not alone in their perceptions.

"Maybe," Eleanor said honestly. "Or maybe we're finally paying attention to something we've always sensed but rationalized away."

"You sound like Dad," Margot said, and for once there was no accusation in it. "He always said your logic was a shield against uncomfortable truths."

Eleanor felt tears prick her eyes. "He said that?"

"All the time. But he loved you for it anyway. Said someone had to be the rational one, and he was glad it was you."

They sat in silence, Marcus's ghost between them, less painful than before. Not gone, never gone, but softening slightly. The house creaked around them, wood settling, stones shifting infinitesimally on their foundations. Or perhaps, Eleanor thought, the house was simply breathing, carrying on its patient existence, waiting to see what these new inhabitants would choose.

At noon precisely, Mrs. Hadley called them to lunch. They ate soup and bread at the vast dining table, three people lost in space designed for twenty. Mrs. Hadley served then retreated to the kitchen, declining Eleanor's invitation to join them.

"She's weird, right?" Margot said quietly. "It's not just me?"

"She's extremely weird," Eleanor confirmed. "But maybe everyone who works here long enough becomes that way."

"Or maybe," Margot said, tearing bread with careful precision, "maybe she's just honest about what this place is, and we're the ones being weird by pretending everything is normal."

Eleanor had no argument for that.

After lunch, Mrs. Hadley departed, saying she would return tomorrow morning unless instructed otherwise. Eleanor watched from the kitchen window as the older woman walked down the drive and disappeared into the trees, following a path that apparently led to her own home somewhere in the woods.

Then they were alone with the house.

Evening came early, the sun setting behind the hill at barely four o'clock. Eleanor found herself following Thaddeus's instructions without consciously deciding to. She walked the ground floor, checking windows, testing locks. When she reached the kitchen, she found a door she hadn't noticed before, half-hidden behind a cabinet. It was locked, but the heavy iron bolt suggested it could be opened from this side if needed.

The cellar entrance.

Never open the cellar entrance after dark.

Eleanor checked the bolt twice, making sure it was secured, then left the kitchen quickly, feeling watched. Upstairs, Margot had retreated to her room with a stack of books from the library. Eleanor knocked gently.

"Come in."

Margot lay on her bed, surrounded by volumes with titles like "Folklore of Limestone Regions" and "Cave Systems of Northern New England."

"Light reading?" Eleanor asked.

"I'm trying to understand why there are so many sinkholes around here." Margot held up one book. "Apparently this whole area is riddled with caves. Natural underground chambers, some of them huge. The locals used to think they were entrances to the underworld."

"Caves are just geology, Margot."

"Sure. Just geology that's hollow and dark and goes down for miles." Margot's smile had an edge. "Nothing ominous about that."

Eleanor wanted to argue, wanted to maintain rationality. But the house breathed around them, and deep below, in spaces neither of them had seen, something else might be breathing too.

"Just, don't go exploring," Eleanor said. "At least not alone. Deal?"

"Deal."

Eleanor left her daughter to her research and returned to her own room. Through the window, she could see the forest had become a wall of darkness, impenetrable and absolute. No moon tonight, or perhaps it had not yet risen.

She prepared for bed, then paused at the window one more time. Was that movement out there, between the trees? A figure, walking the grounds?

Eleanor pressed closer to the glass, straining to see. Yes, there, a shape moving with purpose, following the tree line. Mrs. Hadley? But she had left hours ago.

As Eleanor watched, the figure stopped and turned, and though it was too dark to see features, Eleanor knew with absolute certainty that it was looking directly at her. Looking up at her window. Acknowledging her presence.

She stepped back from the window, heart hammering. When she looked again, the figure was gone.

She closed the curtains with shaking hands and climbed into bed, pulling the covers up despite not being cold. The house breathed around her, in and out, in and out, a rhythm that should have been soothing but instead felt like counting down to something inevitable.

Eleanor didn't sleep. She lay awake, listening, waiting, though she couldn't have said what she was waiting for.

In the hollow beneath the house, in spaces older than human language, something patient continued its wait as well. New inhabitants settling in. New patterns forming. New possibilities emerging from configurations that had repeated for generations.

The game was beginning again, as it had begun so many times before.

The house knew how it ended.

It always ended the same way.
