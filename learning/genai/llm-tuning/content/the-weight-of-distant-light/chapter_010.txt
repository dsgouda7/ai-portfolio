Chapter 10: The Aide Who Knew Too Much

Magistrate Ashe learned of the break-in the same way he learned most things that mattered on the Meridian's Promise: through the quiet, efficient network of aides and clerks he had cultivated over eighteen years of careful Council service, a full six hours before Speaker Renn's office issued any formal notice to the wider Magistrate body. His senior aide, a sharp, discreet woman named Petra Lindqvist who had served him for over a decade, brought him the news at his morning briefing with an expression he had rarely seen her wear: something close to fear.

"Deck Two working group office," she said, setting a data slate before him with hands that were not quite steady. "Forced entry, attempted destruction of the storage terminal, roughly ninety minutes before midnight. Security believes it's connected to an organized encrypted communication network operating across Fabrication and portions of Deck Four and Deck Six. Magistrate, I need to tell you something, and I need you to hear it without deciding in advance how you're going to react."

Ashe felt something cold settle into his chest, some instinct born of eighteen years reading the currents beneath Council politics telling him what was coming before Lindqvist said another word. "Tell me."

"The geographic distribution Wren flagged. Fabrication, Deck Four, Deck Six. Magistrate, that's the Steadfast's core membership. Guill Marsh's network. I've had my suspicions for weeks that Marsh's organizing had moved beyond simple petition-gathering and public advocacy, ever since he came to your office with those four others and you turned him down. I didn't have proof. I have something closer to proof now."

Ashe sat very still, the data slate's cold surface beneath his fingers, and felt the full weight of what Lindqvist was telling him settle over eighteen years of carefully built political identity like a shroud. He had spent his entire Council career arguing that the Charter's procedures were the only legitimate path to any decision, however slow, however frustrating, that a closed system with no rescue and no return could not survive the alternative: factions willing to act outside the chamber, to force their preferred outcomes through fear and force rather than argument and vote. He had built his entire opposition to the Lantern investigation on exactly that principle. And now, it seemed, the very people who had claimed to share his convictions most fiercely had broken that principle in the dead of night, in his name, in service of a cause he had publicly championed for weeks.

"I need to see Marsh," he said finally, his voice carefully level despite the roiling anger beneath it. "Today. Privately. Before Speaker Renn's security identifies the network's full membership and this becomes a matter for Council discipline rather than a conversation I can still have as one Magistrate to one constituent."

Lindqvist hesitated. "Magistrate, if his network is responsible for the break-in, meeting with him privately before security completes its investigation could be seen as—"

"I am aware of how it could be seen, Petra. I am asking you to arrange the meeting regardless, because I intend to end whatever this has become before it costs this ship something none of us can afford to lose." He met her eyes, and something in his own gaze must have carried enough conviction to settle her hesitation, because she nodded, and left to make the arrangements without further protest.

Marsh arrived at a small, disused conference room on Deck Five two hours later, at Ashe's specific instruction, chosen deliberately for its distance from both their usual offices and any Council eye that might note the meeting's occurrence. He looked, Ashe thought, watching him settle into the room's single spare chair, considerably less composed than he had during his visit to Ashe's own office weeks before, a man who had perhaps begun to understand, in the intervening time, the true scope of what he had set into motion.

"Magistrate," Marsh began, but Ashe cut him off with a raised hand before he could continue.

"Someone broke into the Council working group's office last night, Guill. Someone in a Fabrication coverall, using a Fabrication tool case, part of an encrypted network security believes originates substantially from your organizing. Tell me now, plainly, whether that was your doing, and I promise you, whatever the truth is, your honesty in this room will matter a great deal more to what happens next than any attempt to protect yourself through denial."

Marsh's broad face went through several expressions in quick succession, defiance, calculation, and finally, something that looked almost like relief, the particular relief of a man setting down a weight he had been carrying alone for too long. "It was not my doing, Magistrate. I want to be clear about that. But it was done by people who answer, in some part, to organizing I helped build, acting on their own initiative, in a manner I neither authorized nor, I confess, entirely discouraged when I first heard whispers of it three days ago." He met Ashe's eyes, and there was genuine fear in them now, fear that went beyond simple concern for his own consequences. "We believed, Magistrate, truly believed, that the working group's continued investigation was accelerating this ship toward a catastrophic decision faster than the Council's own procedures could be trusted to stop. Some of us decided that delaying that investigation, even by illegitimate means, was a smaller risk than allowing it to reach its conclusion unimpeded. I did not order the break-in. But I did not stop it either, when I had the chance, and I understand now that failure sits on me as surely as if I had carried the tool case myself."

Ashe felt the last of his uncertainty crystallize, in that moment, into a clarity he had not felt since the working group's report first crossed his desk. "You have made a grave error, Guill. Not in your fear, which I share more than you know. In your belief that fear justifies abandoning the very principles that have kept this ship whole for two hundred and fourteen years. I have spent my entire career arguing that this vessel's survival depends on trusting its own procedures even when they move slower than our fear demands, because the alternative, factions acting outside the chamber to force their will by stealth and force, is not a safer path than patience. It is the exact danger our ancestors built the Charter to prevent. If your network continues down this path, you will not save this ship from whatever waits at the Lantern. You will hand it a second catastrophe, entirely of our own making, before the first one ever has the chance to arrive."

"What would you have us do, then?" Marsh's voice had an edge of genuine desperation now. "Wait ninety days for a Council report, while whatever ended a whole galactic civilization keeps advancing toward the only world we have left?"

"I would have you trust the process you claim to believe in, or abandon the pretense of believing in it at all," Ashe said. "I am going to bring what you've told me to Speaker Renn's office today, Guill. Not to destroy you, though I expect there will be consequences you'll need to answer for. I am bringing it because a ship that cannot trust its own Magistrates to police their own factions honestly is a ship already halfway to the kind of collapse the Charter was built to prevent, and I will not be the Magistrate who let that collapse happen on his watch, whatever it costs my own standing with the people who believed I would protect them from it by any means necessary."

Marsh sat with that for a long moment, his broad shoulders slumping, and when he finally spoke again, his voice had lost most of its earlier fire. "I'll give you the names, Magistrate. All of them. God help me, I should have come to you the moment I heard what they were planning, instead of letting my own fear talk me into looking away."

Ashe left that meeting an hour later carrying a list of eleven names, and made his way directly to Speaker Renn's office, where he delivered both the list and his own account of the meeting with an openness that surprised even himself, a man who had spent eighteen years carefully guarding every piece of political leverage he possessed, now handing his own faction's worst failure directly to the Speaker who had spent those same eighteen years as his most persistent opponent.

Renn listened to his full account without interruption, her expression unreadable, and when he finished, she was quiet for a long moment before speaking. "I won't pretend this doesn't complicate things politically for you, Magistrate. Some in your own bloc will call this a betrayal."

"Some in my own bloc broke Charter law in the dead of night to force their will on this Council," Ashe said. "I would call handing you their names an act of loyalty to the ship, whatever it costs my standing with the people who did it."

Something in Renn's expression shifted then, a grudging respect Ashe had genuinely not expected to see directed at him from across the wide political gulf that had separated them for years. "Then let's make sure that loyalty is worth something," she said. "I want Officer Delane's team to move on these eleven names quietly, within the day, before word spreads and any of them decide to escalate further out of panic. And I want you in the room when we do it, Magistrate, standing beside the working group you've spent weeks opposing, so that every person on this ship who might be tempted to follow the Steadfast's example understands, clearly, that this Council's own most careful, cautious voice believes the chamber's procedures are still worth defending, even now, even against people who claimed to share his own convictions."

Ashe agreed, and it was in the strange, uneasy alliance formed that afternoon, Magistrate and Speaker and security officer and the young Hold technician whose sensor report had started this entire crisis, standing together as Oskar Delane's team moved to quietly detain eleven residents across three decks, that the ship's fractured governance began, however tentatively, to knit itself back together.

But eleven names, Aria would learn within the week, were not the whole of the Steadfast's membership, and the network's most committed core, having watched their first attempt fail and their own leadership turn against them, had already begun quietly planning something considerably more drastic than a single midnight break-in.
