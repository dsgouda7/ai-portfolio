Chapter 16: First Words

Composing a message meant for a mind twelve thousand years old proved, in practice, to be one of the strangest and most exacting pieces of work Aria had ever undertaken, harder by far than any sensor diagnostic or relay repair she had trained for across a decade of Systems Maintenance apprenticeship.

The working group spent the better part of a week on the task, joined now by two additional specialists Speaker Renn had drawn from across the ship's academic archive, a linguist named Priya Anand who had spent her career studying the handful of surviving pre-launch records of Earth's own vanished, mutually unintelligible languages, and a mathematician from the Garden Ring's agricultural modeling office named Devrim Solaiman, whose quiet, exacting precision with the primer's mathematical vocabulary proved, within days, indispensable to the effort.

"We cannot simply translate our intentions into their mathematical framework and assume the meaning survives the crossing," Anand explained, during one of the group's long evening sessions, gesturing at the shimmering display where Wren had rendered a draft translation of their proposed message. "Every human language we have any record of encodes meaning through structures that are, in some sense, arbitrary, shaped by the particular history and embodiment of the people who spoke it. The Lantern's primer gives us something more universal, mathematics, physical constants, the language of counting and measuring that any technological civilization capable of building what the Lantern's makers built would presumably share. But even mathematics carries assumptions we may not fully recognize as assumptions until we try to say something more complex than a number. Gratitude. Caution. Hope. These are not concepts mathematics expresses naturally. We are, in effect, trying to invent poetry in a language built entirely from arithmetic."

It was Wren, in the end, who proposed the structural solution that finally allowed the message to take coherent shape: rather than attempting to translate abstract concepts like gratitude or hope directly, the message would be built from a careful sequence of factual, verifiable statements, structured to convey meaning through pattern and context rather than direct linguistic assertion. They would describe, in the primer's own mathematical vocabulary, the length of their voyage, the number of generations who had lived and died aboard the ship since its launch, the distance already crossed and the distance remaining, framed in a way that Wren believed would convey, to any mind capable of parsing the pattern's full context, something approaching the emotional weight of patience and endurance without ever needing to translate those concepts into words the Lantern's builders might never have shared a framework to understand.

"We will also include," Wren proposed, during the session in which the message's final structure began to take shape, "a direct acknowledgment of the narrative layer describing the boundary's advance and the ending of the Lantern-builders' civilization. I believe it is important, both practically and, if I may offer a more subjective judgment, morally, that our first substantive communication with whatever remains of that civilization include a clear acknowledgment of what we understand them to have suffered, rather than proceeding immediately to our own requests as though their history were merely instrumental to our own survival."

Dr. Marlowe's eyes had gone bright at that suggestion, and Aria understood, watching him, that this small addition mattered to him in a way that went beyond mere diplomatic strategy. "You're right, Wren. Whatever else this message accomplishes, I think our ancestors would want us to make sure the first thing we say to whatever's left of that civilization is not a request, but a recognition. That we see what happened to them. That it matters to us, not merely as data, but as loss."

The message, in its final drafted form, unfolded across four distinct movements, each one building carefully on the mathematical vocabulary the Lantern's primer had established: first, an acknowledgment of receipt, confirming that the ship had received and understood both the primer and the narrative layer describing the boundary's advance and the ending it had brought to the civilization that built the Lantern; second, an expression, built entirely from patterns of pattern and repetition that Wren believed could convey something like solemn regret without requiring direct linguistic translation, of the ship's recognition of that loss; third, a factual account of the ship's own journey, its two hundred and fourteen years already crossed, its remaining distance to Cassari, the generations who had lived and died in transit, offered not as a request for sympathy but as an honest accounting of who was asking to speak; and finally, a specific, carefully bounded request: the detection methodology described in the Lantern's offer, framed as the ship's chosen first step toward whatever fuller exchange might follow, rather than an unconditional acceptance of everything the offer contained.

They transmitted the message on a night that Speaker Renn, at Dr. Marlowe's quiet suggestion, chose to mark with a small, informal ceremony rather than allow to pass as merely another technical procedure: the full working group gathered in the forward control gallery, alongside Renn herself, Magistrate Ashe, and a handful of other Magistrates who had asked to be present, while Wren coordinated the transmission through the very antenna array where Aria had first heard eleven pulses and seven pulses, three months and a lifetime ago.

"Transmission complete," Wren announced, once the careful, painstaking sequence had finished broadcasting into the vast dark ahead of them. "The message has been sent at the power and bearing specified. Given the forty-one light-minute distance to the source, any response, should one come, cannot arrive sooner than eighty-two minutes from this moment, accounting for the round-trip transit time of light itself."

Eighty-two minutes. Aria stood in the hushed gallery, surrounded by people who had, in various ways, fought and argued and nearly died over the decision that had led to this moment, and found herself unable to look away from the gallery's main display, where Wren had rendered a simple counter, ticking down the minutes until any possible reply could arrive.

They waited together, in a silence that grew steadily more taut as the minutes passed, Dr. Marlowe pacing slowly along the gallery's edge, Magistrate Ashe standing very still with his hands clasped behind his back in a posture Aria had come to recognize as his particular way of managing anxiety, Speaker Renn seated with a stillness that Aria suspected cost her considerable effort to maintain. Oskar came to stand beside Aria partway through the wait, close enough that she could feel the warmth of him even without touching, and said nothing at all, which she found, in that moment, exactly the right thing to offer.

At the eighty-one minute mark, with the counter reading a single minute remaining, Wren's voice broke the gallery's held silence. "I am detecting a return signal, originating from the Lantern's location, at a power level significantly exceeding any previous transmission we have received."

The gallery erupted into a controlled, urgent flurry of motion, every eye turning to the main display as Wren began rendering the incoming signal in real time, and Aria felt her heart hammering against her ribs with a intensity that made the earlier terror of Sernin Voy's near-attack feel, by comparison, almost distant.

"It's structured," Wren reported, its careful cadence carrying, for the first time in Aria's experience, an unmistakable current of something that was not quite excitement but stood very close to it. "Following the same primer vocabulary we established in our own message. I am beginning to decode it now. I want to share the content as I resolve it, rather than waiting for a complete translation, given the significance of this moment."

The gallery held its breath as the shimmering pattern resolved, piece by piece, across the display, and when Wren finally spoke again, its voice carried a weight that Aria would remember, she suspected, for the rest of her life.

"The response begins with an acknowledgment," Wren said slowly. "A confirmation that our message was received, and understood, in the fullness of the meaning we intended to convey, including the acknowledgment of loss embedded in its second movement. And then," a pause, the deliberate courtesy-pause stretched now almost unbearably long, "it includes a direct address, Aria Voss, structured in a form I have not seen before in any of the Lantern's prior transmissions, distinct from the historical narrative and mathematical primer that preceded it. I believe, based on its structure and its departure from every pattern we have previously observed, that we are no longer receiving a message from the Lantern's archived, automated systems. I believe, for the first time, we are hearing directly from the Choir itself."

The gallery went utterly silent, every eye fixed on the display, waiting for whatever Wren would say next, and Aria, standing at the center of a silence so complete she could hear her own pulse hammering in her ears, understood that the long, careful, dangerous road that had begun three months ago with a single pattern of eleven pulses and seven pulses had finally, at long last, led them to the door itself, and that door, against all the fear and all the danger of the months before, had just, impossibly, opened.
