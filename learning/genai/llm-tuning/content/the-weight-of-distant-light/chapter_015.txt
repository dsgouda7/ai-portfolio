Chapter 15: The Answer We Choose to Give

The Council session convened to consider Wren's latest findings drew the largest public gallery attendance in the ship's recorded history, a fact Steward Halvorsen noted with visible unease as she surveyed the packed tiers above the chamber floor before Speaker Renn called the session to order. Word of the working group's discovery, this time released through careful, deliberate Council announcement rather than uncontrolled leak, had spread through the ship's nine decks and the Under-Hold beneath them with a speed that spoke to just how thoroughly the Lantern had come to dominate every conversation aboard the Meridian's Promise in the weeks since Aria's first quiet discovery at node seventeen.

Speaker Renn opened the session with a summary of Wren's findings, delivered with the same careful, measured clarity she had brought to every public address since the crisis began, and then turned the chamber's attention to the question that had brought every Magistrate and half the ship's population to this session: whether the Council would authorize a formal response to the Lantern's offer, and if so, what that response would contain.

It was Magistrate Ashe who rose first to speak, and Aria, watching from the witness floor where she had once again been summoned to stand, felt a genuine uncertainty about what he would say, uncertainty that would not have existed six weeks earlier, before she had watched him hand Speaker Renn a list of eleven names at real cost to his own political standing.

"I have spent the past six weeks in a position I never expected to occupy," Ashe began, his voice carrying through the packed chamber with the same patient authority it always had, though something in his bearing now felt, to Aria's eye, less certain, more genuinely searching than the confident opposition he had offered at the very first session on this matter. "I opposed this investigation from its earliest hours, believing then, and believing still, that this ship's first duty is the preservation of a stable society across a voyage our ancestors designed with no margin for catastrophic surprise. I have watched that caution, in the hands of people more frightened and less patient than myself, curdle into something considerably darker: sabotage, attempted violence, a betrayal of the very Charter principles I believed I was defending. I do not offer that account to excuse my own earlier opposition, which I stand by as an honest expression of legitimate concern. I offer it because I believe this chamber deserves to understand, from the man who led that opposition, why I have concluded that the question before us today is no longer the question I originally raised."

He paused, and the packed gallery held its collective breath, waiting.

"We are no longer debating whether to look away from an unknown danger," Ashe continued. "That ship, if I may extend Dr. Marlowe's own metaphor, sailed the moment Technician Voss first heard a signal counting itself out in prime numbers, patient and mathematical and undeniably real. We are debating, now, whether to accept an offer of knowledge that may substantially improve this vessel's chance of surviving whatever threat the Lantern's builders encountered and ultimately succumbed to, knowledge that Wren herself, if I may use that term for our ship's own careful, patient intelligence, believes could grant us decades of advance warning against a danger we would otherwise face blind. I have spent six weeks reconsidering what genuine caution actually requires of this Council, and I have concluded that refusing an offer of potentially life-saving knowledge, purely because accepting it requires us to overcome our fear of the unknown, is not caution at all. It is a different kind of recklessness, dressed in caution's clothing. I intend to vote in favor of authorizing a formal response to the Lantern's offer, and I ask this chamber to join me, not because the risk has disappeared, but because I no longer believe refusing to answer is actually the safer choice."

The chamber erupted into a murmur that took Speaker Renn nearly a full minute to quiet, and Aria, watching Ashe return to his seat with an expression that mixed relief and lingering uncertainty in equal measure, felt something settle in her own chest that she recognized, after a moment, as a kind of cautious respect she had not expected to ever feel for the man who had once, in this same chamber, called her discovery an uncontrolled variable of unknown lethality.

The debate that followed ran long into the afternoon, Magistrates rising in turn to raise questions Aria had turned over in her own mind a hundred times in the preceding weeks: what would a formal response actually say, and who would decide its content; what specific technical information should the ship request first, given that the offer's full scope remained only partially understood; what safeguards should be built into any exchange, to ensure the ship retained the ability to disengage if the Choir's responses ever suggested intent other than the patient goodwill the Lantern's narrative implied. It was Dr. Marlowe, called to testify alongside Aria and Oskar as the working group's assembled experts, who offered the framing that ultimately seemed to settle the chamber's remaining hesitation.

"I have spent forty years studying the handful of surviving records from Earth's own early efforts at interstellar communication," he said, his voice carrying an unhurried scholarly authority that seemed to still the restless gallery more effectively than any of the session's more urgent rhetoric had managed. "Those early efforts, crude and limited as they were by the standards of what we understand today, shared one common conviction among the scientists and diplomats who designed them: that the risk of remaining silent, of a civilization curling inward upon itself out of fear of the unknown, was in the long run a greater danger to that civilization's survival and its own moral character than the risk of reaching outward in good faith. I do not offer that conviction as proof that this chamber's decision carries no danger. I offer it because I believe our own ancestors, who built this vessel and wrote its Charter, shared that same conviction, embedding it explicitly in the very first-contact protocols Technician Voss's discovery ultimately triggered. They did not write those protocols to enable endless caution. They wrote them to ensure that if this moment ever came, and I do not think any of them truly expected it would, this ship would meet it with careful, deliberate courage, rather than either reckless haste or fearful paralysis. I believe that is exactly the choice before this chamber today."

The vote, when Speaker Renn finally called it near the session's close, was decisive in a way none of the previous votes on this matter had been: twenty-six in favor of authorizing a formal response, five opposed, no abstentions, a margin that spoke, Aria thought, to just how thoroughly the past six weeks of crisis and revelation had reshaped the chamber's understanding of what genuine prudence actually required.

Speaker Renn, in the session's closing moments, turned to address the working group directly, her expression carrying the particular weight of a decision now fully hers to see through. "I want a formal response drafted within the week," she said. "I want it to acknowledge the Lantern's warning, to express this ship's gratitude for the offer of knowledge regarding the boundary phenomenon, and to request, as a first measure of good faith, the detection methodology Wren described, which I consider the single most valuable and least fraught piece of the Choir's offer to begin with. I want any further exchange, including any direct contact with the Choir itself, to proceed in careful, incremental stages, each one subject to this Council's ongoing review, rather than a single unconditional opening of every door at once. And I want Technician Voss," she said, turning her attention directly to Aria on the witness floor below, "to have a central role in drafting that response, given that she is the one who first heard this ship's counterpart reaching out to us, patient and mathematical, across twelve thousand years and forty-one light-minutes of dark."

Aria felt the weight of that charge settle onto her shoulders with a gravity that made her knees feel suddenly unsteady beneath her, and looking up at the packed gallery, at Ashe's watchful, considering expression, at Dr. Marlowe's proud, misty-eyed smile, at Oskar's steady, encouraging nod from beside the witness floor, she understood that the quiet, ordinary life she had lived before that night at node seventeen, three hours into a diagnostic that should have taken four minutes, had ended for good, replaced now by something far larger and far stranger than she had ever imagined her life aboard the Meridian's Promise could become.

"I'll do it, Speaker," she said, and her voice, to her own surprise, came out steady and sure. "I'll help write our answer."

That evening, in the Deck Two office, the working group began the delicate, painstaking work of composing humanity's first deliberate reply to an intelligence older than its own written history, and Aria, staring at the shimmering, patient patterns of the Lantern's own transmission still displayed on the office's repaired wall screen, found herself thinking, more than anything else, of eleven pulses and seven pulses, patient and unhurried, and of the strange, dizzying honor of finally, after six years of silence, being ready to say something back.
