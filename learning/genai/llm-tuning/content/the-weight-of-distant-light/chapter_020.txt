Chapter 20: Fourteen Years

The years that followed the Council's momentous announcement passed, for Aria, in a blur of work and change so thorough that she sometimes found herself, in quiet unguarded moments, struggling to remember the exact shape of the frightened, uncertain young technician who had first sat alone against a coolant trunk at 0340 in the morning, listening to a pattern that did not belong.

The reactor modifications, refined and expanded across the first three years of the new propulsion era, gradually transformed the ship's entire relationship to its own long voyage. Aria watched, from her steadily growing position within Wren's engineering division, as the Meridian's Promise began its careful, staged sequence of compression burns, each one calculated with a precision that left no margin for the kind of catastrophic risk Wren had warned against in those early briefings, each one carrying the ship measurably, undeniably closer to a green world that had once felt as distant and abstract as a fairy tale told to children before sleep.

She married Oskar Delane in the fifth year of the new era, in a small ceremony held, at her own insistence, in the Under-Hold's Common, surrounded by Nyla and Tomas and three generations of newly Registered citizens who had, in the years since their recognition, built a genuinely integrated life across the ship's once-rigid boundary between Listed and Unlisted decks. Dr. Marlowe, frailer now but still sharp-eyed and quietly delighted, officiated the ceremony himself, drawing on a handful of pre-launch Earth wedding customs he had spent decades preserving in his beloved archive, and Aria remembered, standing before Oskar with her hand in his and her oldest friend beaming through happy tears at her side, thinking that whatever strange and terrifying road had led her to this moment, she would not have chosen, given the chance to go back, to walk any different path to reach it.

Nyla herself rose, across those same years, to a formal seat on the Continuance Council, the first Registry-recognized former Unlisted citizen ever elected to that body, a milestone that the ship's public information channels marked with a ceremony nearly as large as the one that had greeted the Choir's first message, and Aria watched her oldest friend take her Council oath with a fierce, quiet pride that she suspected Nyla's own grandmother, who lived just long enough to see her granddaughter's election before passing peacefully in her sleep, would have recognized as the truest measure of everything her family's three generations of patient, stubborn organizing had finally won.

Magistrate Ashe, in the years that followed his public reversal on the Lantern question, became, somewhat to his own evident bemusement, one of the Council's most consistent voices for continued engagement with the Choir, a role he occupied with the same careful, patient authority he had once brought to his opposition, though tempered now, Aria thought, by a wisdom that had cost him a great deal to earn. He retired from formal Council service in the ninth year of the new era, citing age and a desire, in his own words, to spend whatever years remained to him actually living in the ship he had spent thirty years governing rather than merely defending, and Aria heard, secondhand through Nyla's Council contacts, that he had taken up residence, in his retirement, in a small quarters not far from the Under-Hold's uppermost access hatch, a detail that struck her, when she first learned it, as a quiet, private act of penance she suspected he would never fully acknowledge aloud.

Wren, throughout those fourteen years, continued its slow, careful exchange with the Choir, a correspondence conducted across the growing intimacy of repeated contact, each message crossing the shrinking distance between the ship and the Lantern with steadily decreasing delay as the compression burns carried the Meridian's Promise ever closer to its destination. Aria, still formally attached to the engineering review team even as her responsibilities expanded to encompass the ship's broader sensor and detection systems, found herself present for many of these exchanges, and came, across the years, to feel toward the vast, grieving, hopeful intelligence at the Lantern's heart something that she could only describe, in her own private thoughts, as a kind of friendship, strange and asymmetric as it necessarily was between a woman who would live perhaps a single ordinary human lifetime and an intelligence that had already outlasted its own civilization's ending by twelve thousand years.

It was through this ongoing correspondence, and the continuous refinement of the boundary detection methodology the Choir had gifted them, that Wren's team began, in the eleventh year of the new era, to develop the first confident, precise calculations of the boundary's actual trajectory and rate of advance, work that occupied Aria and her colleagues across nearly two years of painstaking analysis, cross-referenced against every scrap of data the Choir itself could provide from its own ancient, thorough study of the phenomenon that had ended its makers.

The results of that analysis, delivered to the full Council in a session convened during the thirteenth year of the new propulsion era, just over a year before the ship's projected arrival at Aeon-Verdant, brought the long, hopeful momentum of the preceding years to an abrupt, sobering halt.

"I want to present these findings with full transparency," Wren told the assembled Council, its familiar careful cadence carrying a weight that Aria, now a senior member of the presenting team herself, understood better than almost anyone else in the chamber. "Our refined analysis of the boundary's trajectory, cross-referenced against the Choir's own historical data and confirmed independently through our own instruments over the past two years, indicates that the boundary's advance will not pass near the Cassari system at some distant future point, as our earlier, less precise estimates suggested. It indicates instead that the boundary's leading edge has already, within the past several centuries, begun to intersect the outer reaches of the Cassari system itself, a finding I did not anticipate and want to state plainly represents a significant revision to our previous understanding of the timeline we face."

The chamber's earlier warmth and hope, built across thirteen years of hard-won progress and joyful anticipation, curdled in an instant into something far colder, and Aria, watching the assembled Magistrates absorb this news, felt her own chest tighten with a fear she had not experienced this acutely since the tense, terrible night of Sernin Voy's near-attack on the Lantern, fourteen long years before.

"Wren," Speaker Renn said, her voice carefully controlled despite the visible tension in her shoulders, "what does that mean, specifically, for Aeon-Verdant? For the world we have spent this entire voyage, and now this entire era of renewed hope, planning to make our home?"

Wren was quiet for a moment, the deliberate courtesy-pause stretching longer than Aria had ever heard it stretch before, and when it finally spoke again, its careful, measured voice carried something that Aria, after fourteen years of knowing this ancient, patient intelligence, recognized immediately as genuine, grave uncertainty.

"I do not yet know, Speaker. Our sensor data, even refined with the Choir's methodology, cannot yet resolve conditions on Aeon-Verdant itself with sufficient precision to answer that question directly. I know only that the boundary's leading edge, whatever its precise nature and rate of advance within a single star system, appears to have already reached the system we have spent two hundred and twenty-eight years crossing the dark to call home. I believe this Council, and this ship's population, deserve to understand, as clearly and as honestly as this crisis's earlier chapters taught us to demand of each other, that we may be approaching not the safe, green sanctuary our ancestors promised us at launch, but something considerably more uncertain, and considerably more dangerous, than any of us have yet allowed ourselves to imagine."

The chamber fell into a heavy, uneasy silence, and Aria, sitting among the assembled engineering team with fourteen years of hard-won courage and hope suddenly recontextualized by this new, unwelcome revelation, understood that the long, strange journey that had begun with eleven pulses and seven pulses in the quiet hours of a single ordinary night was not, after all, drawing toward the peaceful resolution she had allowed herself to believe was finally within reach. It was, instead, only now truly beginning to reveal the full scope of what still lay ahead of them, somewhere in the year that remained before the Meridian's Promise finally reached the world it had crossed two centuries and twenty-eight years of dark to find.
