Chapter 9: Break

Aria ran the last stretch of corridor to the Deck Two office, her boots loud against the decking in the ship's hushed overnight quiet, and found the office door propped open, its lock housing scorched black around the edges where something had clearly been used to force it, a security tech she didn't recognize crouched beside the frame with a scanner, and Oskar Delane standing just inside, his face set in the tight, controlled stillness she had come to recognize as his particular way of holding back real anger.

"What happened?" she asked, breathless, stepping past the security tech into the small office, and stopped short at the sight of the room's interior: the single wall display cracked across one corner, Dr. Marlowe's satchel of paper notes scattered across the floor as though someone had upended it searching for something, the room's storage terminal, which held three weeks of the working group's accumulated analysis, showing a dark, unresponsive screen.

"Someone forced entry approximately ninety minutes ago," Oskar said, his voice carefully level. "Wren flagged the door breach immediately and alerted Deck Nine security, but by the time my team arrived, whoever it was had already gone. They didn't get far. But they got far enough."

"Far enough for what?"

"They attempted to physically destroy the storage terminal," Wren's voice said, filling the small office from its ceiling grille, and Aria heard, for the first time since she had known the AI, something in its careful cadence that sounded almost like distress. "I want to report immediately that the attempt was unsuccessful in the manner they likely intended. All working group data, including the full record of my analysis of the Lantern's transmission, is mirrored continuously across the ship's distributed cognition lattice, a redundancy built into my core architecture that no single terminal breach can meaningfully threaten. Nothing has been lost. But I believe the intent behind this act is, in itself, significant information, and I want to share what I have determined about it before Officer Delane's team completes its physical investigation."

"Show me," Aria said, and the cracked display flickered to life, rendering a security capture Wren had pulled from a corridor sensor two junctions away from the office, timestamped ninety-three minutes earlier: a figure in a maintenance coverall, face obscured by a work hood pulled low, moving with quick, deliberate purpose down the corridor toward the Deck Two office, carrying a compact tool case.

"I cannot yet identify this individual with confidence," Wren said. "The corridor sensor at this location is an older model with limited resolution, and the individual appears to have deliberately avoided the higher-resolution sensors near the main junction. However, I have cross-referenced the tool case design against ship inventory records. It is consistent with equipment issued to Fabrication deck personnel, rather than Systems Maintenance or Security."

Aria felt something cold settle in her stomach, remembering Nyla's rumor from earlier that evening, the fear that had found its way, as fear always did, toward the people with the least power to fight back, and the Fabrication deck detail suddenly took on a different, more troubling shape in her mind. "Fabrication. Oskar, that's not Under-Hold. That's not even close to Under-Hold."

"No," Oskar agreed grimly. "It's not."

Dr. Marlowe arrived some ten minutes later, summoned from his own quarters by Oskar's alert, and knelt slowly, with the careful joints of a man well past his physical prime, to gather his scattered notes from the floor, his weathered face drawn tight with an anger Aria had rarely seen from him. "They went through everything," he said quietly, sorting pages with hands that trembled slightly, whether from age or fury Aria could not tell. "Every paper note I've made connecting the Lantern's structure to the old Earth interstellar plaques. If they'd found what they were looking for and actually managed to destroy the terminal too, they'd have set our analysis back weeks, maybe longer, at exactly the moment the ninety-day clock is running out fastest."

"Which raises the obvious question," Oskar said, his voice tight. "Who benefits from this working group's analysis disappearing, right now, three weeks before the halfway point of the mandate?"

Nobody in the room said Magistrate Ashe's name aloud, but Aria felt it hanging in the air between them regardless, and found herself, somewhat to her own surprise, reluctant to simply accept the obvious accusation. She thought of Ashe's careful, measured arguments in the Council chamber, his insistence on working through proper channels, his willingness, however reluctant, to accept the Council's votes against his own position rather than simply defying them. It did not fit, she realized, turning the pieces over in her mind, with a man who had spent eighteen years building his authority on patient, procedural persistence.

"I don't think it's Ashe," she said slowly, and both men turned to look at her. "Not directly, anyway. Everything I've seen of him in Council session, he plays by the rules, even when he's furious about how the votes go. This isn't his style. This is someone who's decided the rules aren't moving fast enough."

"Someone in his bloc, then," Oskar said. "Acting without his direct knowledge, or at least without his direct authorization."

"Maybe," Aria said. "Or maybe someone who's decided his bloc isn't moving fast enough either."

Wren's voice cut through the room's uneasy silence. "I want to add a data point to this discussion that I believe is relevant. Over the past eleven days, I have detected an unusual pattern in internal communication network traffic, concentrated among a group of approximately thirty ship residents spread across Fabrication, Deck Six residential, and portions of Deck Four, characterized by frequent private messaging using an encryption protocol not standard to ordinary ship communications, though not sophisticated enough to fully defeat my own monitoring capacity. I had not previously flagged this pattern for escalation, as private encrypted communication is not, in itself, a violation of any ship policy, and I am bound by the First Charter's privacy provisions to exercise restraint in surveilling residents absent specific cause. Tonight's break-in, however, provides specific cause. I am now able to report that the encrypted network's traffic increased sharply in the hours immediately preceding the break-in, and that its geographic distribution overlaps substantially with the Fabrication deck personnel roster from which tonight's tool case appears to have originated."

"A network," Dr. Marlowe said slowly. "Organized. Not just one angry technician acting alone."

"That is the pattern my analysis suggests, yes."

Oskar's expression had hardened into something Aria recognized, from her weeks of working alongside him, as the particular focus he brought to a genuine security concern rather than a routine procedural matter. "I need to bring this to Speaker Renn immediately, tonight, not in the morning. If there's an organized faction willing to break into a secured Council office and attempt to destroy working group data, that's not a policy disagreement anymore. That's a threat to this ship's governance, and it needs to be treated as one before it escalates into something worse."

He was already moving toward the door as he spoke, and Aria found herself following, some instinct telling her that whatever came next in this strange, accelerating crisis, she did not want to be left behind in a Deck Two office with a cracked display and Dr. Marlowe's scattered notes, waiting for news secondhand.

Speaker Renn received them in her private office at nearly 0100 ship-time, her practical coat thrown hastily over sleep clothes, her short dark hair rumpled in a way that made her look, for the first time since Aria had met her, genuinely young, genuinely tired, genuinely human beneath the careful composure of her office. She listened to Oskar's report without interrupting, her expression growing steadily more grave, and when he finished, she sat quietly for a long moment before speaking.

"I need you to understand something, all three of you, before this goes any further," she said finally. "Two hundred and fourteen years, this ship has never had an organized faction willing to act outside the Council's own procedures. Not once. Every disagreement, every crisis, every hard vote, it's all gone through the chamber, through the Charter, through the mechanisms our ancestors built specifically to keep us from ever becoming the kind of closed, frightened system that turns on itself. If what Wren's monitoring suggests is real, if there's a genuine organized faction willing to break Charter law to force its preferred outcome on the Lantern question, then we are facing something I don't think any Speaker in this ship's history has had to face before. Not an external threat. An internal one." She looked up at them, her eyes hard with a resolve that Aria found, despite the lateness of the hour and the weight of the news, genuinely steadying to witness. "I want Officer Delane's security team to identify this network fully before we move against it, quietly, carefully, without tipping our hand until we understand its full scope. And I want the working group to continue its analysis exactly as before, without letting tonight's incident slow you down, because whatever internal fracture is forming in this ship, the Lantern and whatever's advancing behind it have not stopped being real while we sort out our own house. We have sixty-seven days left in the mandate. I intend for this Council to still have all the information it needs, and all the security it needs to act on that information wisely, when that deadline arrives."

It was nearly dawn by the time Aria finally made it back to her bunk, exhausted in a way that went beyond simple lack of sleep, and lying in the grey half-light of the ship's simulated morning, she found herself thinking not of the Lantern, or the boundary, or the twelve-thousand-year warning, but of Nyla's rumor from earlier that evening, of fear finding its way toward the people with the least power to fight back, and of a masked figure in a Fabrication coverall who had, for reasons still unclear, decided that breaking Charter law in the dead of night was a smaller risk than trusting the ship's own careful, patient process to reach the right conclusion in time.

She did not know yet how close that masked figure's network had come to succeeding, nor how much further they were willing to go before the ninety-day mandate finally ran its course. She knew only that the quiet, careful ship she had grown up in was changing shape around her, faster than any of them, Council or working group or Under-Hold organizer alike, seemed fully able to control.
