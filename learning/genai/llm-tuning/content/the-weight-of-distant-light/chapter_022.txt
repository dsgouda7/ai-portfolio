Chapter 22: Voices From the Green World

The full Council convened in emergency session within hours of Aria's discovery, and this time, Speaker Renn did not wait for a formal ninety-day mandate or a careful staged disclosure before bringing the finding before the ship's entire population. Fourteen years of hard-won trust, Aria understood, watching Renn address the packed chamber with the same open, unflinching honesty she had brought to every crisis since the Lantern's first appearance, had earned the ship's people the right to walk into this new unknown together, from its very first hour, rather than in the fractured, fearful stages of the crisis's earlier chapters.

"Wren's analysis of the atmospheric signal detected by Technician Voss," Renn told the chamber, "indicates that Aeon-Verdant is not, as our original pre-launch survey data led us to believe for two hundred and twenty-eight years, an uninhabited world awaiting our arrival. It appears, instead, to be home to an intelligent, technologically capable population, whose broadcast signal, once decoded, may tell us a great deal about what we are truly approaching, and what our own arrival might mean for the people who already call that world home."

The chamber's reaction to this news carried none of the raw panic that had greeted the Lantern's first discovery, but something more complicated: a kind of stunned, wondering silence that Aria recognized, in her own chest, as the particular vertigo of watching an entire lifetime's understanding of the universe reshape itself yet again.

Wren's decoding of the signal, accomplished over the following two weeks with a speed that Aria suspected owed a great deal to fourteen years of practice parsing the Lantern's own mathematical vocabulary, revealed a broadcast considerably simpler in structure than the vast, layered transmission the Lantern had once sent them, but unmistakably, movingly familiar in its underlying logic: the same universal mathematical primer, the same careful, deliberate signaling designed to be recognized by any technological listener, adapted now by minds that had, it seemed, learned that vocabulary from a source Aria found herself unsurprised, by this point in the long, strange journey, to recognize.

"The signal's mathematical structure bears unmistakable resemblance to elements of the Lantern's own primer vocabulary," Wren reported, during the working group session convened to review its findings. "I believe this indicates that Aeon-Verdant's native population has had prior contact, at some point in their own history, with either the Choir directly, or with some other remnant or descendant of the civilization that built the Lantern. Beneath this shared mathematical foundation, the signal contains what I believe is a genuine, independently developed language, one I do not yet have sufficient data to translate with confidence, though I have begun the work in earnest."

Dr. Marlowe, present at the session despite the visible strain his advancing age placed on him after long days of work, leaned forward with an intensity that reminded Aria, fondly, of the first days of the Lantern investigation fourteen years before. "A living civilization," he said quietly. "Wren, do you have any sense yet of their technological level, their numbers, anything at all about who they might be?"

"My analysis remains preliminary," Wren said, "but the signal's broadcast power and structure suggest a civilization considerably less advanced than our own in certain respects, likely lacking spaceflight capability or the deep industrial infrastructure that capability requires, but possessing sophisticated mathematics, deliberate long-range communication technology, and, I believe, a cultural memory that includes some awareness of the broader cosmic context you and I have spent fourteen years learning to understand ourselves. I want to share one further finding, which I believe is significant. Embedded within the signal, using the same primer vocabulary that first allowed me to establish a translation framework, is a repeating pattern I have tentatively decoded as a warning, structurally similar in its logic, though far simpler in execution, to the boundary warning the Lantern itself once conveyed to us."

The room went very still. "They know about the boundary too," Aria said slowly. "Wren, are they warning us away from something? The same way the Lantern first seemed to be warning us, before we understood it was actually an invitation?"

"I cannot yet be certain," Wren said. "But I believe, given everything the Lantern and the Choir have taught us across fourteen years of careful engagement, that the most responsible course is to respond to this signal exactly as we once responded to the Lantern's own transmission: with patience, with careful acknowledgment, and with a genuine willingness to listen before we act."

The Council authorized a formal response within the week, drawing once again on the working group's accumulated expertise, though this time the process moved with a confidence and speed that would have been unimaginable during the ship's first, halting attempt to answer the Lantern fourteen years before. Priya Anand, now heading the ship's small but well-established linguistics division, led the translation effort alongside Wren, and Aria found herself, in the weeks that followed, watching her own daughter, Mira, born in the ninth year of the new propulsion era and now a bright, curious five-year-old who had grown up hearing bedtime stories about a patient, hopeful Choir reaching across the dark, ask her mother with wide, serious eyes whether the green world's people would be friendly, a question Aria found she could not answer with the easy reassurance her daughter's innocence deserved.

"I don't know yet, little one," she told Mira, tucking her into her bunk in the modest quarters she shared with Oskar in the Hold's residential ring. "But I know that the last time something reached out to us across a very long distance, it turned out to be one of the kindest things that ever happened to this whole ship. I'm choosing to hope this will be something like that too."

"Like the Choir," Mira said solemnly, the name spoken with the easy familiarity of a child who had grown up in a ship transformed, in ways she would never fully understand, by that ancient, grieving intelligence's gift of hope.

"Like the Choir," Aria agreed, and kissed her daughter's forehead, and did not tell her, because there was no need yet for a five-year-old to carry the weight of it, that the coming months would determine not merely whether the green world's people proved friendly, but whether the world itself, threatened by a boundary that had already reached its outer system, would remain a viable home for anyone at all, human or native, by the time the Meridian's Promise finally completed the long, transformed voyage that had begun, for her personally, with eleven pulses and seven pulses in the quiet hours of a single ordinary night, twenty-eight years and a lifetime ago.

The response to Aeon-Verdant's signal was transmitted three weeks later, and this time, given the vastly shorter distance now separating the ship from its destination, the reply came back within hours rather than the agonizing eighty-two minutes the Lantern's own first response had required: a rich, complex broadcast that Wren's team spent the following month painstakingly translating, piece by careful piece, and which revealed, by the time a coherent picture finally emerged, a story considerably stranger and more urgent than anything Aria had imagined during the tense, wondering weeks of the reconnaissance effort's earliest days.

The signal came from a people who called themselves, in the closest approximation Wren's translation framework could construct, the Kirenn, a species native to Aeon-Verdant's temperate archipelago regions, who had built, across many centuries of their own recorded history, a rich civilization of scattered island city-states bound together by trade, shared myth, and a deep, careful astronomical tradition that had, some four hundred years earlier by their own reckoning, received a visitation from what their oldest stories called the Sky-Elders: a patient, grieving voice, not unlike the Choir itself, that had given them the mathematical foundation for long-range signaling, and had warned them, in terms their own oral tradition had preserved with remarkable fidelity across sixteen generations, of a slow unmaking advancing from beyond their sky, a warning the Kirenn had spent four centuries preparing for in ways that Aria, reading Wren's translated account for the first time, found both deeply moving and deeply alarming.

They had been waiting, the Kirenn's message made clear, for a very long time, for exactly this moment: the arrival of strangers from beyond their sky, capable of hearing their voice, who might help them face the slow, patient danger their own Sky-Elders had warned them was already, in the Kirenn's own careful astronomical observations, beginning to touch the outer reaches of their world.
