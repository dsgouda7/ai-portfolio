Chapter 19: Preparation

The year that followed was the most intense of Kerra's life. She returned to Deepwater Crossing with evidence of Verden's plans and a deadline that hung over everything like an approaching storm. The council, presented with proof of ongoing Hollowed activity, finally took the threat seriously.

Military preparations began immediately. The joint Enclave-Holds defense force, which had existed mostly symbolically since the Hollow King's defeat, was expanded and trained specifically to counter void-weaving tactics. The academy became not just a teaching institution but a strategic asset, with students learning combat applications alongside cooperative techniques.

Kerra hated it. She'd built the academy to promote peace, and now it was being transformed into something resembling a military training camp. But the alternative was leaving everyone unprepared for Verden's assault.

"We can't abandon our principles just because someone threatens them," she argued during a tense staff meeting. "If we turn the academy into a weapons forge, Verden wins even if we defeat her. She'll have proven that power inevitably militarizes."

"Then what do you suggest?" Davin asked, frustration evident. "We have nine months until Verden's deadline. We need to be ready."

"We prepare for defense, yes. But we also double down on our core mission—proving that cooperative weaving creates better outcomes than competitive accumulation of power. We document our successes, share our methods, build alliances. When Verden comes, we meet her not with greater violence but with undeniable evidence that her philosophy is wrong."

It was idealistic, maybe naively so. But Kerra refused to abandon what made the academy valuable just because it was difficult.

They implemented a dual approach: practical combat training for those who needed it, alongside accelerated community outreach projects that demonstrated cooperative weaving's benefits. It was exhausting, managing both simultaneously, but gradually it seemed to work.

The resonant population grew as more individuals came forward. By the six-month mark, they had thirty-seven confirmed resonants connected to the academy network, plus another dozen undergoing evaluation. Each one represented a person who'd been hiding in fear and isolation, now finding community and purpose.

Kerra spent time with each new resonant personally, sharing her story and listening to theirs. Every tale of failed bindings and shameful exile hurt, but it also reinforced why this work mattered. They weren't just preparing for conflict—they were healing wounds that had been inflicted for generations.

Jarek proved invaluable, his natural empathy making him excellent at helping newly discovered resonants adjust. Senna organized support networks, creating structures so resonants could help each other rather than depending solely on the senior instructors. Toran, surprisingly, became their best combat instructor, his skepticism translating into rigorous preparation that didn't romanticize violence.

Mira, the young resonant Kerra had taken personal responsibility for, was now twelve and showing remarkable control for her age. She'd also developed a talent for teaching other children, explaining complex concepts in simple terms that adults often couldn't match.

"You've built something extraordinary," Mira told Kerra one evening as they watched students practice cooperative weavings on the training grounds. "When I first came here, I thought I was broken. Now I know I'm just... different. And different can be good."

It was exactly what Kerra had hoped to create—an environment where being different wasn't shameful but simply another way of experiencing the world.

But as the deadline approached, tension increased. Scouts reported Hollowed cells across the archipelago becoming more active. Nothing overtly hostile, but clear preparation for coordinated action. Verden was building toward something big.

Three months before the deadline, Kerra received an unexpected letter. It was from her brother Finn, now nineteen and a skilled wind-weaver serving with the Skyward Holds' aerial corps.

He was coming to visit, bringing their parents.

Kerra's first instinct was to say no—she didn't have time for family reunions with everything else happening. But Davin convinced her otherwise.

"You need grounding," he said. "Something to remind you what you're fighting for beyond abstract principles. Let them come. Even if just for a few days."

The reunion was awkward at first. Her father, still stiff with old guilt over how he'd treated her after the failed binding, struggled to meet her eyes. Her mother kept touching Kerra's arm as if confirming she was real. And Finn, now tall and confident, looked at her with a mixture of awe and big-brother protectiveness that made her want to laugh and cry simultaneously.

They walked the academy grounds together, and Kerra showed them what she'd built. Her father watched resonant students practicing multi-tide weavings and actually wept.

"I was so wrong about you," he said quietly. "I thought you were broken. I thought I'd failed as a parent. But you were extraordinary all along, and I was too blind to see it."

"I was terrified and lost," Kerra corrected. "I just got lucky enough to find people who helped me figure things out."

"No." Her father finally looked at her directly. "Luck doesn't build institutions. Luck doesn't save the world. You did that, Kerra. You and your courage."

The forgiveness she'd been seeking for seven years finally materialized, and it was as sweet and painful as she'd imagined. They talked for hours, filling in gaps, sharing stories, slowly rebuilding connections that had been strained almost to breaking.

Finn pulled her aside on the last evening of their visit. "I hear there's trouble coming. A fight."

"There's always trouble," Kerra deflected.

"Don't do that. I'm not a child anymore. I know there's a threat, and I know you're standing between it and everyone else." He gripped her shoulder. "Let me help. I'm a competent wind-weaver, I've got combat training, and I'm your brother. Use me."

Kerra wanted to refuse, to keep him safe and far from danger. But she saw the determination in his eyes and recognized it—the same stubbornness that had driven her to keep fighting when everyone said she should quit.

"All right," she said finally. "But you follow orders, you train with our instructors first, and if I tell you to run, you run. Understood?"

"Understood." Finn grinned. "Though I should warn you, I'm terrible at following orders I disagree with. Family trait, I think."

Despite everything, Kerra laughed. Having family around, being part of something bigger than herself again, reminded her of what normal life looked like. It was good to remember, especially with what was coming.

Her parents left with promises to visit again soon. Finn stayed, integrating into the academy's defense preparations. Watching him work with other students, joking and training and living fully in the moment, Kerra felt something she'd been missing: hope that there could be life after this conflict, that survival wasn't just possible but worth fighting for.

Two months before the deadline, scouts reported Hollowed forces consolidating. They were gathering in the Southern Reach, exactly as Verden had promised. The year of preparation was ending. Soon, words and plans would give way to action.

The academy held a ceremony to mark the occasion—not a celebration, but an acknowledgment of what they'd built and what they were about to risk. Kerra addressed the assembled students, instructors, and allies, trying to find words adequate to the moment.

"A year ago, we learned that our enemies weren't defeated, just delayed. That threat returns now, and we'll face it together. But remember—we're not fighting for dominance or revenge. We're fighting to prove that power can be wielded responsibly, that different people can work together toward common goals, that fear doesn't have to define how we treat those who are different." She paused. "I don't know if we'll win. But I know we're right to try. And whatever happens, I'm honored to stand with all of you."

The response wasn't cheers or rallying cries but something quieter and more powerful: nods of understanding, hands gripping in solidarity, the simple determination of people who'd found something worth defending.

When Verden came, she'd find them ready.

Not because they wanted violence, but because they'd built something too valuable to surrender without a fight.

The last month passed quickly. Final preparations, last-minute training, quiet moments stolen with friends and colleagues who might not survive what was coming. Kerra wrote letters to everyone she cared about, just in case. She also wrote a letter to herself, to be opened if she survived but found herself corrupted by power—a reminder of who she'd been and what she'd valued before the conflict.

On the final night, she stood alone on the observation deck and reached out with her tide-sense, feeling the vast ocean around them. Somewhere to the south, Verden and her forces were moving. Tomorrow they'd arrive. Tomorrow, everything would be decided.

Kerra thought about the girl she'd been at sixteen—ashamed, broken, convinced she had no value. That girl would never have believed she'd end up here, leading a community, preparing for battle, trusted by hundreds.

Transformation wasn't just possible. It was inevitable if you refused to give up.

She'd transformed from broken to capable, from isolated to connected, from shameful to proud. Now she just had to survive one more transformation: from defender to victor.

Or, failing that, from living to remembered.

Either way, she'd given everything she had. No one could ask more than that.

The stars wheeled overhead, constant and indifferent. Kerra took comfort in their permanence. Whatever happened tomorrow, the stars would still be there, still guiding sailors home.

She'd learned to navigate by them as a child. Now she'd navigate by them one more time, into the darkest waters she'd yet faced.

But she wouldn't navigate alone.

And that made all the difference.
