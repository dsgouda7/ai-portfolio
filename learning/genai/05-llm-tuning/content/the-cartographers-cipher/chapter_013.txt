Chapter 13: The Artist's Death

Six weeks after closing the Mordecai inheritance case, Elena returned to Ashmont Bay to find the city transformed. The exposure of the founding families' fraud had triggered a renaissance of historical reckoning. Museums mounted new exhibits about the Kahale family and other indigenous groups displaced by the city's founding. Plaques acknowledging Chinese and other Asian investors appeared throughout the waterfront district. The Founders' Memorial had been supplemented with new monuments recognizing those who had been erased from official histories.

It was messy, contentious, and necessary. The city was finally confronting its past honestly.

Elena's office phone rarely stopped ringing. The publicity from the Guardians conspiracy and Mordecai cases had made her the most sought-after private investigator in the region. She could pick and choose her cases now, taking only those that interested her.

So when Sophia Winters called asking for help, Elena agreed to meet immediately.

They sat in Elena's office, Sophia looking far more troubled than their last encounter.

"I need your help," Sophia said without preamble. "My best friend was murdered last night. The police are calling it a suicide, but I know it wasn't. Michelle would never kill herself."

"Tell me about Michelle."

"Michelle Torres, she was a painter, incredibly talented. She had a studio in the Maritime Arts Building downtown. Last night, the building's security guard found her dead in her studio. She'd apparently overdosed on sleeping pills and alcohol. But Michelle didn't even drink. She was sober three years, attended AA meetings regularly. She would never touch alcohol, let alone use it to commit suicide."

"What do the police say?"

"They found a suicide note on her laptop. It said she was depressed about her career failures, couldn't handle the pressure anymore, wanted to end it. But Michelle was doing better than she'd ever done. She'd just sold three paintings for significant money, was scheduled for a gallery show next month. She was happy, Sophia. Happier than I'd seen her in years."

Elena made notes. "Did Michelle have enemies? Anyone who would want her dead?"

"That's the thing. Michelle was beloved in the art community. She was kind, generous, always helping younger artists. I can't think of anyone who would want to hurt her."

"But you think someone did."

"I know someone did. And I think it's connected to a project Michelle was working on. She'd been commissioned to paint a series of historical murals for the new city hall building. Scenes from Ashmont Bay's history, showing both the official story and the hidden truths that your investigation revealed. Indigenous peoples, Chinese investors, all the people who were erased. It was important work, politically sensitive work."

"Who commissioned the murals?"

"The city council, as part of their reconciliation initiative. But not everyone was happy about it. Some people think the city is overcompensating, that we're tearing down our heritage to appease political correctness. There were protests when the commission was announced."

"Was Michelle threatened?"

"Not directly. But there were angry letters to the editor, social media attacks, that sort of thing. Michelle tried not to let it bother her, but I could tell it was affecting her."

"I'll look into it. Can I see her studio?"

"The police have released the scene. I have a key. Michelle gave it to me years ago in case of emergencies."

They drove to the Maritime Arts Building, a converted warehouse similar to Elena's office. Michelle's studio was on the third floor, a large space with north-facing windows and canvases leaning against every wall.

The studio was chaotic in the way creative spaces often are. Paint-splattered drop cloths, brushes soaking in jars, finished and half-finished works everywhere. But beneath the creative chaos was something else. Signs of a search, hastily concealed.

"Have the police been through everything?" Elena asked.

"They did a basic investigation. But they were convinced it was suicide, so they didn't look too carefully."

Elena began a methodical search, starting with Michelle's desk. She found sketches for the city hall murals, detailed drawings showing historical scenes. The Treaty negotiations, honestly depicted. The incorporation ceremony, with Chinese investors visible in the crowd. The Kahale family's displacement, shown with dignity and sorrow.

Powerful work. Politically charged work.

"Were these sketches approved by the city council?"

"Yes. Michelle presented them two weeks ago. The council approved them unanimously, though some members were clearly uncomfortable with how explicitly the murals showed historical wrongs."

Elena found Michelle's laptop and powered it on. The suicide note was still displayed on the screen, exactly as the police had found it.

"I can't continue this charade. My art is a lie, my life is a lie. I've tried to be strong, but I'm not. I'm sorry to everyone I'm disappointing. I just can't do this anymore."

The language was generic, the sentiment vague. It could have been written by anyone.

Elena checked the document's metadata. The note had been created at ten-fifteen PM the previous night. Michelle's body had been found at midnight by the security guard.

"What was Michelle's typical evening routine?"

"She usually worked in her studio until around nine or ten, then went home. She lived alone, about ten blocks from here."

"So someone could have created this note on her laptop, forced her to take pills and drink alcohol, and staged a suicide."

"That's what I think happened. But the police say there's no evidence of a struggle, no defensive wounds, nothing suggesting foul play."

Elena examined the studio more carefully. Near the sink area, she found a wine glass with residue. She photographed it carefully.

"The police would have tested this for Michelle's fingerprints and the contents."

"They did. Her prints were on it, and it contained traces of sleeping pills and red wine. That's part of why they're so sure it was suicide."

"But you said Michelle never drank."

"Never. Not for three years. Her sponsor would confirm it."

Elena bagged the glass carefully. "I'm going to have this tested independently. If Michelle was forced to drink, there might be evidence the police missed."

She continued searching and found something interesting behind one of Michelle's larger canvases. A small digital camera, the kind artists used to photograph their works in progress.

"Did Michelle usually photograph her work?"

"Always. She documented everything from initial sketches to final paintings."

Elena turned on the camera and scrolled through the images. Hundreds of photographs showing Michelle's creative process. But the most recent images, taken three days before her death, showed something unexpected.

Michelle had been painting a mural sketch, one not included in the approved series. It showed a group of men in a contemporary setting, clearly recognizable despite being rendered in Michelle's artistic style. They were standing around a table, papers spread before them, expressions serious and calculating.

"Do you recognize any of these people?" Elena showed the images to Sophia.

Sophia's face went pale. "That's James Ashmont's defense team. The lawyers who represented him during his trial for Adelaide Thorne's murder and the Guardians conspiracy. Why would Michelle paint them?"

"She wasn't just painting them. She was documenting them." Elena zoomed in on the papers visible on the table in the painting. They showed bank statements, wire transfers, offshore account numbers. "Michelle found something. Evidence of financial crimes connected to the Ashmont case. And she was painting it to preserve the information."

"But why paint it? Why not just photograph the actual documents?"

"Because photographs can be dismissed as digital fabrications. But a painting, created by hand, witnessed by others, documented in progress? That's harder to deny. Michelle was creating an indelible record of what she'd discovered."

Elena photographed every detail of the camera's images, then secured the camera itself as evidence. "Where would Michelle have seen these documents? How would she have access to James Ashmont's defense team's private papers?"

"I don't know. Michelle never mentioned anything about investigating the Ashmont case."

"But she was commissioned to paint historical murals showing the truth about the founding families. Maybe she was researching the case for historical accuracy and stumbled onto something current. Something that powerful people wanted to keep hidden."

They spent the next hour searching the studio thoroughly. Elena found more sketches, more photographs, more evidence that Michelle had been documenting something significant. But the actual source documents, the ones Michelle had been painting from, were missing.

"Someone took them," Elena said. "Whoever killed Michelle stole the documents she'd been copying. But they missed the camera."

"What do we do now?"

"We follow the evidence. We find out what Michelle discovered and who killed her to keep it quiet. And we make sure her death isn't ruled a suicide and forgotten."

Elena called Detective Chen from the studio. "I need your help with a case. A painter named Michelle Torres was found dead last night, ruled suicide. But I think she was murdered to cover up financial crimes related to the Ashmont conspiracy."

Chen arrived thirty minutes later with a crime scene team. They re-examined Michelle's studio, now understanding what they were looking for.

"The suicide ruling was premature," Chen said after reviewing the evidence. "The autopsy is scheduled for tomorrow. I'll request additional toxicology tests. If Michelle was forced to ingest the pills and alcohol, there might be evidence in her stomach contents or tissue samples."

"What about the missing documents? Michelle was clearly painting from originals. Someone took them after killing her."

"We'll check building security footage, see if anyone entered or left the building around the time of death. And we'll interview everyone who knew Michelle, find out if she mentioned the investigation to anyone."

Over the next several days, the investigation expanded. The autopsy revealed that Michelle had indeed been forced to swallow the pills. Her throat showed signs of trauma consistent with someone forcing her to ingest substances against her will. The alcohol in her system was accompanied by traces of a sedative, suggesting she'd been drugged before being forced to drink.

Building security footage showed a figure entering the Maritime Arts Building at nine-thirty PM the night of Michelle's death. They wore a hoodie and kept their face down, avoiding camera angles. They left at ten-forty-five PM, carrying a large envelope or folder.

Elena interviewed Michelle's AA sponsor, who confirmed Michelle had been sober for three years and would never voluntarily drink alcohol. She interviewed Michelle's gallery representative, who confirmed Michelle had been excited about the upcoming show and showed no signs of depression.

But it was Michelle's phone records that provided the breakthrough. Three days before her death, Michelle had called Catherine Reed's law office. The call had lasted seven minutes.

Catherine Reed was in custody awaiting trial for David Mordecai's murder. But she'd been out on bail at the time of Michelle's death.

Elena arranged to interview Catherine at the detention facility where she was now being held without bail after Michelle's murder came to light.

Catherine looked diminished, exhausted by weeks in custody. But her eyes were still sharp, still calculating.

"Miss Voss, I heard you were investigating Michelle Torres's death. I want to help."

"You killed David Mordecai. Why should I believe you want to help now?"

"Because I didn't kill Michelle. Yes, I killed David, and I'll face punishment for that. But Michelle's death is different. She called me three days before she died. She said she'd found documents showing that James Ashmont's legal defense had been funded through illegal means. Embezzled money from the Guardians conspiracy, hidden in offshore accounts, used to pay lawyers and experts to minimize his sentence."

"James is already serving life without parole. What would it matter if his defense was improperly funded?"

"It matters because some of that money came from the Kahale family's settlement. Money that was supposed to go to restitution and historical justice was diverted to protect James Ashmont. That's fraud, theft, and a dozen other crimes."

"What did you tell Michelle when she called you?"

"I told her to be careful. I told her that if she'd found evidence of embezzlement from the Kahale settlement, powerful people would want to silence her. I told her to go to the police immediately, not to confront anyone or tell anyone what she'd found."

"Did she say how she'd discovered the documents?"

"She'd been researching the Ashmont family for her historical murals. She interviewed family members, looked at family archives, traced financial records to understand how the founding wealth was maintained. In the process, she found recent documents that showed current financial crimes."

"Who would have killed her to keep that quiet?"

"Whoever benefits from the embezzlement remaining hidden. James Ashmont's lawyers, certainly, they'd face disbarment and prosecution. But also whoever else was involved in diverting the Kahale settlement funds."

Elena left the detention facility with more questions than answers. She reviewed Michelle's paintings again, studying the faces in the unfinished mural. James Ashmont's defense team: five lawyers, all from prominent Ashmont Bay firms.

She began investigating each of them. Financial records, professional histories, connections to the founding families. What she found was a web of corruption even more extensive than the original Guardians conspiracy.

The Kahale family's two-hundred-million-dollar settlement had been placed in a trust, supposedly managed for the family's benefit. But over the two months since the settlement, nearly thirty million had been embezzled through false invoices, fraudulent expense reports, and creative accounting.

Some of that money had gone to James Ashmont's defense team. But most had been diverted to offshore accounts controlled by people Elena hadn't suspected.

Dr. Rebecca Kahale's uncle, Samuel Kahale, who had confessed to being on the Guardians board, hadn't just disappeared from the city to avoid prosecution. He'd embezzled from his own family's settlement and fled.

Dr. Kahale herself hadn't been involved in the embezzlement. But when Elena contacted her to discuss the discovery, Dr. Kahale's reaction was unexpected.

"I know about the embezzlement. I've been investigating it privately for weeks. I suspected my uncle but couldn't prove it. If Michelle found documents showing the fraud, that's why she was killed. My uncle has too much to lose."

"Where is Samuel now?"

"I don't know. He disappeared six weeks ago. I've been trying to find him, trying to understand the extent of what he stole."

"Michelle found evidence. She was painting it to create a permanent record. Someone killed her and stole the documents, but I have photographs of her work in progress. That might be enough to trace the money and identify everyone involved."

"Let me help. This is my family's money that was stolen. I want everyone responsible held accountable."

Together, Elena and Dr. Kahale traced the embezzled funds through shell corporations and offshore accounts. What they found was staggering. Samuel Kahale had been embezzling from the Guardians conspiracy for years before it was exposed. When the conspiracy was revealed and the Kahale settlement was established, he'd seized the opportunity to steal even more.

But he hadn't worked alone. Two of James Ashmont's lawyers had been accomplices, helping to hide the money and receiving substantial kickbacks. And they had killed Michelle Torres when she discovered their scheme.

Elena presented the evidence to Captain Martinez, who immediately issued arrest warrants. Within days, all three suspects were in custody. Samuel Kahale was found in the Cayman Islands, attempting to access his offshore accounts. The two lawyers were arrested in Ashmont Bay, caught completely by surprise.

All three confessed quickly when presented with Michelle's paintings and the financial evidence. Samuel admitted to killing Michelle personally, drugging her, forcing her to swallow the pills and drink the wine, then staging the scene to look like suicide.

"She called me," he told investigators. "She said she'd found documents proving I'd been embezzling from my own family. She wanted to give me a chance to come clean, to return the money, to face justice with dignity. I panicked. I couldn't go to prison. So I killed her."

The trial was swift. Samuel Kahale and his lawyer accomplices were convicted of murder, embezzlement, and fraud. The remaining embezzled funds were recovered and returned to the Kahale family trust.

And Michelle Torres's murals were completed by a team of artists who worked from her sketches and paintings. The final mural included her portrait, honoring her as someone who had died seeking truth and justice.

Elena attended the mural dedication ceremony at the new city hall. Dr. Kahale gave a speech about Michelle's courage, about how one person's dedication to truth could expose corruption and change the course of justice.

"Michelle painted what she saw," Dr. Kahale said. "She created beauty from truth, even when that truth was ugly. She reminds us that art can be activism, that creativity can be courage, that a single person armed with a brush and a conscience can change the world."

Elena stood at the back of the crowd, looking at Michelle's murals covering the walls of the city hall lobby. Scenes of injustice acknowledged. Stories of erasure told. Histories hidden no longer.

It was beautiful and painful and necessary.

Just like the truth.
