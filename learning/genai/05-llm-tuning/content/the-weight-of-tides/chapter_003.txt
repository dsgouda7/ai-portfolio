Chapter 3: Town Meeting

The Willowport Community Center smelled of old coffee and institutional floor cleaner. Claire stood at the back of the crowded room, Maya beside her, watching townspeople file in for the monthly town meeting. She recognized some faces: former classmates now middle-aged, teachers who had seemed ancient when she was young and now looked merely old, shopkeepers and fishermen and the town's small professional class. And scattered among them, the newcomers. Cleanly dressed people with expensive watches and careful smiles, developers and their representatives, come to reshape Willowport in their image.

"This is where Grandpa died," Maya whispered.

Claire nodded. Jake had described it: her father at the podium, voice raised in passionate argument, face reddening with the effort. Then the collapse, the chaos, the ambulance. And through it all, the vote continuing, democracy grinding forward over one man's final protest.

Dottie appeared from the crowd, touching Claire's arm. "You came."

"Jake said I should. Said there would be another vote on the waterfront rezoning."

"There will be. They're calling it the Coastal Access and Development Initiative now. New name, same land grab." Dottie's voice was bitter. "They've learned to package it better. Jobs. Revenue. Sustainable development. All the words that make people think they're voting for progress instead of selling their souls."

The meeting began. Standard business first: budget reports, road maintenance schedules, updates on the school system's declining enrollment. Claire watched the mayor, a woman she vaguely remembered from high school, navigating parliamentary procedure with practiced ease. The audience was restless, waiting for the main event.

It came at agenda item seven: Public Comment on Proposal 2025-12, Coastal Access and Development Initiative.

The mayor read the proposal summary. Development of forty-two acres of waterfront property, currently zoned for commercial fishing and marine industry. New zoning would allow mixed-use development: condos, retail space, a boutique hotel, public boardwalks. The developers promised three hundred construction jobs, ongoing employment in hospitality and retail, increased property tax revenue. They promised careful environmental stewardship and maintained public beach access.

"Mr. Caldwell will present on behalf of Seabrook Development."

A man in an expensive suit approached the podium. Richard Caldwell, according to the agenda. He had the look of someone who had never worried about money, who moved through the world expecting doors to open. He placed a laptop on the podium, connected it to the projector, and began his presentation.

Renderings appeared on the screen: tasteful buildings designed to echo traditional Maine architecture, landscaped walkways, happy families walking dogs along a pristine waterfront. It looked lovely. It looked nothing like Willowport.

"This development represents a partnership between Seabrook Development and the town of Willowport," Caldwell said. "We're not here to destroy what makes this place special. We're here to preserve it, to create economic sustainability for future generations."

Maya snorted softly. "That's some impressive BS."

"Shh," Claire said, but she agreed.

Caldwell continued, citing economic studies, environmental impact assessments, letters of support from local business owners. He spoke about heritage tourism, about preserving maritime culture while adapting to economic reality. He was smooth, practiced, persuasive.

When he finished, the mayor opened the floor to public comment. Several people spoke in favor: the owner of the diner, who saw opportunity in tourist dollars; a young teacher, worried about job security if the school lost more students; an elderly man on a fixed income, struggling with rising property taxes.

Then Jake Landry stood.

"I've lived in Willowport my whole life," he said. "My father fished these waters, and his father before him. I understand the economic pressures. I see the young people leaving. I know change is necessary. But this proposal isn't about preserving our heritage. It's about packaging it for sale."

He pulled out documents. "I've reviewed the environmental impact assessment. It's inadequate. The surveys were done in winter when marine activity is minimal. There's no long-term study of how construction will affect fish migration patterns, no assessment of chemical runoff impacts, no consideration of sea level rise and storm surge in a changing climate. They're building on land that might be underwater in thirty years."

Murmurs rippled through the crowd. Caldwell looked annoyed.

Jake continued. "Tom Merritt spent the last five years of his life documenting environmental changes in our waters. He kept meticulous records. Before his death, he filed a formal complaint with the state environmental agency, claiming the impact assessment was insufficient. That complaint is still pending. We should wait for the state review before voting."

"Mr. Landry," the mayor said carefully, "Tom Merritt's complaint has been reviewed. The state found no grounds for further investigation."

"How thoroughly did they review? Did anyone actually look at his forty years of data?"

"The state has procedures—"

"The state has a backlog and understaffing and political pressure to approve development. Tom knew that. He was preparing a federal complaint, going to NOAA and the EPA. He died before he could file it."

Claire felt eyes turning toward her. People knew she was here. News traveled fast in small towns.

"Tom Merritt's daughter is a marine biologist," someone called out. "Maybe she should speak to this."

The mayor looked directly at Claire. "Dr. Merritt? Would you like to comment?"

Maya nudged her. "Go on. This is what Grandpa wanted."

Claire's legs felt unsteady as she walked to the front. She hadn't prepared remarks, hadn't planned to speak. But standing at the podium where her father had died, looking out at faces expectant and skeptical and desperate, she found words.

"I'm Dr. Claire Merritt. I teach marine biology at Boston University and conduct research on ecosystem changes in the Gulf of Maine. I've spent the last two days reviewing my father's documentation. While his methods were informal and his conclusions speculative, his observational data is extensive and internally consistent. He documented water temperature changes, species migration patterns, and chemical composition variations over four decades. That kind of long-term local data is actually quite rare and scientifically valuable."

She paused, choosing words carefully. "I can't speak to the specifics of this development proposal. I've only just arrived in town. But I can say that any environmental impact assessment that doesn't account for long-term ecosystem changes and accelerating climate impacts is inadequate. The Gulf of Maine is warming faster than ninety-nine percent of the world's oceans. Sea level rise projections have been revised upward repeatedly. Building permanent structures on low-lying coastal land is increasingly risky, both economically and environmentally."

Caldwell stood. "With respect, Dr. Merritt, our assessment used the most current climate models and exceeded all state requirements. We've consulted with multiple environmental engineering firms—"

"Who are you paying?" Claire interrupted. "Because my research is funded by federal grants and published in peer-reviewed journals. I have no financial stake in what happens to Willowport's waterfront. Can your consultants say the same?"

Silence. Then scattered applause, growing louder. Caldwell's expression hardened.

"Dr. Merritt, are you volunteering to conduct an independent environmental assessment?"

She hadn't been. She had been defending her father's memory, pushing back against smooth certainty that felt wrong. But now, watching Caldwell's calculating eyes, feeling the hope in the room, she heard herself say, "Yes. Give me sixty days to review the existing data, conduct additional surveys, and prepare a comprehensive report. If your assessment is adequate, my work will confirm it. If it's not, the town deserves to know before making irreversible decisions."

The mayor looked uncertain. "Dr. Merritt, I'm not sure we have authority to delay—"

"The state review of Tom Merritt's complaint is still open," Jake interjected. "I checked this afternoon. Technically, the town can't approve the rezoning until the state makes a final determination."

"That's a technicality."

"It's the law. And if the town votes now anyway, the decision can be challenged in court. Expensive for everyone. Much simpler to wait sixty days and get proper science."

Claire saw the calculation happening across the room. People wanted to believe development was the answer, but they also wanted to feel they'd been careful, thorough, responsible. Sixty days gave them cover.

The mayor called for discussion. Arguments flared, pragmatists against preservationists, old-timers against newcomers. Finally, she called a vote: postpone the rezoning decision for sixty days pending independent environmental review.

It passed by twelve votes.

Afterward, people approached Claire with a mixture of gratitude and expectation. She had inadvertently become the representative of her father's cause, the scientist who would prove or disprove decades of observation. The weight of it pressed down.

Caldwell intercepted her near the door. "Dr. Merritt. I respect your credentials, but I hope you understand the economic reality here. This town is dying. Our development could save it."

"Or speed its death. I'll let the data decide."

"Data can be interpreted many ways. I hope you'll keep an open mind."

"I hope you will too."

He smiled without warmth and left. Claire watched him go, then turned to find Maya filming something on her phone.

"Did you just record that whole exchange?"

"Evidence," Maya said. "He basically threatened you."

"He didn't threaten me."

"Mom. He told you to keep an open mind, which means he wants you to reach his preferred conclusion. That's an attempted scientific manipulation if not an outright threat." Maya pocketed her phone. "You're going to need help with this assessment. Sixty days isn't much time."

"I know." Claire felt the exhaustion of sudden responsibility. "I'll need water samples, temperature readings, biodiversity surveys. Equipment I don't have. Time I'm not sure I can spare."

"You have help," Jake said, joining them. "Half the lobster fleet will collect samples if you ask. Vernon Shaw can provide equipment. And you've got local knowledge that no outside consultant could match."

"I'm not sure I'm qualified for this."

"You're the most qualified person here. Tom knew that. Why do you think he kept all those records so carefully? He was waiting for you to come home and make sense of them."

Dottie appeared, her eyes bright with something Claire recognized as pride. "Your father would be proud of what you did tonight. Standing up to them. Demanding good science."

"I just committed to sixty days of work I'm not sure I can do."

"You can do it. You're a Merritt. We don't quit." Dottie paused. "I have something for you. Come by the house tomorrow. There are more of Tom's papers. Things he kept separate from the blue folder. Things about the family."

"What kind of things?"

"Tomorrow," Dottie said firmly. "It's a conversation that needs proper time."

They left the community center together, walking through the cool spring night. The harbor glittered below, boats rocking gently at their moorings. Somewhere out there, beneath the dark water, mysteries waited. Changes occurring too slowly to see but too important to ignore.

Claire thought about her father, obsessively documenting changes for forty years. What had driven him? Scientific curiosity? Guilt? Love for a place that was disappearing? Perhaps all of those things. Perhaps obsession was just another form of devotion.

"Are you scared?" Maya asked as they reached the car.

"Terrified."

"Good. That means you're taking it seriously." Maya smiled. "You were amazing tonight. All cool scientist logic while basically telling that developer to shove it."

"I didn't tell him to shove it."

"Subtext, Mom. You totally did. It was awesome."

They drove home through dark streets, passing houses where people who had voted tonight were going to bed, trusting or doubting or simply confused. Claire had given them sixty days. In sixty days she had to deliver answers, or at least honest uncertainty. In sixty days she had to honor her father's life work and maybe understand why he had pushed her away to protect her from something she still didn't fully grasp.

The house was dark and quiet. Claire lay in bed, staring at the ceiling, listening to old house sounds. Downstairs, her father's study waited, filled with documents and mysteries. Tomorrow she would begin the work in earnest. Tonight, she let herself feel the weight of what she had promised.

Her phone buzzed. A text from an unknown number: "Good speech tonight. Your father would be proud. But be careful. Powerful people have invested in this development. They don't like obstacles. -V. Shaw"

Claire stared at the message. A warning or a threat? She couldn't tell. She typed a response: "I only care about the science."

Shaw's reply came immediately: "That's what Tom said too. It didn't protect him."

Claire put the phone aside and closed her eyes. Sleep came slowly, filled with dreams of dark water and things moving beneath the surface, seen and unseen, known and unknowable. Her father's voice echoed through the dreams: The water's wrong. Everything's wrong. Come home. Understand. Save what matters.

She woke at dawn to sunlight on water and the sound of seagulls crying like mourners. The weight of tides had pulled her home. Now she had to discover what currents ran beneath the surface, and whether understanding them would be enough to change the course of what was coming.

In sixty days, the town would vote again. In sixty days, she would stand before them with evidence and recommendations. In sixty days, she would honor or betray her father's legacy.

Claire rose and went to his study. There was work to do, mysteries to unravel, truth to discover in four decades of careful observation. She opened the blue folder and began again, reading more carefully now, looking for patterns she had missed, connections waiting to be made.

Outside, the tide was turning. Inside, Claire Merritt was coming home to herself, to her past, to work that mattered in ways she was only beginning to understand. The ocean kept its secrets close. But she was her father's daughter. She knew how to be patient. She knew how to watch and wait and eventually, inevitably, see what the water was willing to reveal.
