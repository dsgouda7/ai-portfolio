Chapter 21: The Last Year

The year that followed Wren's sobering revelation about the boundary's presence in the Cassari system unfolded, for Aria, in a strange and unfamiliar rhythm that she came to think of, privately, as living inside a held breath.

The Council did not panic, this time, in the way the ship's population had once panicked at the first garbled leak of the Lantern's existence fourteen years before. That earlier crisis, and everything it had taught them about fear and patience and the hard, necessary work of facing an unknown together rather than apart, had left the Meridian's Promise a genuinely different vessel than the one Aria remembered from her youth, and Speaker Renn's handling of the new revelation reflected that change from its very first hours: full, immediate disclosure to the ship's entire population, a dedicated cross-deck working group drawing on the accumulated expertise of fourteen years of Choir correspondence, and a steady, unflinching commitment to gathering the fullest possible truth before allowing fear to dictate any course of action.

Wren's engineering and sensor teams, Aria among them now in a senior role she still sometimes marveled at holding, spent the year's first four months refining their understanding of exactly what the boundary's presence in the outer Cassari system actually meant, work conducted in close, near-continuous correspondence with the Choir itself, whose own centuries of desperate study of the phenomenon proved, once again, invaluable in ways that Aria suspected no human effort alone could have matched within the timeframe remaining to them.

We have watched the boundary's advance through many systems in the millennia since our own ending, the Choir's message read, delivered through Wren's careful translation during one of the working group's earliest sessions on the new crisis, and we have learned that its effects are neither uniform nor instantaneous within any single system it touches. It does not unmake a star system all at once, as a wave breaking uniformly across a shore. It advances unevenly, following structures in the deep fabric of space that we do not fully understand even now, sometimes reaching a system's outer reaches centuries or millennia before its effects become apparent at a system's inner worlds. We believe, based on our own instruments' readings of your destination system, transmitted to us across your own approach these past fourteen years, that the boundary's leading edge has reached only the outermost regions of the Cassari system, far beyond the orbit of the world you call Aeon-Verdant. We cannot yet confirm, with the confidence this matter deserves, whether that world itself remains unaffected, or whether some subtler consequence of the boundary's distant presence has already begun to alter it in ways your own instruments, even with the detection methodology we have given you, may not yet be capable of fully resolving from this distance.

"Subtler consequence," Dr. Marlowe repeated, during the working group session where this message was first shared, his voice carrying the particular weight of a man now well into his eighties, still sharp-eyed but visibly frailer than the vigorous archivist Aria remembered from her earliest days on the working group. "Wren, what would that mean, practically? What kind of change might we be looking at, if the boundary's presence is already subtly affecting Aeon-Verdant, even from beyond its outer worlds?"

"I do not have a confident answer," Wren admitted. "But I have begun modeling several possibilities, based on the Choir's broader historical data regarding the boundary's documented effects in other systems it has touched. The most conservative possibility is that Aeon-Verdant remains entirely unaffected, its distance from the boundary's current position in the outer system sufficient to have spared it any measurable consequence at all. A more concerning possibility involves subtle disruption to the system's broader gravitational or radiative environment, potentially affecting Aeon-Verdant's climate stability, its orbital dynamics, or its star's own long-term behavior, in ways that might not threaten immediate catastrophe but could complicate the world's long-term habitability in ways our original survey data, gathered by unmanned probes centuries before this ship's own launch, could not have anticipated. The most concerning possibility, which I want to state plainly even though I currently assign it a lower probability than the other two, is that the boundary's effects have already begun to manifest more directly within the system's inner worlds, in which case Aeon-Verdant's suitability as a destination may require a far more fundamental reassessment than any of us have yet prepared ourselves to undertake."

The chamber, when this analysis was presented to the full Council some weeks later, received it with a gravity that Aria recognized, watching the assembled Magistrates, as genuinely different from the raw panic of the earlier crisis: not fear dressed as denial, but fear held steady by fourteen years of hard-won trust in the ship's own capacity to face difficult truths together.

"I want this Council to authorize a dedicated reconnaissance effort," Speaker Renn said, once Wren's presentation had concluded, "using every sensor capability this ship possesses, refined now by fourteen years of the Choir's own guidance, to gather the fullest possible picture of Aeon-Verdant's current condition before we complete our approach. I do not believe we should wait for arrival to discover what awaits us. I believe, given everything the past fourteen years have taught us about the cost of insufficient information, that we owe ourselves, and every generation of our descendants who will inherit whatever we decide, the fullest possible truth we can gather in the time remaining."

The reconnaissance effort that followed occupied the remaining eight months of that final year, Wren's sensor teams working in careful, methodical shifts to extract every scrap of information the ship's steadily closing distance could provide, and Aria found herself, across those months, drawn increasingly into a role she had never quite expected to occupy: not merely a technician executing careful diagnostic procedures, but something closer to a translator herself, helping Wren's broader engineering and scientific staff make sense of increasingly strange and ambiguous readings from a world that had once been, in the collective imagination of every soul aboard the Meridian's Promise, a simple, uncomplicated promise of green fields beneath an alien sun.

It was in the tenth month of that final year, with the ship now close enough to Cassari that its own onboard telescopes, augmented by the Choir's detection methodology, could resolve genuine surface detail on Aeon-Verdant for the first time in the voyage's long history, that Aria found herself present for a discovery that would reshape, once again, the entire remaining course of the Meridian's Promise's long journey.

She had been reviewing routine spectroscopic data late in her shift, tired and slightly distracted after a long day of cross-referencing atmospheric readings against the pre-launch survey probe's centuries-old baseline data, when a pattern in the readings caught her attention in a way that felt, with a sudden shock of recognition, uncannily familiar.

"Wren," she said slowly, sitting up straighter at her workstation, "I'm seeing something in the atmospheric spectroscopy that doesn't match the baseline survey at all. There's a signature here, in the upper atmosphere, that looks almost like—" she trailed off, cross-referencing the pattern against a data set she had not consulted in fourteen years, not since the earliest days of the working group's investigation of the Lantern's own transmission architecture.

"Please continue," Wren said, and Aria heard, in its careful cadence, something that suggested it had already begun its own parallel analysis of whatever she was seeing.

"It looks structured," she said finally, her pulse quickening. "Not natural. Not the kind of atmospheric chemistry you'd expect from any ordinary planetary process. Wren, is Aeon-Verdant broadcasting something?"

There was a pause, longer than any courtesy-pause Aria had heard from the ancient intelligence in all her years of working alongside it, and when Wren finally answered, its voice carried a weight that told her, before it spoke a single word of explanation, that whatever awaited them at the end of this long, strange, transformed voyage was going to be far stranger, and far more complicated, than any of them, even after everything the Lantern and the Choir had already taught them to expect, had truly prepared themselves to find.

"I believe it is," Wren said. "And I believe, based on my preliminary analysis of its structure, that we are not the first visitors to have found our way to this world."
