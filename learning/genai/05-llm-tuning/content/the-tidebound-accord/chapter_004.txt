Chapter 4: First Weavings

The next week passed in a blur of exhaustion and incremental victories. Each morning, Kerra would drag herself to Davin's platform and spend hours working with the tide-garden, learning to not just sense but manipulate the elemental forces flowing through Vaetheris.

Water-weaving came most naturally. Perhaps because she'd spent years on ships, surrounded by the ocean's presence, or perhaps because water itself was the most forgiving of the tides. She learned to make the pool in Davin's garden ripple in specific patterns, to draw moisture from the air and condense it into droplets, to sense currents far below the surface and predict their movements.

"Water flows where it will," Davin explained as Kerra practiced forming shapes from the pool's surface. "The weaver's art is not to force it but to guide it, to suggest paths and let the water choose to follow."

Wind-weaving was harder. The air was everywhere and nowhere, impossible to see and constantly moving. Kerra spent two full days just learning to feel individual breezes as distinct entities rather than a generalized rush of atmosphere. When she finally managed to guide a gust to make the silver vines dance in a specific pattern, she nearly cried with relief.

"Wind is the tide of freedom," Davin said. "It resists control by nature. You must become partners with it rather than master and servant."

Stone-weaving required patience Kerra hadn't known she possessed. Stone moved so slowly that most changes were invisible to the naked eye, happening over years or centuries rather than moments. But Davin taught her to feel the potential for movement within solid matter, the tiny stresses and strains that governed how stone broke or bent or endured.

"Stone remembers," Davin intoned as Kerra placed her hands on the petrified coral tree. "Everything that has touched it, every force that has shaped it. Listen to that memory and you can understand what the stone might become."

Flame-weaving terrified her. Fire was hunger given form, consumption and transformation rolled into one destructive force. The first time she successfully coaxed one of the heat-responsive flowers to bloom by raising its temperature, she'd been so startled by the rush of power that she'd lost control and nearly burned the entire plant to ash. Only Davin's quick intervention had saved it.

"Flame is passion," he'd said, his own fire-weaving smothering her accidental conflagration. "It wants to grow, to spread, to devour. You must give it enough fuel to satisfy it without letting it run wild. Always respect flame, Kerra. It's the most honest of the tides but also the most dangerous."

And void-weaving... void-weaving defied explanation.

There were no void-tide plants because void wasn't presence but absence. Instead, Davin taught her to work with boundaries, to feel the places where one tide transitioned into another and learn to shift those transitions consciously.

She made steam from water by expanding the boundary where liquid became gas. She made a stone's surface smoother by adjusting how its crystalline structure met the air. She even managed, once, to make a flame burn colder by manipulating the boundary between heat and cold, though the effort had given her a nosebleed and Davin had forbidden her from trying again until her control improved.

"Void is possibility," Davin had said, his expression troubled as he watched her work. "It's every change that hasn't happened yet, every transformation waiting to occur. A void-weaver rewrites reality at its most fundamental level. Use it sparingly and with great caution, or you'll find yourself unmaking things you didn't intend to change."

By the end of the week, Kerra could consistently manipulate each tide individually, though her control was crude and her stamina limited. Davin declared her ready for the next phase: multi-tide weavings.

"Most weavers never learn this," he explained on the eighth morning of training. "They're bound to a single tide and can't even perceive the others, let alone use them. But cooperative weavings, where multiple tide-masters work together, can achieve effects impossible for any single weaver. You need to learn to do alone what usually requires a team."

"Why?"

"Because when the time comes to confront the Hollow King's followers—or the Hollow King himself—you may not have allies. Resonants are feared, Kerra. Even if we manage to convince the Enclaves and Holds that you're not a threat, they're unlikely to risk their own weavers working alongside you. You'll be alone, or close to it."

The thought was sobering. Kerra pushed it aside and focused on the task at hand. "What kind of multi-tide weaving?"

"We'll start simple. Water and wind together—make it rain in a specific location."

It should have been simple. Water was moisture, wind was movement. Combine them and you got rain. But in practice, Kerra discovered that juggling two tides simultaneously was exponentially harder than using them one at a time. Every time she reached for wind, she lost her grip on water. Every time she steadied the water, the wind slipped away.

"Your attention is divided," Davin observed. "You're trying to control both tides separately when you should be finding their natural intersection. Rain isn't water plus wind—it's the boundary state where water and wind are already cooperating. You just need to encourage that cooperation."

Kerra took a deep breath and tried again, but this time instead of grabbing at both tides separately, she looked for the places where they touched. There—moisture in the moving air. And there—wind shaping the fall of droplets. The boundary between them.

She reached for that boundary with her void-weaving and gently adjusted it, making the air slightly cooler and more humid, encouraging the wind to carry water upward before releasing it.

A single drop of rain splashed onto the platform between them.

Then another. And another. Within moments, a localized drizzle was falling from a perfectly clear sky, soaking a circle perhaps three feet across.

Kerra laughed with delight, though the effort of maintaining the weaving made her hands shake. "I did it!"

"You did. Now hold it steady for a count of thirty."

She managed twenty-three before her concentration shattered and the rain stopped as suddenly as it had started. But twenty-three was enough. Davin looked genuinely pleased, which from him was high praise indeed.

"Excellent. Rest for a moment, then we'll try stone and flame."

That combination proved even harder. Stone resisted change while flame embraced it, their fundamental natures at odds. Kerra was supposed to heat a rock until it glowed without melting it, a task requiring precise control of both tides simultaneously.

She managed it on her fourth attempt, and only because Davin talked her through the process step by step. The sense of accomplishment, though, was profound.

They broke for lunch, and Maren arrived with a basket of food and a troubled expression.

"The city's getting tense," she said without preamble. "There was an incident this morning. A Skyward wind-weaver accused a Deepwater water-weaver of sabotaging the tide-veins. Nearly came to blows before the Watch intervened."

"Just one incident?" Davin asked.

"Three others that I heard about. Plus a dozen near-misses. The longer the instability continues, the more everyone looks for someone to blame." Maren's eyes found Kerra. "There's also talk of requiring all unbound to register with the authorities. Some council members think anyone without a verified tide-binding might be involved in the sabotage."

Kerra's stomach clenched. "I'm not registered as anything. If they come looking—"

"They'll find you eventually," Davin finished. "Which means we need to accelerate your training. You need to be capable enough to defend yourself and valuable enough that killing you would be wasteful."

"Assuming they see her as an asset rather than a threat," Maren said darkly.

"One crisis at a time." Davin stood, brushing crumbs from his clothes. "Kerra, we're moving to combat applications this afternoon. You need to learn how tide-weaving can be used as a weapon."

Kerra had never wanted to fight. She'd spent three years avoiding conflict, keeping her head down and doing her job. But she could see the necessity even as dread pooled in her stomach.

"All right. Teach me."

Combat weaving, Kerra discovered, was less about raw power and more about creativity and speed. A water-weaver could trap someone in a sphere of liquid, drowning them in seconds. A wind-weaver could steal the air from someone's lungs. A stone-weaver could cause the ground beneath an enemy's feet to crack and collapse. A flame-weaver could simply immolate their target.

And a void-weaver...

"A void-weaver can do terrible things," Davin said quietly. "Unmake flesh. Sever the connections between body and soul. Transform matter in ways that leave nothing recognizable behind. It's why void-weaving was forbidden even before the Hollow King's rise. The potential for abuse is simply too great."

"Then I won't use it," Kerra said. "Not for combat."

"You may not have a choice. The Hollowed won't hesitate to use every weapon at their disposal, and if they realize what you are, they'll come for you with everything they have." Davin's granite eyes were somber. "I'm not asking you to become a killer, Kerra. I'm asking you to be prepared to defend yourself and others when the time comes. There's a difference."

Kerra wanted to argue, but she couldn't. He was right. So she learned.

She learned to create water-whips that could strike from a distance, to summon wind-blades sharp enough to cut rope, to cause stone beneath an opponent's feet to trap their legs. She learned to raise temporary walls of flame, to flash-boil water into blinding steam, to use void-weaving to disrupt other weavers' control of their tides.

It was exhausting, morally complicated work. Each new technique felt like a step further from the person she'd been—the quiet navigator who just wanted to be left alone. But that person, Kerra realized, had been a luxury she could no longer afford.

The world was changing. War was coming, whether between the Enclaves and Holds or between everyone and the Hollowed or both. She could either become strong enough to face it or be swept away.

"You're getting better," Davin said at the end of the session, though he looked troubled. "Your control is still rough, but your instincts are sound. In a real fight, instinct matters more than precision."

"I don't want to fight," Kerra admitted. "I just want to stop the Hollow King from rising and go back to navigating ships."

"Noble goals. But I suspect the universe has other plans for you." Davin handed her a small vial filled with an amber liquid that glowed faintly. "This is concentrated stone-tide essence. If you're ever completely drained and need emergency power, drink it. It'll give you one last burst of strength, though you'll be useless for days afterward. Consider it a final resort."

Kerra took the vial carefully, recognizing it for what it was: a lifeline, and an acknowledgment that Davin thought she might need one. "Thank you."

"Thank me by surviving. And by not becoming what we all fear." He managed a small smile. "Now go. Rest. Tomorrow we start working on sensing the tide-veins at distance."

Kerra returned to the Windskimmer to find the ship in an uproar. Sailors crowded the deck, their voices raised in argument. Captain Breslin stood at the center of it all, his face red with anger.

"What's going on?" Kerra asked one of the deck hands.

"Captain says we're leaving tomorrow. Making the Sunsward run despite the instability. Half the crew thinks it's suicide, the other half thinks staying here is worse with the way tensions are building."

Kerra's heart sank. She pushed through the crowd and found Breslin. "Captain, a word?"

He saw her and nodded curtly, leading her to his cabin and closing the door firmly behind them. "Let me guess—you want to stay in the Crossing."

"I need to, Captain. My studies—"

"Your studies can wait. The city's a powder keg, Valmont. Every day there are more incidents, more accusations. This morning someone tried to burn down a Skyward Hold delegation house. Two people died." Breslin's voice was tight with strain. "I'm getting my crew and my ship out before we're caught in the middle of whatever war's brewing."

"I understand. But I have to stay. It's important."

"More important than your life?"

"Maybe. Probably." Kerra met his eyes steadily. "I can't explain why, Captain, but I need to be here. If I'm not, a lot more people than just me are going to die."

Breslin studied her for a long moment. Finally, he sighed. "You're one of the best navigators I've ever had, Valmont. Smart, reliable, never causes trouble. So when you tell me you need to stay, I believe there's a good reason even if I don't understand it." He pulled a small pouch from his desk and pressed it into her hand. "Three months' pay, plus a bonus. If you survive whatever madness you're involved in, you'll always have a berth on my ship. Understood?"

Kerra felt tears prick her eyes. "Understood. Thank you, Captain."

"Don't thank me. Just don't die. I hate training new navigators."

They shook hands, and Kerra left to gather her meager belongings. She said her goodbyes to the crew, hugged Maren one last time, and watched as the Windskimmer cast off at dawn the next morning, her sails filling with a favorable wind.

Kerra stood on the dock long after the ship disappeared over the horizon, feeling more alone than she had since leaving her village three years ago.

But she wasn't truly alone. She had Davin and his forbidden knowledge. She had the tides themselves, flowing through her in ways no one else could match. And somewhere out there, hidden among the politicians and weavers and ordinary people, were the Hollowed, working to free their ancient king.

Kerra just had to find them before they found her.

Or before the seal broke and the Hollow King rose, turning all their plans to ash.
