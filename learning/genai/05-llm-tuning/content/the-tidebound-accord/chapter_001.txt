Chapter 1: The Unbound

Kerra Valmont stood at the bow of the merchant vessel Windskimmer, salt spray stinging her face as she adjusted the brass sextant against the morning sun. The instrument was older than she was, its surface worn smooth by decades of hands before hers, and it had never once lied to her. Unlike people. Unlike the tide-binding ceremony that had marked her as broken three years ago.

The sea rolled beneath the ship in long, lazy swells, deceptively calm. Kerra knew better. She'd learned to read the water the way others read the tides themselves, watching for the subtle shifts in color and current that warned of reefs, riptides, or worse. This morning, the eastern horizon shimmered with an odd greenish cast that made her neck prickle with unease.

"Valmont!" Captain Breslin's voice cut through the wind. "What's our bearing?"

Kerra lowered the sextant and made her way aft, her bare feet finding purchase on the salt-slick deck with practiced ease. The Windskimmer was a three-masted schooner built for speed rather than cargo capacity, and every sailor aboard could feel her respond to the slightest shift in wind or weight. Kerra had crewed on her for eighteen months now, ever since Breslin had taken pity on an unbound girl with nowhere else to go.

"Seventeen degrees north of the Sunsward line, Captain," Kerra reported. "Current's running fast today. Faster than it should be."

Breslin frowned, his weathered face creasing into deeper lines. He was Shoreborn through and through, his family's clan-marks tattooed in blue ink along both forearms in the traditional spiral pattern. "Show me."

Kerra pulled her navigation journal from her belt pouch and flipped to the chart she'd been updating since dawn. "See here? Yesterday the Mistral Current was running at maybe four knots. This morning it's closer to seven. And the water temperature's dropped three degrees."

"Tide-vein flux," Breslin muttered, rubbing his grizzled jaw. "Been happening more and more lately. My cousin in Portwatch says his water-weaver daughter can barely feel the tide anymore. Says it's like trying to grab smoke."

Kerra said nothing. What could she say? She wasn't a tide-weaver. She'd failed her binding at sixteen, standing in the sacred circle on Moonwatch Isle while her entire village watched, and when the ceremony-master had called upon the tides to claim her, nothing had answered. Not water, not wind, not even the smallest flicker of stone or flame. The void-tide was forbidden, never spoken of except in whispers about madness and corruption, so no one had even considered that possibility.

She'd been unbound. Tideblind. Empty.

The shame had been crushing. Her father, a respected wind-weaver who'd served the Skyward Holds for thirty years, had barely looked at her afterward. Her mother had tried to hide her disappointment behind tight smiles and false reassurances, but Kerra had seen the truth in her eyes. Her younger brother Finn, only twelve at the time, had been the only one who'd still talked to her like she was human.

So Kerra had left. She'd walked to the nearest port with nothing but her clothes and her father's old sextant—a gift from her grandfather, meant for her brother but given to Kerra in a moment of what her father probably considered misplaced sentimentality. She'd talked her way onto a fishing boat, then a cargo hauler, then the Windskimmer. Navigation didn't require tide-weaving. It required mathematics, careful observation, and an intimate understanding of the sea. All things Kerra possessed in abundance.

"Valmont!" Breslin's sharp tone snapped her back to the present. "Did you hear me?"

"Sorry, Captain. No, I didn't."

"I said, plot us a course to Deepwater Crossing. If the tides are acting up, I want to talk to the Enclave-masters before we make the Sunsward run."

Kerra's stomach tightened. Deepwater Crossing. The only place where Shoreborn, Deepwater, and Skyward territories met, a neutral city built on floating platforms above one of the deepest tide-veins in the known world. She'd been avoiding it for three years, terrified of running into anyone from her old life.

But orders were orders.

"Aye, Captain. Crossing is four days southwest at current speed. We'll need to tack into the wind once we round the Saltstone Cliffs."

"Make it so."

Breslin turned away, already barking orders at the crew to adjust the sails. Kerra returned to her charts, trying to ignore the knot of anxiety in her chest. Four days. Then Deepwater Crossing, with its tide-weavers and diplomats and all the people she'd spent three years trying to forget.

The wind picked up as morning stretched into afternoon, and Kerra stayed busy with her navigation duties, taking readings every hour and adjusting their course as needed. The Windskimmer was undermanned for a vessel her size—Breslin ran a lean operation, hiring only sailors who could pull triple duty—which meant Kerra also helped with the rigging when needed. She'd just finished securing a loose halyard when she noticed young Tam, the ship's cook's apprentice, staring over the port rail with an expression of pure terror.

"Tam? What's wrong?"

The boy, barely fourteen, pointed with a shaking hand. "Miss Kerra, what is that?"

Kerra followed his gaze and felt her blood run cold.

The water was moving. Not with the normal swell of waves or the predictable pattern of a current, but with a writhing, serpentine motion that looked distinctly wrong. The greenish cast she'd noticed at dawn was stronger here, concentrated into a roiling patch of ocean about fifty yards across. And rising from the center of it, barely breaking the surface, was something dark and enormous.

"Captain!" Kerra's shout came out sharper than she'd intended, but Breslin was already moving, his hand dropping to the water-charm at his belt.

Every Shoreborn sailor carried such a charm, even the unbound. They were blessed by water-weavers and supposedly offered protection against sea-monsters and angry spirits. Kerra had never put much faith in hers, but she found herself touching it now anyway.

The dark shape in the water resolved as it rose higher: a massive stone pillar, easily ten feet in diameter, carved with intricate spiral patterns that seemed to writhe and shift even as Kerra watched. It was covered in barnacles and coral growth, clearly ancient, clearly not supposed to be surfacing in the middle of open ocean.

"What in the tides?" Breslin whispered.

The pillar continued to rise, water streaming off its surface in sheets. It rose twenty feet, thirty, forty, until it towered above the Windskimmer's mainmast. The spiral carvings began to glow with a sickly green light that made Kerra's eyes water.

And then she felt it.

A pull. Not physical, but deeper, like something was tugging at her very soul. It started as a whisper at the edge of her awareness and grew rapidly into a roar that threatened to overwhelm her entirely. For one dizzying moment, she could feel everything: the water surging around the pillar, the wind filling their sails, the stone of the pillar itself resonating like a struck bell, even the distant heat of the sun above and the vast, cold emptiness of the void between the stars.

All five tides. All at once.

Kerra gasped and stumbled backward, nearly falling. Tam caught her arm, his young face pale with fear. "Miss Kerra?"

"I'm fine," she lied, though she was very much not fine. The sensations faded as quickly as they'd come, leaving her dizzy and nauseated. Around her, the crew was shouting, running to their stations as Breslin bellowed orders to bring the ship about and flee.

But Kerra couldn't stop staring at the pillar. At the glowing symbols that seemed to burn themselves into her vision. At the way the water around it churned with power that made every hair on her body stand on end.

Deep beneath the waves, something ancient was waking. And somehow, impossibly, Kerra could feel it calling to her.

The Windskimmer turned hard to starboard, her timbers groaning with the strain. The sails snapped taut as the crew worked with frantic efficiency, every person aboard knowing that whatever was happening, they wanted no part of it. Kerra forced herself to move, to help with the rigging, to be useful even as her mind reeled with impossible sensations.

By the time she looked back, the pillar was sinking again, sliding beneath the surface with the slow inevitability of a closing door. The green glow faded. The churning water calmed. Within minutes, there was nothing to mark where it had been except a slowly dissipating patch of disturbed sea.

But Kerra could still feel it. A thrumming in her bones, a whisper at the back of her mind. The tides hadn't answered her during the binding ceremony because she didn't belong to just one of them.

She belonged to all of them.

And that, Kerra knew with sick certainty, meant she was either the most powerful tide-weaver in generations or an abomination that should never have been born.

Possibly both.

That night, after the crew had settled into an uneasy watch and the immediate panic had faded into nervous speculation, Kerra stood alone at the stern rail and tried to make sense of what had happened. She'd carefully avoided telling anyone about the sensations she'd felt. Bad enough to be unbound without adding claims of impossible tide-sensitivity to her reputation.

The stars wheeled overhead in their familiar patterns, and Kerra found comfort in their predictability. Navigation by stars was honest work. The celestial bodies didn't care if you were bound or unbound, blessed or cursed. They simply were, and if you knew how to read them, they'd guide you true.

If only the rest of life were so simple.

"Troubled thoughts make for poor sleep, girl."

Kerra spun, her hand instinctively reaching for the belaying pin at her belt, but it was only Maren, the ship's oldest crewmember and the closest thing the Windskimmer had to a tide-weaver. She was ancient, her hair white as seafoam, her clan-marks faded with age. She'd been a stone-weaver once, back in her youth, but she rarely spoke of those days.

"Just watching the stars," Kerra said carefully.

"Mm." Maren moved to stand beside her, her joints creaking audibly with each step. "And trying not to think about that pillar, no doubt. Or the way it looked at you."

Kerra's heart skipped. "What do you mean?"

"I may be old, girl, but I'm not blind." Maren's dark eyes glinted in the starlight. "I saw your face when that thing rose. You felt something, didn't you? Something the rest of us missed."

There was no point in lying. Maren had a way of seeing through falsehoods that had nothing to do with tide-weaving and everything to do with having lived too many years to be fooled by anything.

"I felt... everything," Kerra admitted quietly. "All the tides at once. Just for a moment."

Maren was silent for a long time. When she finally spoke, her voice was troubled. "Do you know why the void-tide is forbidden, child?"

"Because it drives weavers mad. Because it corrupts."

"That's the story we tell children. The truth is more complicated." Maren sighed. "The void-tide isn't corruption. It's connection. It's the space between the other tides, the emptiness that lets them flow and mix and change. A true void-weaver doesn't control emptiness. They control the boundaries. The spaces where water becomes wind, where stone becomes flame. They're the bridges, you see."

"I'm not a void-weaver," Kerra said. "I'm not any kind of weaver. I failed my binding."

"Or your binding failed you." Maren turned to face her fully, and in the starlight, she looked less like a frail old woman and more like something carved from ancient stone. "What you felt today, girl—that wasn't normal. That wasn't even void-tide weaving, not really. That was something older. Something from before the tides were divided into neat little categories that the Enclaves and Holds could control."

"I don't understand."

"Neither do I. Not fully." Maren's hand, gnarled with age and scarred from a lifetime of labor, gripped Kerra's shoulder with surprising strength. "But I know danger when I feel it, and that pillar rising? That's the start of something bad. Something that's been sleeping a very long time and probably should have stayed that way."

"The Hollow King," Kerra whispered, not knowing where the words came from. They simply rose from that same deep place that had sensed the tides around the pillar, certain and terrible.

Maren's eyes widened. "Where did you hear that name?"

"I didn't hear it. I just... knew it. Like I know the stars or the shape of the waves." Kerra's hands were shaking. "What is it? What's the Hollow King?"

"A story. A nightmare. A warning from the oldest days that most people think is just a legend." Maren released Kerra's shoulder and turned back to the sea, her expression grim. "They say that before the tides were divided, before the first weavers learned to bind themselves to single elements, there was a weaver who tried to control all of them at once. He succeeded, for a time. Became more powerful than anyone before or since. But the power consumed him, ate away at everything human until only hunger remained. The Hollow King, they called him. The void that devours."

"How was he stopped?"

"He wasn't. Not really. The first tide-weavers, working together, managed to seal him away in the deepest place they could find. The Abyssal Rift, where all the tide-veins converge and the ocean floor drops away into crushing darkness. They bound him with wards and chains of living stone and swore that no one would ever weave all five tides again, lest they risk becoming what he had become."

Kerra's stomach churned. "So the binding ceremony—"

"Was designed to prevent exactly what you might be," Maren finished. "Someone who can touch all the tides. Someone who could either seal the Hollow King forever or become him."

The weight of those words settled over Kerra like a shroud. She wanted to deny it, to insist that Maren was wrong, that she was just unbound and nothing more. But she couldn't forget the sensation of the pillar calling to her, or the certainty with which she'd known the name Hollow King.

"What do I do?" she asked quietly.

"For now? Nothing. Say nothing to anyone, least of all the Captain. We get to Deepwater Crossing, we report what we saw, and we let the Enclave-masters and Hold-generals sort it out." Maren paused. "But if I were you, girl, I'd be ready to run. Because once the powers-that-be figure out what you are, they'll either try to use you or kill you. Maybe both."

With that cheerful pronouncement, Maren shuffled back toward her hammock, leaving Kerra alone with the stars and her spiraling thoughts.

Sleep did not come easily that night.
