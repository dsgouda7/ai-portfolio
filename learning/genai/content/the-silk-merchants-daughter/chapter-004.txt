Chapter 4: The Household Balance

Three months into her marriage, Wei Lian had established a precarious balance in the Zhang household. The mornings belonged to Lady Zhang and domestic duties: supervising servants, reviewing accounts, managing supplies, and performing the endless rituals required of a daughter-in-law in a respectable family. The afternoons, after Lady Zhang retired for her midday rest, belonged to Wei Lian herself.

It was during these afternoon hours that she felt most like herself. Zhang Ming had kept his promise about valuing learning. He had shown her to the family library, a modest but well-curated collection of classical texts, histories, and poetry. "My father spent his lifetime building this collection," Zhang Ming had explained. "He believed that a house without books was like a body without a soul."

Today, Wei Lian sat in the library copying a passage from the Daodejing, the ancient text of Daoist philosophy. The calligraphy brushes Zhang Ming had given her moved across the rice paper with fluid precision. Outside the window, autumn was advancing, the compound's maple trees turning brilliant shades of red and gold.

"Still practicing your characters?" Zhang Ming's voice came from the doorway. He had arrived home early from the Ministry of Rites, still wearing his official robes.

Wei Lian set down her brush. "The afternoon is mine to use as I wish, is it not?"

"Of course. I did not mean to criticize." He entered and examined her work. "Your calligraphy is exceptional. Better than mine, if I am honest."

"You are too modest."

"No, I am observant. Look at the balance in these characters, the confidence of the strokes. This is the work of someone who has practiced for years." He paused. "Have you considered that you could sell work like this? Wealthy merchants pay well for fine calligraphy to display in their homes."

Wei Lian looked at him in surprise. "Sell my calligraphy? That would be... unusual."

"Unusual but not improper. Many wives contribute to household income through various skills. My mother, for instance, manages rental properties that her father left her. The income is substantial."

"I did not know that."

Zhang Ming smiled wryly. "My mother is full of surprises. She presents herself as a traditional matron, but beneath that exterior is a woman who understands finance as well as any merchant. Where do you think I learned to value educated women?"

Wei Lian considered the possibility. The independent income her father had settled on her provided some autonomy, but it was modest. Additional income from her own work would be meaningful. "I will think about it."

"Do. And speaking of income and household matters, I wanted to discuss something with you." Zhang Ming's expression turned more serious. "I have been offered an opportunity. One of the senior officials at the Ministry is organizing a delegation to travel along the Silk Road, inspecting the tribute system and assessing trade conditions. He has invited me to join as a junior official."

Wei Lian's heart leaped with unexpected excitement. "The Silk Road? For how long?"

"Six months, perhaps longer. We would travel as far as the western regions, possibly to Samarkand." He paused. "Traditionally, I would not take you. Official delegations do not usually include wives."

Wei Lian felt the excitement drain away as quickly as it had come. "I understand."

"However," Zhang Ming continued, "this is not a military expedition or a formal embassy. It is more of an extended inspection tour. Some of the other officials are bringing their families. And I thought... you grew up hearing your father's stories of the Silk Road. Would you want to come?"

For a moment, Wei Lian could not speak. The possibility seemed too enormous, too unexpected. "Your mother would never approve."

"My mother does not control every decision I make. I am asking you, Wei Lian. Would you want to see the western regions?"

"Yes," she breathed. "More than almost anything."

Zhang Ming grinned, and Wei Lian realized it was the first time she had seen him truly smile, not just the polite expressions of courtesy but genuine, unguarded pleasure. "Good. Then we will go. It will be difficult travel, uncomfortable at times. The desert is harsh, and the mountain passes are treacherous. But you will see things few people from the capital ever witness."

"When would we leave?"

"In two months, at the start of spring. That gives us time to prepare and, more importantly, time to convince my mother that this is a reasonable plan."

Convincing Lady Zhang proved to be as difficult as anticipated. When Zhang Ming informed her of the plan that evening over dinner, her reaction was immediate and vehement.

"Absolutely not. It is improper for a respectable woman to travel such distances, exposed to all manner of rough company. What will people say?"

"They will say that my wife accompanies me on official business, as is her right," Zhang Ming replied calmly.

"You have been married only three months. Wei Lian should be focused on establishing herself in this household, not gallivanting across the empire."

"Mother, Wei Lian has established herself admirably. The household runs smoothly, the servants respect her, and she has fulfilled every duty required of her. Six months on an official delegation will not change that."

Lady Zhang turned her sharp gaze on Wei Lian. "And you want this? To leave a comfortable home for the hardships of the road?"

Wei Lian chose her words carefully. "Honorable mother, I am grateful for your guidance and the home you have welcomed me into. But I also value learning and experience. My father traveled the Silk Road in his youth, and his stories have always fascinated me. If my husband believes I can contribute to the journey without dishonoring the family, I would welcome the opportunity."

"Contribute? What possible contribution could you make to an official delegation?"

Zhang Ming intervened. "Wei Lian's father is one of the most successful silk merchants in Chang'an. She grew up learning about trade routes, quality assessment, and merchant negotiations. That knowledge could be valuable as we inspect the tribute and trade systems."

Lady Zhang fell silent, her expression thoughtful. Wei Lian recognized the look: it was the face of a woman calculating advantages and disadvantages, assessing risks and benefits. Finally, she spoke.

"If you insist on this plan, there are conditions. First, Wei Lian will maintain all proper proprieties during travel. No behavior that could bring shame to this family. Second, you will write regular letters keeping me informed of your progress. Third, before you depart, Wei Lian will ensure that all household accounts are in perfect order and that proper arrangements are made for the servants during your absence."

"All reasonable conditions," Zhang Ming agreed.

"And one more thing." Lady Zhang fixed Wei Lian with an intense stare. "If you are with child before the departure date, you will not go. I will not have a pregnant daughter-in-law risking herself and my potential grandchild on such a journey."

Wei Lian felt her cheeks warm but kept her voice steady. "Understood, honorable mother."

After Lady Zhang departed, Zhang Ming let out a long breath. "That went better than I expected."

"You expected worse?"

"I expected her to forbid it outright. Instead, she merely set conditions. That is practically enthusiastic approval by her standards."

Wei Lian laughed softly. "Your mother is formidable."

"She is. But she is also practical. She recognized the value in your merchant knowledge, even if she would never admit it directly." He paused. "Wei Lian, I want you to know... I suggested this trip partly for selfish reasons. I think we will work well together on the road, away from the constraints of household hierarchy. But I also hoped it would please you."

"It does please me. More than I can adequately express."

"Good. Then we have two months to prepare. And you will need to write to your father. He will want to know about this journey, and he may have advice about the routes and the merchants we will encounter."

That night, Wei Lian sat at her writing desk with the fine brushes Zhang Ming had given her and composed a letter to her father. The characters flowed easily, her excitement evident in every stroke.

"Honored Father, I write with news that will surprise and perhaps please you. Zhang Ming has been assigned to join an official delegation traveling the Silk Road, and he has invited me to accompany him. We will depart in the spring and travel as far as the western regions, inspecting trade conditions and tribute systems..."

She described the plans in detail, then asked for his counsel. What should she know about the road? Which merchants could be trusted? What dangers should they anticipate?

The reply came within a week. Wei Chen's pleasure was evident even in the formal language of the letter.

"Daughter, your news fills this old merchant's heart with joy. The Silk Road shaped my youth and taught me lessons no academy could provide. That you will have this opportunity is fitting. Here is my advice..."

The letter contained several pages of detailed guidance: which oasis cities had the most honest merchants, which caravanserais offered the best lodging, which mountain passes were most treacherous in spring. He also included a sealed letter of introduction to several merchant contacts along the route, men who had done business with the Wei family for decades.

"These men will help you if you need assistance," Wei Chen wrote. "The Silk Road has its own code of honor among those who travel it regularly. Show them this letter, and they will treat you as family."

Wei Lian treasured both the practical advice and the implicit approval in her father's words. She had feared he might think the journey inappropriate for a married woman. Instead, he seemed to view it as a continuation of her education.

The next two months passed in a blur of preparation. Wei Lian worked with Lady Zhang to ensure the household would run smoothly in their absence, training the senior servants in additional responsibilities and organizing the accounts with meticulous care. She also began assembling the supplies they would need for travel: sturdy clothing, practical shoes, medicines, and dried foods that would keep for the long journey.

Zhang Ming, meanwhile, attended countless meetings at the Ministry, learning the details of their mission and the officials he would be working with. The delegation leader was a man named Lord Sun, a senior official with decades of experience in frontier administration. He was known for his competence and his lack of patience for incompetence in others.

"Lord Sun questioned me for an hour about why I was bringing my wife," Zhang Ming reported one evening. "I explained your background in the silk trade. He seemed skeptical at first but eventually agreed that merchant knowledge might prove valuable."

"And what did he say when you told him I was actually knowledgeable?" Wei Lian asked with a slight smile.

"He said, 'Let us hope your wife is as useful as you claim, Zhang. Otherwise she is merely additional baggage.'" Zhang Ming's expression darkened. "I nearly took offense, but then I realized it was a test. He wanted to see if I would defend you or be cowed by his authority."

"And what did you do?"

"I said, 'My wife is many things, Lord Sun, but she has never been baggage.'" Zhang Ming smiled at the memory. "He laughed and told me I had more spine than he expected from a junior official. I think we may get along."

As winter deepened and then began to loosen its grip, Wei Lian felt herself changing. The months of marriage had taught her to navigate the complexities of the Zhang household, to balance Lady Zhang's demands with her own needs, to be both a proper wife and her own person. And now, the prospect of the journey ahead filled her with an anticipation she had not felt since childhood.

She continued her calligraphy practice, but now she also spent hours reading her father's old journals from his travels, studying maps of the western regions, and learning what she could about the peoples and places they would encounter. The Sogdian merchants who dominated much of the Silk Road trade, the oasis kingdoms of the Tarim Basin, the great cities like Kashgar and Samarkand that existed at the crossroads of civilizations.

One afternoon, as she studied a particularly detailed map of the route, Lady Zhang entered the library unexpectedly. Wei Lian rose and bowed, but Lady Zhang waved her back to her seat.

"Show me what you are studying."

Wei Lian explained the map, tracing the route they would follow westward from Chang'an through Lanzhou and along the Hexi Corridor, then through the jade Gate and into the desert oases beyond.

Lady Zhang listened in silence, her expression unreadable. Finally, she spoke. "My husband traveled this route once, many years before he died. He used to tell me stories about it, especially in his final years when he could no longer travel. He said the desert had a beauty all its own, harsh but magnificent."

"I did not know he had traveled the Silk Road."

"There is much about this family you do not yet know. My husband was a scholar, yes, but in his youth he was posted to Dunhuang, at the very edge of the empire. He spent five years there, learning about the frontier peoples and the trade that flows along the Silk Road. It shaped him, gave him perspectives most scholars lack."

Lady Zhang moved to the window, looking out at the garden where winter was slowly giving way to spring. "When Zhang Ming told me about this delegation, my first instinct was to forbid it. Not because I doubted your capabilities, but because I feared losing my son. The journey is dangerous. Bandits, disease, accidents in the mountains. But then I thought of my husband and how those years on the frontier were perhaps the most meaningful of his life. And I realized I was being selfish."

She turned back to Wei Lian. "You have proven yourself a capable daughter-in-law. The household is well-managed, you have been respectful, and you have made my son happy. That last matters more than you perhaps realize. Zhang Ming has always been serious, scholarly, somewhat isolated. Since marrying you, he smiles more. He seems more alive. So I will not stand in the way of this journey."

"Thank you, honorable mother."

"But hear this, Wei Lian. If anything happens to my son because of this trip, I will hold you responsible. And I promise you, my anger is not something you would wish to experience."

"I understand."

Lady Zhang's stern expression softened slightly. "Good. Now show me more of this map. If you are to represent this family in the western regions, you should understand the history and politics of the places you will visit."

And so, in an unexpected turn, Lady Zhang became Wei Lian's teacher, sharing knowledge gleaned from her late husband's experiences and her own considerable reading. The stern matron revealed herself to be deeply learned, with a strategic mind that understood the complex web of alliances and conflicts that characterized the frontier regions.

As the departure date approached, Wei Lian felt increasingly grateful for this unexpected gift. She was learning not just facts about the Silk Road but also seeing a different side of her mother-in-law, one that Lady Zhang rarely showed. Beneath the strict exterior was a woman of intelligence and depth, someone who had spent decades navigating the constraints placed on women in their society while finding ways to exercise power and influence.

On the night before their departure, the Zhang family gathered for a formal farewell dinner. Lady Zhang presided over the meal with her usual authority, but there was an undercurrent of emotion beneath her composed facade. After the meal, she presented Wei Lian with a small package.

"This was my husband's. He carried it throughout his years in Dunhuang. I want you to have it now."

Wei Lian unwrapped the package to find a compass, beautifully crafted with a jade casing and fine bronze needle. On the back was inscribed a line from the Daodejing: "The journey of a thousand miles begins with a single step."

"I will treasure this," Wei Lian said softly.

"See that you do. And see that you return safely with my son. Both of you."

As Wei Lian lay beside Zhang Ming that night, their last night in the comfortable bed they had shared for five months, she thought about the journey ahead. Tomorrow they would begin traveling the Silk Road, following ancient paths that had carried silk and spices, ideas and religions across the vast distances of Asia. It was the opportunity she had dreamed of, the adventure that had seemed impossible when she was simply the silk merchant's daughter awaiting marriage.

"Are you nervous?" Zhang Ming's voice came quietly through the darkness.

"Yes. And excited. And grateful."

"As am I. We are fortunate, Wei Lian. Not many couples in our position would have this opportunity."

"I know." She reached out and found his hand in the darkness. "Whatever comes, I am glad we will face it together."

"As am I, wife. As am I."

They drifted into sleep holding hands, the compass resting on the table beside the bed, ready to guide them on the journey that would begin at dawn. The first steps of a thousand-mile journey, into an unknown that held both danger and possibility.

The door to Wei Lian's old life in Chang'an was closing. But the road ahead was opening, stretching westward toward horizons she had only dreamed of seeing. And for the first time since her marriage, Wei Lian felt not constraint but possibility, not duty but adventure.

Tomorrow, the journey would begin. Tonight, she slept peacefully beside her husband, her grandmother's jade pendant and her father-in-law's compass both close at hand, symbols of the past guiding her toward an uncertain but thrilling future.
