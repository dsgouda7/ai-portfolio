Chapter 15: Teaching and Learning

Jarek's story emerged slowly over the next days as the Stormrunner continued its voyage. He'd been born on the mainland, in a small fishing village in Enclave territory. His binding ceremony had failed just like Kerra's, marking him as unbound and shameful.

But unlike Kerra, who'd fled to find her own way, Jarek had been exiled. His village, superstitious and fearful, had put him on a boat with minimal supplies and set him adrift, believing he was cursed. He'd drifted for weeks before washing up on that uninhabited island, surviving through desperation and the accidental discovery of his resonant abilities.

"I thought I was going mad at first," he admitted as they sat together on the deck during his first training session. "Feeling all the tides at once, not knowing how to separate them or control any of it. I'd have moments where I'd accidentally set things on fire or cause the ground to shake. I learned to isolate myself so I couldn't hurt anyone."

"You weren't going mad," Kerra said firmly. "You were manifesting without training or guidance. It's terrifying, but it's not madness. And now you'll learn to control it properly."

She taught him the same basic exercises Davin had taught her—isolating individual tides, learning to focus on one while keeping the others in background awareness, building mental barriers that could be lowered or raised as needed. Jarek struggled at first, his control ragged from two years of survival-driven improvisation. But he was motivated and intelligent, and he made rapid progress.

"Why did the ceremony fail for us?" Jarek asked one evening. "Why are some people resonants while others bind normally?"

"No one knows for certain. Davin thinks it's random genetic variation, maybe one in ten thousand births. The binding ceremony was designed to force single-tide alignment, but resonants resist that forcing because our natures are inherently multi-elemental."

"So we're not broken. The ceremony is."

"Essentially. Though don't expect the mainstream weaving community to admit that anytime soon. Change comes slowly to established traditions."

Lysse adjusted their route to allow more time for Jarek's training. They stopped at islands for supplies but didn't linger, keeping to a schedule that balanced exploration with education. The crew adapted quickly to having two resonants aboard, though Kerra noticed some nervous glances when she and Jarek practiced combat weavings together.

"They're afraid of you both," Oskar confided to Kerra one afternoon. "Not hostile afraid, just... aware that you could unmake the ship without effort if you chose to."

"We'd never—"

"I know that. They probably know that. But fear isn't always rational. You're powerful in ways normal weavers can't match. That creates distance."

It was frustrating but understandable. Kerra had faced similar reactions at the academy, though familiarity had eventually bred comfort. Maybe that was the solution—normalization through exposure. The more people saw resonants behaving responsibly, using power carefully and cooperatively, the less frightening they'd become.

Assuming enough resonants could be found and trained to create that exposure.

"How many of us do you think there are?" Jarek asked when Kerra shared her thoughts. "Resonants, I mean. You said maybe one in ten thousand births. Over the whole archipelago, that could be..."

"Dozens, possibly hundreds," Kerra calculated. "Though many probably died in childhood, mistaken for broken weavers or suppressed before they could manifest fully. Or they learned to hide their abilities and live as normal single-tide weavers."

"Should we look for them? Try to gather them somehow?"

"Eventually, maybe. But we can't force it. People need to come willingly, when they're ready. Forced gathering would just create the kind of concentration of power that frightens everyone." Kerra paused. "What we can do is make sure everyone knows that resonants exist, that we're not monsters, and that help is available if they want it."

"A beacon instead of a demand."

"Exactly."

They found two more resonants during the voyage—both adults who'd been hiding their abilities for years, living as unbound and pretending to have no power rather than revealing what they were. One, a woman named Senna, had been surviving as a healer in a remote island community, using subtle tide-weaving disguised as herbal remedies. The other, a man called Toran, had been working as a ship's carpenter, using stone-weaving to reinforce hulls while claiming he was just skilled with wood.

Both were hesitant at first, but Kerra's patient demonstration of control techniques and Jarek's enthusiastic testimony about how much training helped eventually convinced them to trust.

By the time the Stormrunner turned back toward Deepwater Crossing, Kerra had four resonants under her care—herself, Jarek, Senna, and Toran. It wasn't many, but it was a start. Proof that resonants could work together peacefully, that they could form community rather than compete for dominance.

"You're building something important," Lysse said as they approached familiar waters. "A network of people who understand each other's struggles and can support each other. That's how real change starts—not grand gestures but patient accumulation of small victories."

"I hope you're right. Because right now it feels fragile. Like one mistake could turn everyone against resonants permanently."

"Then don't make mistakes. Be the example others need to see."

They docked at Deepwater Crossing to cheers and curious crowds. Word had spread that the Stormrunner was carrying multiple resonants, and everyone wanted to see these rare individuals. Kerra felt the weight of expectation settle back onto her shoulders—welcome home, now perform.

But then Davin pushed through the crowd, his face lit with genuine delight, and pulled her into an unexpected hug. "You're back! And you brought students! This is perfect!"

"Perfect for what?"

"For the academy's expansion. We've been building a new dormitory wing specifically for advanced weaving studies. With four resonants to teach and demonstrate cooperative techniques, we can offer programs no other institution in the archipelago can match."

Kerra introduced Jarek, Senna, and Toran, watching as Davin immediately began peppering them with questions about their experiences and abilities. Within hours, he'd integrated them into the academy structure, finding roles that played to their strengths while respecting their individual needs for space and autonomy.

It was exactly what Kerra had hoped for—not isolation or institutionalization but community and purpose.

That evening, she stood with her three fellow resonants on the academy grounds and looked up at the stars.

"Is it always like this?" Jarek asked quietly. "Everyone watching, waiting for you to do something amazing or terrible?"

"Yes," Kerra admitted. "But it gets easier. You learn to ignore the watchers and focus on the work. And having others who understand helps more than you'd think."

"I never imagined I'd have that," Senna said. "Community. People who wouldn't fear me."

"You have it now," Kerra promised. "All of you. We're building something new here—a tradition that honors power without fetishizing it, that teaches restraint alongside technique. It's not perfect, but it's ours."

Toran, who'd been quiet most of the voyage, finally spoke. "What happens when more resonants are found? When we're not four but forty or four hundred?"

"Then we deal with that challenge when it comes. Right now, we focus on building the foundation—trust, training, demonstration that resonants can exist peacefully within society. Everything else builds on that."

It was the right answer, though Kerra knew it only delayed harder questions. Eventually, they'd have to grapple with what a world with many resonants looked like. Whether power would concentrate or distribute. Whether cooperation or competition would define their relationships.

But those were problems for the future. Tonight, she had four resonants including herself, an academy full of eager students, and stars overhead to remind her that some things remained constant even as everything changed.

It was enough for now. More than enough.

Tomorrow would bring new challenges. But tomorrow always did, and somehow, Kerra kept finding ways to meet them.

She was learning to trust that pattern. To believe that preparation and good intentions and willingness to adapt could carry her through whatever came next.

She was learning, slowly, to trust herself.

And that, perhaps, was the most important lesson of all.
