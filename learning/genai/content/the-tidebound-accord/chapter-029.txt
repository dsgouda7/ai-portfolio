Chapter 29: The Void-Touched

The first report came from Shellhaven, a fishing community on the western curve of the outer archipelago, six weeks after Sera's Council presentation.

A water-weaver named Cors Brinestep, forty-three years old, third-generation weaver, no prior history of unusual tide-behavior, had been mending nets in her yard one evening when she had — in her own words, relayed through the island's Academy correspondent — gone somewhere. She had still been sitting in the yard. Her hands had still been holding the nets. But the nets had not felt like nets and the yard had not felt like a yard and what she had experienced for the three minutes that her neighbor reported she had been sitting motionless and unresponsive was a darkness with cold and texture and the vertiginous sense of enormous depth in all directions and then, without warning, a presence that had turned toward her with the specific quality of a thing that had been searching and had briefly found.

She had returned to herself without injury. She remembered nothing of the three minutes. She had asked her neighbor afterward whether she had seemed all right, because she felt foggy and strange, and the neighbor had said she had looked as though she was asleep with her eyes open.

Cors Brinestep was a water-weaver. She had no void-tide access. In thirty-two years of practice she had never touched any element other than water.

Tam brought the report to Sera on a Thursday morning, paper flat on the table between them in the small office he had given her to work in — a room off the Council corridor with a window that looked at the courtyard wall, narrow shelves, and a desk that had belonged to three previous junior researchers whose names were carved into the underside of the drawer. Sera had found the names and found them companionable. She thought about those previous junior researchers sometimes: what they had worked on, whether they had found what they were looking for, whether their work had mattered in ways they lived to understand.

She read the Brinestep report twice.

"The secondary indicator," she said.

"Yes."

"One incident."

"One reported incident," Tam said. "The correspondent in Shellhaven notes that Brinestep was reluctant to report it at all. She thought it made her sound unstable. Her neighbor convinced her that the Academy correspondence process was confidential and no one would think poorly of her." He paused. "How many unreported incidents do we think have occurred, if the reported ones require this much social overcoming?"

This was the kind of question Sera had been learning to ask herself over the six weeks since the Council session: not just what is visible but what proportion of the real situation does the visible represent. Tam was very good at this kind of second-order thinking and was actively teaching it to her, not through direct instruction but through the way he framed his own questions. She had begun to see how much of the skill of institutional work was this: the recognition that the information you receive is a sample, not the whole, and that the qualities of the gap between sample and whole were often more important than the sample itself.

"The involuntary channeling would be disorienting and frightening," Sera said. "And it would feel shameful to a single-tide weaver — like losing control of your own element. They'd be likely to keep it private, at least initially."

"Especially in communities where there's any residual tension about weaving," Tam agreed. "Which is most communities."

Three more reports came in the following week, from different islands, none of them in communication with each other as far as the Academy correspondents could determine. A flame-weaver in Highstone Croft. Two water-weavers from different communities in the Outer Shelves, both reporting within days of each other though neither knew the other. All described the same essential experience: the abrupt displacement into a dark and spatially ambiguous space, the presence that turned toward them briefly and then released, the return to ordinary consciousness with disorientation but no lasting physical effect.

None of them had any memory of what had passed through them during the episode.

None of them were resonants. None of them had void-tide access.

What struck Sera, reading through the reports in sequence, was the consistency of their language even accounting for the fact that none of these people knew each other or had access to any common framework for describing what had happened. The darkness with texture. The sense of depth in all directions. The presence that found rather than sought. Four people on four different islands, speaking four slightly different dialects of the archipelago's common tongue, had arrived independently at the same essential description. This was not the consistency of a shared story being passed around. This was the consistency of a shared experience.

She added this observation to her session notes and brought it to Tam. He read it, nodded, and added it to the Council correspondence file that was growing steadily thicker on his desk.

Windcrest came to Tam's office on the day the fourth report arrived and sat across the table from both of them with the expression of a man who had been hoping to be wrong about something and was now processing the implication of not being wrong.

"Four incidents," he said. "Widely distributed geographically. Single-tide weavers, no prior void history, same phenomenological report. No organic explanation." He was quiet for a moment. "I was skeptical of the student's initial report because it was a single datum from a subjective source. Four corroborating reports from independent sources who have no knowledge of the theoretical framework they are confirming —" He stopped.

"Changes the evidential landscape," Tam said.

"Yes." Windcrest looked at Sera. "You have been reading the sealed journals. What does Kerra say about the mechanism of the involuntary channeling?"

Sera pulled the relevant notes. "She speculates in the later journals that the fragments don't choose their channels. They're drawn to openings — moments when a weaver's tide-contact is particularly intense or particularly raw. She uses the word unguarded. She says the void is sensitive to emotional states, that the other four tides carry something of the emotional quality of the person accessing them, and that the void — the real void, the space between tides — registers extremes. Grief, fear, rage. She calls them resonant emotions, not because resonants feel them more intensely but because those particular states create a kind of amplified signal in the tide-contact."

Windcrest looked at his copy of the incident reports. "The flame-weaver in Highstone Croft — the correspondent notes that she had lost her father the previous week."

"The two water-weavers in the Outer Shelves," Tam said. "One of them had been in a violent dispute with her neighbor, according to the correspondent's background notes. The other — I'm less sure of."

Sera looked at the fourth report. "The Shellhaven water-weaver, Brinestep. The correspondent mentions she has been managing a difficult fishing season. The community's primary catch has been failing for two seasons. Economic anxiety, sustained over months."

Windcrest set his papers down. "So the fragments are not selecting randomly," he said. "They are selecting by emotional intensity. Weavers who are in states of acute grief, rage, or sustained despair create an amplified signal in their tide-contact, and the fragments are drawn to that signal as an access point."

"Not for possession," Sera said. "Kerra is very clear on this. The fragments aren't trying to take over these weavers. They're using them to — she uses the phrase test the terrain. To probe what's available. To locate themselves in relation to existing resonant signatures." She paused. "In relation to me, possibly. She describes the fragments as navigating. Looking for something specific."

The room was quiet.

"Looking for what?" Windcrest said.

Sera thought about the vast broken presence in the void, and the specific quality of its recognition of her, and the feeling of being expected.

"I think they're looking for a resonant who can reach them," she said. "A resonant who can access the void at sufficient depth. The single-tide weavers are —" she looked for the right word — "weak signals. They can be used briefly. But they can't be used to do anything purposeful. They're probes, not pathways."

Windcrest looked at her for a long moment with his precise, quick-processing eyes. "You are describing the fragments as having directive intelligence," he said. "That is a significant claim."

"I know."

"Kerra's summary document says they retain mobility and apparent purpose. She does not claim directive intelligence in the sense of coordinated planning."

"The summary document is thirty years older than the journal entries," Sera said. "In the final journals she describes the third observation — the one eight months before her death — as more deliberate. More concentrated. She uses the word purposeful directly." She looked at her notes. "She writes: 'I am no longer confident they are simply drifting. I believe they are converging. Whether this represents the reconstitution of directive consciousness or merely the physics of fragmented void-matter drawn toward common resonance, I cannot determine. But the distinction may matter less than it seems, because the practical outcome is the same.'"

Windcrest was quiet for a long time after that. Then he said: "We need to expand the monitoring protocol. Every Academy correspondent in the outer archipelago needs to be actively soliciting reports. We should not wait for weavers to volunteer information — we need people asking the specific questions."

"Agreed," Tam said. "Tidefold is coordinating the correspondent network; I'll brief her today."

"And the student." Windcrest looked at Sera directly, which he had not done very often during the meeting, as though he had been avoiding the implications of what he was about to say. "You experienced the primary indicator weeks before the secondary indicators began appearing elsewhere. That means you are in some kind of advance relationship with the fragment activity. The fragments found you first." He paused. "That is either because you are particularly sensitive or because you are specifically relevant to them in a way the single-tide weavers are not."

"Or both," Tam said.

"Or both," Windcrest agreed.

Sera thought of Kerra's sealed journals, and the passage she had found in volume three, written in the fifty-eighth year — three years before the first of Kerra's own recognition incidents: One of the things I have come to understand about the Hollow King, in the long retrospective of decades, is that he was not simply powerful. He was specifically powerful in the way that resonants are: drawing all five tides simultaneously, feeling everything. In his final state, in the Rift, when I reached him, he was no longer a person. But he was still resonant. The fragments will be resonant too. They will be drawn, most specifically, to other resonants.

She had not said this to the Council yet. She had been waiting until she understood it better.

She said it now.

Both men were quiet after she finished.

"The fragments specifically target resonants," Windcrest said slowly. "Using single-tide weavers as probes to locate resonant signatures, then orienting toward those signatures. And you experienced recognition before the single-tide events began, which means —"

"They found me before they started using the other weavers," Sera said. "I was the first resonant accessible enough to be found. And they recognized me as what they were looking for."

The silence stretched out. In the harbor visible through the window, a large cargo ship was being guided slowly into the main quay by a pair of smaller vessels, the kind of ordinary massive work that happened every day without anyone thinking about it.

"How many resonants are currently enrolled in the Academy's seven campuses?" Sera asked.

Tam and Windcrest looked at each other.

"Approximately three hundred active students," Tam said. "Plus perhaps a hundred faculty and staff with resonant designation. And another four hundred or so in the general population who have had Academy training and now live and work across the archipelago." He paused. "Roughly eight hundred resonants with the depth of void-access that the fragments could use."

"Eight hundred access points that would be more useful to the fragments than any number of single-tide weavers," Windcrest said, with the precise tone of a man stating a fact he would very much prefer not to be a fact.

"We need to brief the faculty at all seven campuses," Tam said. "And the graduated resonants in the archipelago network." He looked at Sera. "This is going to become public, and when it does, it's going to produce fear. The word Hollow King will be in common usage again within a month of the briefings. We need to think carefully about how to communicate what we know without producing panic."

"And without producing the kind of elevated emotional states that might create additional access points," Sera added.

Both men looked at her. This was, she saw in their expressions, a consideration they had not yet arrived at themselves.

"Grief and rage and despair create the amplified signal the fragments use," she said. "Fear — sustained fear — probably does too. If we communicate this badly, if people become terrified and angry, we may accelerate the problem while trying to address it."

Windcrest pressed his fingers to his forehead briefly. "She's right," he said to Tam.

"She is," Tam said. "Nightcurrent." He looked at her with the particular quality he used when he was about to say something that required her to be ready for it. "I want you to think about this carefully, using the journals and whatever you can extrapolate from your own experience with the fragments: what do they want? Not what are they doing. What are they trying to achieve?"

Sera looked out the window at the ordinary harbor morning. She thought about the recognition in the void. The sense of being expected. The specific quality of enormous, patient anticipation.

"I think they want to come back together," she said. "I think they want to stop being fragments. And I think they need a resonant to do it through."

Neither Tam nor Windcrest said anything to that for a long time.

Outside, the cargo ship had made it to the quay and the lines were being thrown and caught, the ordinary reliable business of a world that had been managing its own complexity for a very long time. The tide-veins ran beneath it all, as they had always run, patient and fundamental and not particularly concerned with the choices of the people above them.

Sera thought about what it would mean to be a fragment of something that had once been complete. To be a piece of a consciousness shattered three hundred years ago, dispersed but not destroyed, drifting through the void-tide with the vestigial knowledge of what wholeness had felt like. She thought about what it meant to wait, patient as stone, for the conditions that might allow return.

She did not pity the Hollow King. She understood that he had been a person who had allowed power to consume him, who had chosen domination when cooperation was available, who had become something that needed to be stopped. Pity wasn't the relevant response to that history.

But she understood, with the particular clarity of a resonant who had felt the fragment's recognition, that the thing in the void was not purely malevolent. It was not hunger, not destruction as an end in itself. It was, at its most essential, the reflex of a fragmented consciousness reaching toward coherence — the same reflex that drove water to seek lower ground and fire to seek fuel and stone to resist displacement. The tides had their natures. What remained of the Hollow King had its nature too. That nature was what it was, regardless of what it cost the people in its path.

That did not make it safe. It did not make it something she could choose not to address.

It made it, she thought, something she could understand. And understanding might matter, when the time came for action.

She hoped it would.
