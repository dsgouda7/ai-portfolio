Chapter 13: What the Ship Decided to Know

Speaker Renn made the decision, in the exhausted, grey hours after Sernin Voy's arrest, that the ship's population had earned the full truth, delivered on the Council's own terms rather than in another fragment torn loose by rumor. She called for a full public address, broadcast to every deck simultaneously, the first such address in Aria's lifetime that had not been scheduled weeks in advance for some ceremonial occasion, and stood before the ship's assembled cameras that evening with a composure Aria, watching from the working group's repaired Deck Two office, found genuinely remarkable given how little sleep the Speaker had managed in the preceding days.

Renn told them everything. The signal, the Lantern's structure and likely age, Wren's provisional interpretation of the boundary and its slow, unmaking advance, the ninety-day mandate and its purpose, and finally, with a bluntness that Aria suspected cost her a great deal, the attempted sabotage, the reactor deck reconnaissance, and Sernin Voy's near-attack on the Lantern itself, framed not as an isolated madman's act but as a genuine, if extreme, expression of a fear that Renn acknowledged many aboard the ship likely shared in smaller, quieter forms.

"I will not pretend to you that this Council has easy answers," she said, near the address's close, her voice carrying through every commissary and corridor and crawlspace of the ship's nine decks and the Under-Hold beneath them. "We do not know, yet, what the boundary described in the Lantern's message truly is, or when, or whether, it might ever threaten the world we have spent two hundred and fourteen years crossing the dark to reach. We do not know whether the intelligence that built the Lantern still exists in any form capable of answering us, or whether we would even recognize an answer if it came. What I can tell you is this: this Council, and the working group whose careful, patient investigation has brought us this far, will not decide the fate of this ship through fear, through force, or through the actions of any single person who believes they alone understand the danger well enough to act outside the rest of us. We will decide together, with the fullest truth we can gather, exactly as our ancestors intended when they wrote the Charter that has carried us safely across two centuries of transit already. I ask every person aboard this vessel, Listed and Unlisted alike, Command Deck and Under-Hold alike, to hold to that same patience, that same trust in each other, in the days that remain before this Council reaches its decision."

The address's reception, in the days that followed, proved considerably more complicated than Renn's careful, hopeful framing had perhaps allowed for. Fear did not simply evaporate because it had been named honestly; if anything, Aria observed, walking the ship's corridors in the address's aftermath, the full truth seemed to settle into different pockets of the population in different, sometimes contradictory shapes. Some residents, she heard in overheard commissary conversations, took genuine comfort in the Council's transparency, relieved to finally understand the shape of the danger rather than imagining worse in its absence. Others grew more afraid, not less, now that abstract rumor had solidified into concrete, terrifying specifics: a boundary that unmade whole civilizations, still advancing, somewhere out there in a dark that no longer felt nearly as empty or as safe as it once had.

And in the Under-Hold, Aria found, visiting Nyla three days after the address, the reaction carried its own particular, bitter edge.

"They're using it," Nyla said flatly, pacing the narrow confines of the small chamber she shared with two cousins, her earlier tired resignation about the petition's delay replaced now with a harder, more focused anger. "Half the Registry review board's newest excuses for stalling our petition further are dressed up in exactly this crisis. Emergency footing considerations. Uncertain population accounting during a period of heightened ship-wide risk. As if giving three generations of Unlisted families the Registry status we've earned three times over is somehow a luxury this ship can't afford while it's busy being afraid of something twelve thousand light-years away."

"It's not twelve thousand light-years," Aria said, more out of exhausted reflex than any real desire to correct the point. "It's forty-one light-minutes, and twelve thousand years old. Different kind of far."

"You know what I mean." Nyla stopped pacing, fixing Aria with a look that held equal parts frustration and something gentler underneath it. "I'm not angry at you, Ari. I know none of this is your fault, not really, even if it started with your name on a sensor report. I'm angry that every single crisis this ship has ever faced, in a hundred and fifty years of Under-Hold history, somehow always ends up being the reason our petition has to wait one more session. Ration shortfalls. Structural failures. Now ancient cosmic horrors. There's always a reason, Ari, and it's never our turn, and I am so tired of being told to be patient by people who've never once had to actually wait for anything that mattered."

Aria had no good answer for that, and did not try to offer one, understanding by now, after eleven years of friendship, that what Nyla needed in moments like this was not a solution but simply to be heard, fully, without her friend rushing to smooth over a frustration that was, in every particular, entirely justified.

"I'll push it," she said instead. "I don't have Council standing, not really, but the working group has Renn's ear right now in a way it didn't three weeks ago, and I intend to use whatever small leverage that gives me to remind her, publicly if I have to, that this ship doesn't get to keep asking the Under-Hold to wait its turn every time something bigger comes along to justify the delay. That's not patience, Nyla. That's just neglect with better excuses."

Nyla's expression softened, just slightly, at that. "I know you will. I've never doubted that about you, not once in eleven years. I just needed to say it out loud to someone who'd actually understand why it matters." She managed a tired, wry smile. "Now go save the ship from ancient boundaries, or whatever it is you actually do all day in that fancy Deck Two office. I've got a petition to keep alive."

Aria kept her promise within the week, raising the matter directly with Speaker Renn during a brief private moment after a working group briefing, framing it not as an accusation but as a genuine strategic concern: that a ship fracturing along old lines of neglect and mistrust was less able, not more, to make the kind of careful, unified decision the Lantern crisis demanded. Renn listened with the same focused attention Aria had come to expect from her, and though she made no immediate promise, she did, within the following two weeks, quietly instruct the Registry review board to expedite its handling of Nyla's petition rather than allow it to be indefinitely deferred behind emergency footing concerns, a small, incomplete victory that Aria nonetheless carried back to the Under-Hold with more genuine satisfaction than she had felt in weeks.

The larger crisis, meanwhile, continued its slow, grinding progress. Sernin Voy's interrogation, conducted over the following days with a careful patience that Renn insisted upon despite considerable pressure from more hardline Magistrates to treat him as a straightforward saboteur deserving immediate, harsh punishment, revealed a network considerably smaller and less coordinated than Aria's earlier fears had suggested: not a vast conspiracy, but a handful of deeply frightened, deeply convinced individuals, radicalized gradually over years of quiet grievance and finally pushed, by the scale of the Lantern's revealed danger, into actions none of them, examined individually, would likely have contemplated alone.

Magistrate Ashe, for his part, emerged from the crisis's immediate aftermath in a position considerably more complicated than the one he had occupied before it began. His willingness to expose his own faction's radical fringe, to stand publicly alongside Renn and the working group he had spent weeks opposing, earned him a grudging new respect from across the Council's political divide, even as it cost him support among the more hardline members of his own Preservationist bloc, several of whom accused him, in increasingly bitter private correspondence that eventually found its way, through the ship's persistent rumor networks, to Aria's own ears, of having betrayed the very cause he had spent eighteen years championing.

It was in the wake of this fractured, uneasy period, some six weeks into the original ninety-day mandate, that Wren delivered a finding that would, once again, reshape the entire trajectory of the crisis, this time not through fear or danger, but through something Aria had not expected to feel at all in connection with the vast, ancient mystery ahead of them: hope.
