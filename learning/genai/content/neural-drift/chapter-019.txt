Chapter 19: New Frequencies

The alert came through Razor's monitoring system on a Wednesday, buried in a routine data analysis report that Kai almost pushed to the end of his queue.

It was three months after the Haruto Mori ruling, which meant the initial shock of Operation Clean Slate had faded from the news cycle and been replaced by the longer, slower story of the prosecutions. The public was still engaged, but differently. The outrage of the raids had settled into something more institutional: regular coverage of tribunal proceedings, advocacy group statements, legislative debates about consciousness protection frameworks. The story had become important rather than urgent, which was arguably the correct relationship to have with it, but which also meant that the specific, daily vigilance required to catch the next threat was harder to maintain.

Razor's alert was titled "Anomalous Neural Modification Patterns: Flagged for Human Review." The attached analysis was twenty-two pages. Kai made a coffee and read it carefully.

The subject was a company called ClearMind Technologies, founded eighteen months earlier and registered in the Macau Special Economic Zone. On its public website, ClearMind offered something it called Neural Optimization, described as a voluntary personality enhancement service. The website was polished and corporate and full of the language of wellness and self-improvement. Testimonials from satisfied clients described feeling clearer, more focused, better aligned with their professional goals. The pricing started at forty thousand yuan for what the website called a Standard Refresh and scaled up to three hundred thousand for the Premium Core Realignment, which promised comprehensive optimization of core cognitive and personality architecture.

Kai had seen enough corporate obfuscation in the past two years to read the language correctly. Standard Refresh. Core Realignment. The words meant something, and what they meant was not what the website said they meant.

Razor's report documented the anomaly she had found in ClearMind's public clinical filings, which companies offering any kind of neural modification service were required to submit to the federal medical registry. The filings were technically compliant: procedure descriptions, consent documentation, follow-up assessments. But Razor had run the follow-up assessments through a pattern-matching algorithm originally developed to detect the signature of unauthorized consciousness mapping, and she had found something unexpected.

The before-and-after neural profiles of ClearMind's clients were not showing the kind of incremental modification you would expect from a legitimate optimization service. They were showing replacement.

Not every client. Maybe forty percent of the cases in the registry showed the signature. But forty percent of the clinical filing population was not noise. Forty percent was a pattern.

The signature Razor was seeing was similar to, but not identical to, the one used in Project Drift and Project Continuance. It was subtler, more gradual, designed to be mistaken for natural personality evolution rather than the abrupt architectural overwrite of the earlier systems. Where Project Drift had essentially snapped a photograph of a person's consciousness and stored it, what ClearMind appeared to be doing was something more like a slow renovation: over multiple sessions, replacing one structural element at a time, until what remained was sufficiently different from the original that calling it the same person was a philosophical stretch.

What replaced the original? Razor's analysis of the modified profiles pointed to a common architecture. The replaced elements were not random. They were converging toward specific templates, personality frameworks that she had identified in corporate psychological screening databases. The kind of person who scored high on corporate compatibility metrics. The kind of person who was less likely to question authority, more likely to find satisfaction in hierarchical structures, more inclined toward the risk-averse caution that corporate management rewarded over creativity or dissent.

"They're building employees," Kai said when he got Razor on the channel. "Not just screening for compatible employees. Actually building them."

"The pattern is consistent with that interpretation," Razor said. Her voice filters gave her words the same measured weight regardless of content, which had the effect of making horrifying findings sound like laboratory observations. "I need to note that I am not certain the clients are unwilling. ClearMind is framing this as a voluntary service, and the consent documentation appears genuine. People are signing up for this."

"Why would anyone voluntarily replace their personality?"

"Economic pressure," Razor said. "I pulled employment records for the subset of clients I was able to identify. Ninety-one percent were either unemployed at the time of their first ClearMind session or had been passed over for significant promotions in the twelve months prior. A substantial number had failed corporate compatibility screenings at multiple employers." She paused. "The largest single employer of ClearMind's clients, post-procedure, is NeuroAxis Group."

Kai brought up NeuroAxis's profile on his terminal. Third-largest media-technology corporation in the Asia-Pacific zone. Significant political influence in the Shanghai-Beijing corridor. Their corporate compatibility screening program had been written about in business media as a model of rigorous candidate selection. High standards, the articles said. Exceptional organizational fit. Extraordinary employee retention rates.

Of course the retention rates were extraordinary. You retained people who had been built to stay.

He forwarded Razor's report to Agent Zhang with a three-line summary and requested an emergency briefing. Zhang responded in six minutes: "Tomorrow morning, seven o'clock. Good catch, both of you."

The briefing the next morning included Zhang, Kai, Wu, and a new addition to their senior team, Dr. Priya Nair, a philosopher and ethicist who had joined the Division six months earlier. Dr. Nair's specialty was the ethics of identity, which was a field that had barely existed ten years ago and had become urgently necessary since the consciousness rights debates had begun. She was quiet and precise in meetings, in the manner of someone trained to find the exact question rather than the approximate one.

She read Razor's report during the briefing and asked the exact question. "Is this identity theft or identity replacement? Because the legal framework treats them very differently."

"Explain the distinction," Zhang said.

"Identity theft would be taking someone's consciousness backup and storing it without permission, as in Project Drift. That is clearly criminal under the new frameworks. Identity replacement is different. If a person consents to a neural modification procedure, and the modification alters their fundamental personality, they may cease to be the same person in any meaningful sense, but they have consented to the procedure. The corporation offering the procedure may have concealed the full extent of what it does, but the client did sign a form." Dr. Nair's expression carried the specific strain of someone working through a problem that she found deeply uncomfortable but intellectually inescapable. "The consciousness rights frameworks we have were written to protect the people whose consciousness was taken without consent. They were not written to address the situation where someone voluntarily erases themselves."

"Can you voluntarily consent to your own erasure?" Kai asked.

"Legally, that's exactly the question we need the tribunal to answer before we can prosecute ClearMind," Dr. Nair said. "Philosophically, I have a fairly strong view. Practically, this case may require legislation rather than prosecution, and legislation takes longer."

Zhang turned to Kai. "We need more than a pattern analysis before we take this to the prosecutor's office. We need direct evidence of what ClearMind is actually doing in those procedure rooms. What the consent forms actually disclose, what the clients are actually told, whether there's coercion in addition to the economic pressure." She looked at him steadily. "We need someone inside."

"I know," Kai said. He had known it since the first page of Razor's report. The kind of evidence that would survive a prosecution required a witness who had experienced the intake process firsthand: the sales pitch, the consent documentation, the actual procedure. "I'll build the cover."

The cover took three weeks. Michael Shen was a forty-year-old former executive at a mid-tier media technology firm who had been passed over twice for senior positions because his psychological assessment scores indicated low organizational alignment. He was wealthy enough to afford ClearMind's services without the payment appearing suspicious, had a history of increasingly frustrated job applications at top-tier corporations, and was the kind of man who had started to believe that the problem might be with himself rather than with the institutions that kept rejecting him. His entire digital history, constructed by Razor in collaboration with Kai's own profile-building skills from his memory broker days, was impeccable. The backstory was designed around the truth that Kai had observed in the ClearMind clients: a person who genuinely wanted to fit and had been excluded long enough to consider doing something drastic to change that.

The hardest part of the cover wasn't the digital history. It was the psychological truth of it. Kai had to understand Michael Shen from the inside, had to inhabit the specific quiet desperation of a man who had been told he didn't fit so many times that he had started to agree. He spent the three weeks reading the profiles of actual ClearMind clients, understanding the texture of that experience: the particular shame of failing compatibility screens, the way the corporate world communicated rejection while maintaining plausible deniability that the rejection was about anything other than fit.

He understood it more than he expected to. He had grown up in the Lower Stacks, and the Lower Stacks were full of people who had been told they didn't fit, people who had internalized the institution's verdict on their worth. The specific emotional landscape that ClearMind was exploiting was recognizable to him at a level deeper than professional analysis.

That recognition was uncomfortable. He filed it and used it.

Lin reviewed the cover in their last preparation session, two days before Kai's initial contact with ClearMind.

"Michael Shen is more vulnerable than you usually play," she said.

"Michael Shen is the profile that ClearMind targets," Kai said. "I need to match the profile."

"I know." She was reviewing the psychological assessment they'd built for Michael Shen's fictional employment history, a document designed to show the specific pattern of near-success and systemic rejection that ClearMind's intake counselors were trained to identify. "I just want to make sure you can carry it without it getting under your defenses."

"It'll be fine."

"That's the answer Michael Shen gives," Lin said. "I'm asking what Kai Chen says."

He looked at her. They had a particular kind of shorthand now, after eighteen months of working together at this level of intensity. The shorthand included knowing when the professional and personal versions of a question were different questions wearing the same words. "Kai Chen says he's aware of the material and knows where it comes from and will maintain separation."

"Good enough," Lin said, and moved on to the operational logistics.

ClearMind's initial contact process was slick and warm in the way of a high-end service industry, designed to make the prospective client feel understood rather than assessed. The intake coordinator who contacted Michael Shen via neural interface message was named Belen, and her communication style was precise, empathetic, and utterly professional. She asked good questions about his career history, listened to the answers with apparent care, and reflected them back in language that subtly reframed failure as misalignment rather than inadequacy. It was excellent therapeutic technique deployed in the service of a sale.

She was also, Kai noted, using a script that had been optimized through hundreds of client interactions to identify the specific psychological profile that ClearMind's procedure was designed to modify. The question about his family relationships. The question about what he valued most in a professional environment. The way she listened for the particular tension between what he said he wanted and what the texture of his language suggested he actually felt.

By the end of the intake call, Michael Shen had been offered a complimentary initial neural assessment, with no obligation, simply to understand his current baseline. Belen described it as the first step in understanding how to be the person he was meant to be, and the language was so carefully calibrated to resonate with the specific longing she'd identified in the preceding thirty minutes that Kai had to make a conscious effort to maintain the separation Lin had warned him about.

He accepted.

The assessment appointment was scheduled for the following week, at ClearMind's flagship facility in Neo-Shanghai's Pudong district, in a building that was glass and calm neutrals and the kind of plants that corporate designers used to signal that a space was about wellness rather than transactions.

Kai confirmed the appointment, filed his briefing with Zhang, and spent the evening reviewing Razor's updated analysis. Three new clients had appeared in ClearMind's clinical filings in the past seventy-two hours. All three were now employed by NeuroAxis Group.

The pattern was accelerating.

Whatever ClearMind was doing in those procedure rooms, they were becoming better at it, faster at it, more systematic. If they were not stopped, and quickly, they would perfect a technology that could quietly reshape human personality at scale, doing by consent and economic pressure what Project Drift had done by theft. The result would be indistinguishable: a population of people who had ceased to be themselves, rebuilt to the specifications of corporate utility.

The difference was that nobody would be able to point to a server rack full of stolen consciousness backups. The evidence would be in people walking around in their own bodies, living lives that fit perfectly into corporate structures, with no memory of having ever wanted something different.

Kai Chen went to bed in his Geneva apartment and lay in the dark for a while, thinking about the people who had signed ClearMind's consent forms.

He thought about what it meant to be so excluded from the world's opportunity structures that erasing yourself started to look like a rational option.

He thought about Michael Shen, who was not real but who contained enough of the truth of real people that the distinction felt thin.

He was going to go into that facility next week and find out what they were doing. He was going to get the evidence. He was going to make it stop.

Outside, Neo-Shanghai's familiar electromagnetic hum was visible in the data feeds even from Geneva, the pulse of the world's largest city maintaining its ancient rhythm of neon and rain and ten million lives making their calculations about what survival required.

Some of those calculations were going wrong, in ways that the people making them couldn't see.

Kai closed his eyes and made a plan.
