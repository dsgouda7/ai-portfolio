Chapter 15: Below the Surface

Marcus Chen arrived at Elena's office at nine-fifteen with two coffees from the bakery around the corner and a brown accordion folder that had the slightly distended look of something that had been periodically opened and added to over several months. He set the coffees on the desk and the folder beside them, and Elena noted that he had not bothered to knock — a small thing, but it marked something about how their relationship had settled over the past year. The complicated professional distance was still there, but it had acquired texture and worn patches, like a coat that had been repaired enough times to feel inhabited.

"I started the Santos file in March," he said, sitting down and pulling the accordion folder toward him. "Three separate times I thought I had enough to justify opening an official inquiry and three separate times I had it redirected. The first time, Captain Martinez told me to focus on cases with open dispositions. The second time, the Deputy Chief's office sent a memo reminding the department that cases under concurrent jurisdiction with county or state agencies required formal coordination protocols before independent investigation. The third time, someone from the Mayor's liaison office called the Captain directly."

"Who in the Mayor's office?"

"A deputy chief of staff named Henderson. He was very polite. He noted that David Santos' death had been thoroughly investigated by the county medical examiner and that any suggestion to the contrary risked creating a climate of distrust around a major state infrastructure project. He used the phrase 'reputational harm to public investment' twice."

Elena pulled the first coffee toward her and held it without drinking. "What did you tell the Captain?"

"That I would drop it." He looked at her steadily. "I didn't drop it. I just stopped generating paperwork about it."

She nodded. That was Marcus Chen — methodical, cautious, capable of operating in the space between what the institution permitted and what basic conscience required. It was one of the things she had always found both admirable and occasionally maddening about him.

He spread the folder's contents across the available surface of her desk. The Santos file was thorough for something assembled outside official channels. The county coroner's inquest report, obtained through a public records request. Santos' personnel file from the Department of Infrastructure, also public record once it was categorized as related to a completed employment term. His assignment records, showing the Harbor Development Project audit as the last active case on his docket. Medical examiner photos that Marcus had obtained through department-to-department channels before the redirections began. And a single sheet of notes in Marcus's handwriting that Elena leaned forward to read.

The notes were brief and precise. Santos had been found floating in the harbor near the northern pier access at approximately six forty-five in the morning of February eighteenth. He had been dead for an estimated seven to ten hours, placing time of death between nine PM and midnight of the seventeenth. His vehicle was found in the Port Authority parking structure two blocks from where the body was recovered. His briefcase was found on the front seat of the vehicle, unlocked, apparently undisturbed. His laptop was not with the vehicle or at his Port Authority visitor access office. The official conclusion was that he had walked to the pier area after leaving his vehicle, possibly to view the development site at night, and had fallen into the harbor. The water temperature in February was forty-one degrees. He had not been a strong swimmer according to his family.

"No laptop," Elena said.

"His work laptop. Issued by the Department of Infrastructure, catalogued as government property. It was reported as unrecovered in the inquest report. The Department of Infrastructure's IT records show it as last connecting to the state network on February sixteenth."

"The day before he died."

"The day before. After that, nothing. The device was never recovered." Marcus picked up his coffee. "The briefcase on the front seat of his vehicle. I've been thinking about that since March. A man who drove to the Port Authority to work on an audit, found his briefcase on the car seat unlocked when his body was found the next morning. If he went to look at the pier and fell in, why isn't the briefcase with him? If he deliberately left it in the car before going to the pier, why? Why not lock the car?"

"Because he didn't go to the pier voluntarily," Elena said. "He was taken there. Or he was killed and moved there."

"That's what the medical examiner found consistent with the physical evidence, unofficially. The published inquest report ruled accidental drowning. But the ME's private notes, which I found through a contact in that office, indicate that the bruising pattern on Santos' forearms was more consistent with being held than with struggling in water. Post-mortem bruising on his upper back suggesting he may have been placed in the water rather than having fallen."

"And this didn't change the ruling."

"The official position is that the bruising could have resulted from impact with pier structures after entering the water. The ME is a county employee. The county executive is a strong public supporter of the Harbor Development Project."

Elena sat with this for a moment. A state auditor was investigating a billion-dollar development project, found financial irregularities significant enough to require an independent physical survey of the harbor, and drowned the night before he was apparently planning to complete his report. His laptop vanished. The replacement auditor cleared the project and then went to work for a company that profited from that clearance. The lead detective who tried to investigate was redirected from the mayor's office.

The architecture of the cover-up was not subtle, precisely because it didn't need to be. It needed only to be distributed across enough institutional layers that no single actor appeared responsible, and to have enough political backing that official channels would prefer the easy conclusion to the expensive one.

"I need to talk to harbor workers," Elena said. "People who knew Santos or who've been paying attention to the development site. Whitmore said there are others who've noticed discrepancies."

"Be careful how you approach them. Three of Whitmore's colleagues were told their renewal certificates were under administrative review within two weeks of asking questions about the project. These are people whose livelihoods depend on maintaining good standing with the Port Authority."

"I'll be careful." She began reassembling his documents into a working order she could follow. "What do you know about Pacific Harbor Corp's people? Who actually runs it?"

"The CEO is a man named Richard Calder. He has an operational background in port management, worked for several major development companies before Pacific Harbor Corp was incorporated. Clean record professionally, no criminal history. His name doesn't connect to the founding families." Marcus paused. "The CFO is a woman named Anne Strickland. Her maiden name was Blackwell."

"Robert Blackwell's daughter?"

"Niece. He has two children from a first marriage and three from a second, plus several extended family members in professional roles throughout his various business interests. Anne Strickland née Blackwell has an MBA from the University of Washington, sixteen years in corporate finance, and the CFO position at Pacific Harbor Corp since its incorporation three years ago."

So the founding-family connection to the corporation had an operational presence, not just a financial stake. Someone named Blackwell was managing Pacific Harbor Corp's money. Elena noted this carefully.

She spent the morning reviewing everything Marcus had brought, taking her own notes, building a parallel timeline that she pinned to her whiteboard: Santos' assignment to the audit in October of the previous year, his interviews with harbor pilots including Whitmore in November and December, his final connection to the state network on February sixteenth, his death on February seventeenth, the replacement auditor's clearance of the project in June, Foust's retirement in September, Foust's consulting relationship with Coastal Maritime Partners beginning two months later.

The timeline was not a proof of anything. But it was a structure — a sequence of events with logical relationships and suspicious gaps that suggested an underlying mechanism she had not yet seen.

In the afternoon she drove to the harbor.

Her first contact was a man named Dale Ferrara, a master mariner who ran a small towing company out of a slip on the working waterfront's south end. Whitmore had given her his name with the explanation that Ferrara had been one of the harbor pilots Santos approached and was, unlike Whitmore, officially retired from the Port Authority and therefore harder to pressure. Ferrara met her on the dock beside his boat, a sixty-foot tug called the Elsinore, and shook her hand with the grip of someone who had spent fifty years doing physical work.

"Whitmore says you're straight," Ferrara said. "So I'll talk to you. But I'm going to tell you the same thing I told Santos: I'm not going to sign anything or go on record until I see something official behind it. I have grandkids. I don't need trouble."

"I understand. Talk to me off the record. What did you tell Santos?"

Ferrara leaned against the tug's wheelhouse and looked out at the harbor for a long moment. The afternoon light was thin and grey, the kind of autumn light that made everything look provisional.

"I told Santos that the northern channel depth surveys in the development company's environmental filing were wrong in a way that made no physical sense to anyone who works this harbor. The survey shows the northern approach at a consistent forty-two feet below mean lower low water across the entire relevant stretch. I've been working that channel for thirty-two years. It's never been consistently forty-two feet. There are shoal areas that have always come in shallow — twenty-eight, thirty feet in places — because of how the glacial till deposited out there after the last ice age. The development company's survey doesn't show those shoals. It shows a smooth, uniform bottom that doesn't exist."

"Could the shoals have shifted?"

Ferrara gave her a look that suggested the question was politely stupid. "Glacial till doesn't migrate over a four-year period. Those shoals have been there since before the city was founded. The 1886 hydrographic survey, which is the oldest one we have, shows them in the same positions they're in today, give or take a few feet."

"So the survey is wrong. Could it be a mistake? Calibration error?"

"It's wrong in one specific direction. The channel appears deeper and more uniform than it is. A calibration error would produce random variance. This is systematic. It shows exactly the kind of bottom you'd want to see if you were planning to build significant pier infrastructure without acknowledging the dredging costs you'd actually incur." He paused. "Or if you were trying to make the harbor floor appear geologically different from how it actually is for some other reason I don't understand."

Elena stored that carefully. A falsified bottom survey. Not randomly falsified — falsified in a particular direction, toward a particular outcome.

"Did you tell Santos anything about what's below the bay floor? The seabed geology?"

Ferrara gave her a different kind of look — not dismissive, but attentive. "He asked me the same question. Why do you ask?"

"I'm not sure yet. I'm trying to understand what Santos was looking at."

"I told him I didn't know the deep geology. That's not harbor navigation information. But there was a regional geological survey published about two years ago — a Pacific Northwest coastal resources assessment. Santos mentioned it. He'd been reading it."

She filed that. Santos had been reading a geological resources survey of the Pacific Northwest coast. That put a specific frame around the question Whitmore had described: what Santos was asking about the ocean floor was not a navigation question but a resources question.

Her second contact was harder to find. Her name was Rosa Medina, a marine biologist who had worked as a contractor for the original Harbor Development Project environmental impact assessment before being removed from the project team four months into the process. Elena had found her name in the project's acknowledgment section of the final environmental filing, listed among contributors — but when she looked for Medina in the report's methodology section, the biologist's name appeared in the marine habitat survey work but not in the final water quality or seabed assessments, which had been completed by a different firm.

Rosa Medina now worked as a science teacher at Ashmont Bay's north side high school. Elena called the school's main office and left a message. Medina called back at four in the afternoon.

"I was wondering when someone would call," Medina said. Her voice was careful, measured in the way of a person who had thought about this conversation before having it.

"What made you leave the environmental assessment project?" Elena asked.

"Technically, my contract wasn't renewed after phase one. Practically, I was told by the project manager that the second phase of the marine habitat survey would be conducted internally by Pacific Harbor Corp's own science team. That was unusual. Standard practice for a public infrastructure project of this scale is to use independent contractors throughout to maintain objectivity."

"What did you find in phase one that you weren't able to finish?"

A pause. "I found that the benthic community — the organisms living on and in the seabed — in the northern harbor area was significantly different from what the company's preliminary survey suggested. The preliminary survey showed a relatively simple substrate with limited biological significance. What I was finding was more complex. Unusual concentrations of certain invertebrate communities. Sediment chemistry that suggested the presence of metal-rich substrates."

"Metal-rich substrates," Elena repeated. "What kind of metals?"

Another pause. "The preliminary samples I was able to analyze before my contract ended showed elevated concentrations of several elements. Including rare earth elements. Specifically, neodymium, dysprosium, and what looked like preliminary indicators of a bastnäsite deposit." She paused again. "I'm a marine biologist, not a mining geologist. I don't know what those readings meant in terms of commercial viability. But I know they were not in the company's environmental filing, and I know I was removed from the project before I could complete my survey."

Elena sat for a moment with the weight of what she had just heard. Rare earth elements. The components of electric motors, wind turbines, defense technology, computer hardware. Elements whose global supply was dominated by a handful of producers, whose commercial value had been increasing for a decade as demand for clean energy technology expanded, and whose extraction from a domestic coastal deposit would be worth an enormous amount of money to whoever held the rights to extract them.

The harbor floor. Not just the water, not just the infrastructure, not just the development value of the northern shoreline real estate. What was below the bay floor, potentially worth billions. And whatever rights Pacific Harbor Corp was establishing through the Harbor Development Project, whatever claim they were building through the construction of piers and facilities and navigational infrastructure, those rights might extend to something far more valuable than the development itself.

She drove back toward the city slowly, thinking about the relationship between what was happening on the surface and what was lying beneath it. Santos had found the falsified surveys, the misleading environmental assessment, the financial irregularities. He had started asking about the seabed geology. He had been killed before he could connect those things into a single coherent picture.

She stopped at a traffic light two blocks from her office and looked at her own face in the rearview mirror. She had a habit of doing this when she was working through something — not out of vanity, but because it reminded her that she had a stake in the cases she took, that she was not an abstract analytical process but a person who could also be hurt, who was also visible, who could also be watched.

Someone in a dark SUV had been behind her since she left the harbor. She had noticed it at the waterfront, noted the plates, and noticed them again at the turn onto the main arterial. The vehicle was maintaining careful distance — not tailing clumsily, but present consistently in a way that had a practiced quality.

She drove past her office and kept going, taking a circuitous route through the Maritime District that required two turns against traffic and a pass through an alley connecting to the public parking behind the old municipal building. The SUV did not follow her through the alley. When she emerged on the other side, it was gone.

Back at her office, she locked the door and stood at the window for a moment, looking at the street below. Then she called Marcus.

"I was followed from the harbor," she said. "Dark SUV, Washington plates." She read him the number. "Can you run that?"

"I'll run it now." She heard keyboard sounds. A pause. "Registered to a company called Pacific Rim Security Consulting. That company's registered address is a UPS Store on Eastwick Avenue. Its principal is a man named Todd Halverson, who has a background in private intelligence work — three years Army intelligence, then twelve years in corporate security for a mining company."

"A mining company," Elena said.

"Pacific Northwest Extraction Partners. They ceased operations in two thousand and nineteen."

Elena looked at her notes from the afternoon. Rosa Medina's preliminary seabed samples. Rare earth elements. A marine biologist removed from an environmental assessment project. A corporate security operative with a background in mining surveillance, watching her from a dark SUV on the harbor road.

"Marcus," she said. "I think Santos found something much bigger than cost overruns."

"Tell me."

She told him. She described Ferrara's account of the falsified depth surveys, Medina's preliminary findings of rare earth concentrations, the way the two pieces fit against what Whitmore had described about Santos' questions regarding the seabed geology. She described the geological resources survey Santos had apparently been reading.

The silence on the other end of the call was careful and complete.

"You're saying the Harbor Development Project isn't primarily about developing the harbor," Marcus said finally.

"I'm saying that the Harbor Development Project may be primarily about establishing legal and physical infrastructure that creates a basis for a claim on what's below the harbor floor. And that the people behind it killed David Santos to prevent exactly that from being discovered."

Another silence. "Come to the department tomorrow. I'm going to make some calls tonight to a friend at the state Attorney General's office. Not officially — I just want to know what I'm walking into before we take this anywhere formal."

"Be careful who you talk to. If someone in the Mayor's office is protecting this project, the AG's office might have similar vulnerabilities."

"I know." A pause. "I have one person I trust there. Her name is Carolyn Vessey. She's been there twenty years and she doesn't play political games. If anyone in that office can tell me whether the AG is safe to approach, it's her."

Elena agreed and hung up. She sat at her desk and looked at the whiteboard with its preliminary timeline, then added new entries. Rosa Medina — removed from environmental assessment, phase one. Preliminary rare earth indicators. Pacific Rim Security Consulting — surveilling Elena.

Then she added a question at the bottom of the board in large letters: What legal mechanism allows claiming the ocean floor?

She didn't know the answer yet. But she thought Santos had found it. And she thought it was the reason he was dead.

She locked the office carefully and set a new habit for herself: checking the street from the window before leaving, varying her departure time, noting what vehicles were on the block. She had done this before, in the worst stretches of the founding conspiracy investigation. She had hoped she wouldn't need to do it again.

The harbor was visible from the window's north face, the dark water catching the ambient light of the city and returning it as something cooler and less legible. Whatever was buried in the seabed out there, it had been worth enough to build a billion-dollar fiction over. It had been worth enough to drown a man who was getting too close to it.

Elena turned off the office lights and took the stairs carefully, checking the street from the ground floor door before she walked out into the October night.
