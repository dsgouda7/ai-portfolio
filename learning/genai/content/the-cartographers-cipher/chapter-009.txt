Chapter 9: Three Months Later

The Ashmont Bay Tribune had called it "The Trial of the Century." For twelve weeks, the city had been riveted by testimony about the founding families' conspiracy, the embezzlement, the murders. James Ashmont was convicted on all counts and sentenced to life without parole. The other Guardians board members received lighter sentences in exchange for cooperation.

Victoria Ashmont testified against her cousin, her voice steady as she described years of deception and theft. The Kahale family won their civil suit, receiving a settlement of two hundred million dollars and the return of several key properties. Dr. Rebecca Kahale announced the money would be used to establish a foundation supporting indigenous education and land rights.

The city's legal framework survived, but barely. Property titles dating back to the founding were examined and, in many cases, upheld under statutes of limitations and adverse possession laws. The past couldn't be entirely undone, but it could be acknowledged and, in some small way, addressed.

Elena had testified in four separate trials and given countless depositions. She had become a reluctant public figure, her face recognized on streets, her investigation turned into podcast episodes and documentary proposals.

She was thoroughly sick of it.

Today, three months after James Ashmont's conviction, she sat in her office trying to catch up on paperwork when her phone rang. An unknown number with a Seattle area code.

"Voss Investigations."

A woman's voice, crisp and professional. "Miss Voss, my name is Catherine Reed. I'm an attorney with Mercer and Blake in Seattle. I'm calling about a potential case that may interest you."

"I'm not taking new clients right now. I'm still dealing with the aftermath of my last case."

"I understand. I read about your work exposing the Ashmont conspiracy. That's actually why I'm calling. This case may be related."

Elena sat up straighter. "Related how?"

"I represent the estate of a man named David Mordecai. He died six weeks ago in Seattle, and his will contains some unusual instructions. He left a substantial inheritance to his heirs, but only if they can prove their descent from J. Mordecai, the original Ashmont Bay financier. Given your recent investigation into the Mordecai family history, we thought you might be able to help authenticate the family connection."

"Thomas Crane is J. Mordecai's confirmed descendant. He testified in the trials."

"Yes, and Mr. Crane is one of several people claiming to be heirs. But David Mordecai's will specifically states that Thomas Crane is not eligible to inherit. In fact, the will explicitly excludes him. Which suggests David believed there were other Mordecai descendants beyond Thomas."

"How much is the inheritance worth?"

"Forty million dollars."

Elena whistled softly. "That's enough to motivate substantial fraud. How many people are claiming to be heirs?"

"Seven so far. Three have documentation that appears legitimate on first examination. The others have more questionable evidence. I need someone who can research the Mordecai family tree thoroughly, verify each claim, and identify the true heirs."

"Ms. Reed, I'm an investigator, not a genealogist."

"But you've already done extensive research on the Mordecai family. You have documents and knowledge that would take someone else months to acquire. And frankly, Miss Voss, after your work on the Ashmont conspiracy, you have a reputation for finding the truth even when powerful people want it hidden."

Elena thought about it. Genealogical research sounded tedious compared to murder investigations, but it would be less dangerous than recent cases. And forty million dollars provided significant motive for deception and fraud.

"Send me the case file. I'll review it and let you know if I can help."

"Thank you. I'm sending it now via encrypted email. If you decide to take the case, I'll need you to come to Seattle next week to interview the claimants and examine David Mordecai's papers."

After ending the call, Elena opened the encrypted email and began reading. David Mordecai had been eighty-three when he died, a retired accountant with no spouse and no children. His will was unusual, almost cryptic.

"I leave my entire estate to the true heirs of J. Mordecai, those who can prove their descent through legitimate documentation and who have honored the Mordecai name through ethical conduct. Thomas Crane, though descended from J. Mordecai, has betrayed the family's honor by participating in the Guardians conspiracy and is therefore disinherited. Those who seek this inheritance must prove themselves worthy of the Mordecai legacy."

The will went on to specify a series of tests for potential heirs, including documentation of descent, evidence of ethical character, and answers to questions about family history that only true Mordecai descendants should know.

It was less a will than a treasure hunt designed to identify worthy heirs while excluding unworthy ones.

Elena spent the afternoon reading through the claimants' files. Three caught her attention immediately.

Sarah Mordecai, age forty-five, a teacher from Portland. She claimed to be J. Mordecai's great-great-great-granddaughter through a previously unknown illegitimate son. Her documentation included birth certificates, marriage records, and family letters. It looked legitimate, but Elena had learned to be suspicious of anything that looked too perfect.

Richard Grant, age fifty-two, a businessman from Vancouver. He claimed descent through J. Mordecai's daughter, who had supposedly married and changed her name to Grant. His documentation was thinner, mostly circumstantial connections between the Grant and Mordecai families. But he had hired expensive genealogists who swore the connection was real.

And then there was the third claimant. Anna Chen, age twenty-eight, a graduate student in history at the University of Washington. Elena nearly choked when she saw the name. Chen wasn't exactly uncommon, but combined with the university connection...

She called Detective Marcus Chen. "Do you have a cousin named Anna?"

A pause. "Yes. Anna Chen, she's getting her PhD in Pacific Northwest history. Why?"

"She's claiming to be a Mordecai heir. Says she's descended from J. Mordecai through her mother's side of the family. Did you know about this?"

"What? No. Anna never mentioned anything about the Mordecai family. Her mother's family name was Liu. How could she be connected to Mordecai?"

"That's what I'm being hired to find out. Your cousin is one of seven people claiming a forty-million-dollar inheritance from a man named David Mordecai."

"Forty million? Jesus. Elena, my family isn't wealthy. We've never had money like that. If Anna really is a Mordecai heir, it would change her life. But I can't believe she wouldn't have mentioned it during the trials, when all the Mordecai family history was being examined."

"Unless she only recently discovered the connection. Or unless she was keeping it quiet for some reason." Elena made a note. "I'm taking the case. I'm going to Seattle next week to investigate the claims. I'll need to interview Anna along with the other claimants."

"Be gentle with her. Anna's brilliant but sheltered. She's spent most of her life in academia. She wouldn't know how to navigate something like this."

"Or she's very good at appearing innocent while running a sophisticated fraud. I'll know more after I talk to her."

After ending the call, Elena continued reviewing the case files. The other four claimants had weaker documentation and less compelling stories. They seemed to be opportunists who had heard about the inheritance and decided to try their luck with fabricated genealogies.

But the three main claimants, Sarah, Richard, and Anna, all had documentation that appeared legitimate at first glance. Which meant either they were all legitimate heirs, or at least two of them were running very good frauds.

Elena spent the next week preparing for the trip to Seattle. She researched the Mordecai family tree exhaustively, going back to the documents she had collected during the Ashmont investigation. She traced J. Mordecai's known children and their descendants, building a genealogical chart that was as complete as historical records allowed.

J. Mordecai had been married once, to a woman named Evelyn, who had died in childbirth in eighteen seventy-eight. The child, a son named John, had survived and been sent east after his father's murder. That son was Thomas Crane's direct ancestor.

But there were gaps in the record. Years when J. Mordecai's whereabouts were uncertain. Rumors of relationships with other women. The possibility of illegitimate children.

If Sarah Mordecai's claim was true, if J. Mordecai had fathered an illegitimate son, that child's descendants would be legitimate heirs just as much as Thomas Crane.

And if Anna Chen's claim was true, if there was a connection through her mother's family, it would represent a previously unknown branch of the Mordecai family tree.

Elena flew to Seattle on a drizzly Monday morning. Catherine Reed met her at the law offices, a sleek modern building in downtown Seattle with views of the sound.

"Thank you for coming," Catherine said. "I've arranged meetings with the three primary claimants. You'll interview each of them, examine their documentation, and provide me with your assessment of their claims' validity."

"What happens if more than one claim is legitimate?"

"The estate is divided among all legitimate heirs. David Mordecai's will specifies that the inheritance should go to 'heirs' plural, suggesting he expected multiple valid claims."

"Or he expected fraud and wanted to make sure the real heirs weren't cheated."

"Also possible."

Elena's first interview was with Sarah Mordecai. She was a middle-aged woman with graying hair and tired eyes, dressed simply, clearly uncomfortable in the expensive law office.

"Thank you for meeting me, Miss Voss. I read about your work in Ashmont Bay. It's because of your investigation that I learned about my family connection."

"How so?"

"When the newspapers published the Mordecai family history during the trials, my mother recognized some details. She had old family stories about an ancestor who had been wealthy in the Pacific Northwest but whose fortune was lost through betrayal. She always thought they were just stories, myths families tell themselves. But when she saw the articles about J. Mordecai, she realized the stories were true."

"What documentation do you have?"

Sarah pulled out a folder. "Family Bible with birth records going back to the eighteen hundreds. Birth certificates, marriage licenses, death certificates for each generation. Letters from my great-great-grandmother describing the family's history and their connection to J. Mordecai."

Elena examined the documents carefully. They appeared authentic, aged appropriately, with details that matched historical records. But authentication would require expert analysis.

"Your claim is that you're descended from an illegitimate son of J. Mordecai?"

"Yes. According to my family's records, J. Mordecai had a relationship with a woman named Clara Morrison in eighteen seventy-three. She bore him a son, Samuel, but they never married. J. Mordecai provided some financial support but wasn't publicly involved in the child's life. After J. Mordecai was murdered in eighteen eighty-eight, the financial support stopped. Clara and Samuel struggled. The family went from modest comfort to poverty in a generation."

"Why didn't Clara or Samuel ever claim inheritance from J. Mordecai's estate?"

"They tried. But J. Mordecai's murder was covered up, his estate was absorbed by the Guardians conspiracy. By the time Samuel was old enough to make legal claims, the paperwork had been buried, the trail had gone cold. He gave up, changed his name from Morrison to Mordecai to preserve the family connection, and moved on with his life."

It was a compelling story, and the documentation supported it. But Elena had learned to be skeptical of compelling stories.

"I'll need to have these documents authenticated by experts. Assuming they're genuine, your claim appears strong."

Relief washed over Sarah's face. "Thank you. I'm not doing this for the money, Miss Voss. I mean, forty million dollars would change my life, obviously. But mainly I want acknowledgment. I want my family's history to be recognized. We've been erased, just like J. Mordecai was erased by the founding families. I want that wrong corrected."

After Sarah left, Elena met with Richard Grant. He was in his fifties, prosperous looking, clearly wealthy already. He wore an expensive suit and expensive watch, and his manner was confident bordering on arrogant.

"Miss Voss, I understand you're investigating the inheritance claims. Let me save you some time. My claim is airtight. I've hired the best genealogists money can buy, and they've confirmed my descent from J. Mordecai through his daughter Elizabeth."

"J. Mordecai's wife Evelyn died in childbirth. The records say the child was a son named John."

"The records are wrong. Or incomplete. My genealogists discovered that Evelyn actually bore twins, John and Elizabeth. Elizabeth was raised by Evelyn's sister after Evelyn's death, while John was raised by J. Mordecai. When J. Mordecai was murdered, Elizabeth was taken even further away, raised under her aunt's married name of Grant. She grew up never knowing her true father."

"That's a substantial claim with apparently no documentary evidence beyond your genealogists' say-so."

"Not true." Richard pulled out a thick folder. "Birth certificate for Elizabeth Mordecai, dated eighteen seventy-eight. Letters from Evelyn's sister discussing raising her niece. Census records showing Elizabeth Grant living with the sister's family. It's all there."

Elena examined the documents. They looked genuine, but she was skeptical. Twins when the records only mentioned one child? A hidden daughter raised under another name? It was possible, but it seemed too convenient.

"I'll have these authenticated as well. If they're genuine, you may have a valid claim."

"They're genuine. And when that's confirmed, I expect to receive my full inheritance without delay."

Richard left, and Elena sat alone for a moment, thinking. Two claimants, both with documentation that appeared legitimate, both with compelling stories. One would inherit through an illegitimate son, the other through a supposedly hidden daughter.

But only one could be telling the truth. The historical record was clear about J. Mordecai having only one child. Either Sarah or Richard was lying, or there was a truth more complicated than either claimed.

Then Anna Chen arrived for the third interview, and Elena's investigation took yet another unexpected turn.
