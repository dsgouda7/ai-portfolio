Chapter 18: The Investigation

Carla Hidalgo's apartment in Little Havana was on the fourth floor of a building that had been built in 1964 and which had been maintained with the specific care of a building whose residents tended to stay for decades and take an ownership interest in the outcome. The walls were thick. The kitchen was original. The view from the window was Calle Ocho, which meant that the view was Miami's Cuban-American soul rendered as commerce and conversation and the specific smell of coffee roasting that no amount of urban change had ever eliminated from this corridor.

She sat at her kitchen table at six in the morning with two things: a cup of cortadito and a federal subpoena.

The subpoena had arrived by courier the previous evening. It was addressed to Detective Carla Hidalgo, Miami-Dade Police Department, and it was issued by the United States District Court for the Southern District of Florida, in the matter of United States v. Reyes-Castillo et al., and it requested her testimony before a grand jury on the following Tuesday.

She drank her coffee and read the subpoena twice.

She had expected this. She had expected it in the way you expect something when you have been watching the information accumulate toward an inevitable legal conclusion. The Reyes-Castillo case was federal — kidnapping across state lines, extortion, conspiracy — and the federal case required building a complete evidentiary record, and she was part of the evidentiary record.

She was fine with this. She had done everything correctly. She had filed her reports on time, she had documented her actions, she had disclosed her working relationship with Jake Malone as required by department policy, and she had made the calls above her pay grade when she had needed to make them. She had done everything she was supposed to do and she could testify to all of it.

What she was thinking about was not the subpoena.

She was thinking about Buck Langford.

The Senator was technically not her case. He was not a Miami-Dade case at all — the jurisdictional maze of a Florida state senator's involvement in an international real estate deal with connections to a federal kidnapping was exactly the kind of thing that federal agencies were designed to handle and that local detectives were expected to provide information to and then step back from.

She had stepped back. She had provided information. She had done this correctly.

But she had spent two weeks since returning from the Keys doing the thing she always did, which was pulling every accessible thread and seeing what it connected to, and what the Langford threads connected to was something considerably larger than one senator and one development company.

She opened her notebook.

The development: Calypso Bay Development, LLC. Registered in Delaware, operating out of a Brickell address. Four principals, all of whom were attorneys or financial professionals with no direct connection to politics. Except: all four of them had also served on boards of entities that had donated to the political action committee Friends of Florida's Future, which was the primary vehicle for Senator Langford's campaign financing.

The permits: The accelerated permitting process for a development in international waters was not, in itself, unusual — the State Department had mechanisms for this, and Florida had interests in the Caribbean that required regular navigation of international regulatory frameworks. What was unusual was the specificity of the permit: it had been issued for a site that was described in the application by coordinates that placed it within five hundred meters of Cayo Espiritu. The coordinates were not public. The site had not been identified in any public record as a development opportunity. Someone had given those coordinates to the permitting application attorney.

The chain of information: The coordinates of Cayo Espiritu as a relevant location had been known, in recent years, to: (a) Arturo Vasquez-Cortez, who had derived them from the Codex and had not shared them with anyone except (b) his graduate student correspondence — but this was encrypted and was not the source — and (c) the Santiesteban family, who had their own map but had not shared it with Langford.

Which meant someone had obtained the coordinates through surveillance of Arturo's research.

Which connected to: the surveillance at La Luz de los Libros. The tap that Jake had found. The professional installation quality that suggested a level of resources above ordinary private investigation.

She had requested a warrant for the tap's technical components. The warrant had been approved. The lab analysis had come back the previous week: the components were manufactured by a company registered in Boca Raton, which subcontracted its surveillance equipment to a Miami-based private security firm, which had a client list that included — among many corporate clients — two entities that were also clients of the same law firm that had represented Calypso Bay Development in the permitting application.

This was not proof. This was connection. The difference between connection and proof was the kind of difference that took months of federal investigation to resolve, and that was not her job.

But she had documented all of it. She had written it up. She had forwarded it to the FBI field office and to the FDLE special agent who was coordinating the federal investigation. She had done this last week.

On Friday, the special agent had called her back and said: "This is very thorough."

Which meant: this is useful.

Which meant: you've done your job.

She finished her coffee and looked at Calle Ocho and thought about doing your job. She had been doing her job for fourteen years and she was good at it in the way that comes from caring enough to keep going past the point where caring becomes uncomfortable.

She thought about Jake Malone, who was not her partner and not her employee and was not technically her anything except a civilian she had worked an information exchange with, and who had done his job in exactly the way she recognized: not flashily, not with heroics, but with the steady pulling of threads until the right one moved.

She thought about calling him. Then she thought: he's off the case. He filed his report. He's sitting on his boat.

She thought: good.

She looked at her watch. Six-fifteen. She had a meeting at nine with the federal prosecutor's office to pre-prep her grand jury testimony.

She wrote three more pages in her notebook, documenting the thoughts she'd had while drinking her coffee.

She did this because you never knew which detail would matter later.

She did this because it was her job, and she was good at it, and she intended to keep being good at it.

---

The grand jury convened on Tuesday.

Carla's testimony lasted four hours. She was asked about the original missing persons report, her assessment of the kidnapping, her coordination with federal agencies, the evidence gathered at the Everglades location, and the chain of events that had led to the arrests in the Windward Passage.

She was also asked about Jake Malone.

She described him as an experienced private investigator with relevant background and operational knowledge who had provided information and logistical support in a manner consistent with the department's guidelines for civilian cooperation in active investigations.

The prosecutor asked: "Was Mr. Malone's involvement at any point outside appropriate civilian boundaries?"

Carla thought about this with the specific care she brought to precise questions.

She said: "He operated in international waters on a privately owned vessel pursuing a private contract with a client who was also the victim's family member. His actions in those waters were within the scope of what a licensed private investigator can do." She paused. "He kept me informed of his activities and shared information promptly when requested. I had no grounds and have no grounds to characterize his involvement as outside appropriate boundaries."

The prosecutor accepted this.

Then asked: "Did Mr. Malone's involvement ever take him to locations where, to your knowledge, he was in physical danger?"

She thought about an Everglades fish camp in the dark.

She thought about a cavern in the Caribbean.

She said: "In the course of a kidnapping investigation, physical danger is a known risk factor for all parties involved." She paused. "Mr. Malone is experienced in managing such risks."

Which was, she thought, the accurate version.

Afterward, in the corridor outside the federal building on Miami Avenue, she stood in the sun for a moment and took out her phone and looked at it.

She thought about calling Jake.

She called him instead.

He answered on the fourth ring. "Malone."

"Grand jury testimony's done," she said.

"How'd it go?"

"They were interested in the surveillance chain. The Langford connection."

"They should be."

"They are." She paused. "The developer's attorney is already cooperating. The law firm suspended two partners pending the investigation."

"That was fast."

"That's what happens when the federal government decides something is a priority." She paused. "The Friends of Florida's Future PAC is under audit. Campaign finance." She paused again. "Langford announced he's not seeking re-election."

"I heard."

"There's more to come." She paused. "I'm being told I'll be called back for the trial, whenever that happens."

"You'll do fine."

"I know." She paused. "Vasquez-Cortez — how is he?"

"Last I heard, he's in Seville. Working with the Simancas archive on the comparative document analysis. He's got a colleague from Havana and a team from the Vatican doing the physical excavation at the island. He sends updates."

"The archive — what are they finding?"

"Everything they expected and things they didn't expect." He paused. "Arturo says the Philip letters alone will change the Templar historical record substantially. But he says the more interesting discovery is the mundane documents — ship manifests, accounts, personal correspondence. The human record of the operation. He says that's what most historical archives are: mostly ordinary life, preserved."

She thought about this.

"That's what police files are too," she said. "Mostly ordinary life."

"Mostly." He paused. "How are you doing, Hidalgo?"

She looked at Miami Avenue in the morning. The federal building behind her. The traffic. The noise. The specific vitality of a city that was always in the process of becoming something slightly different from what it had been.

"Good," she said. "I'm good." She paused. "Got promoted."

"I heard."

"How'd you—"

"Roberto Sanchez told me. He heard it from someone in the department who heard it from someone who knows you."

She thought: Miami. A city where everyone knows someone who knows you.

"Senior detective," she said.

"You've been senior detective for ten years," Jake said. "The title just caught up."

She laughed. It was a real laugh — the kind that surprises you.

"Get back on your boat, Malone," she said.

"I'm already on it."

She looked at the city.

"Good," she said.

She hung up. She stood in the sun for another moment. Then she went back inside to file her post-testimony report.

---

Three weeks later, the Florida state attorney general's office announced that a formal investigation into the permitting process for offshore development projects had been opened. The announcement named no individuals but referenced irregularities in twelve separate applications over a four-year period.

The investigation would take eighteen months.

It would result in three indictments, two plea agreements, and a comprehensive reform of the State Department's offshore development permitting process that was subsequently cited as a model for other states.

Buck Langford was not indicted. His involvement remained, in the official record, at the level of association rather than direction. He resigned from the Senate Armed Services Committee sub-panel on coastal security, citing personal reasons.

He went back to Tallahassee.

He was not heard from significantly again.

Which was, Jake thought, when he read about it in the paper, about the right outcome for a man who had been careless about the difference between wanting something and being entitled to take it.

Not a villain. Not a hero.

Just a man who had made choices, and who was now living with the shape those choices had made.

---

The Reyes-Castillo trial was shorter than expected. Alejandro had entered the plea agreement in January, which meant that the trial that proceeded was the trial of his three associates, and that trial relied heavily on his cooperation testimony, which was precise and detailed and credible in the way of a professional who understands that precision and credibility are now his only assets.

Andrea Montaldo's trial was separate and longer and complicated by the international jurisdiction questions and by the Vatican's interest, mediated through channels that were not entirely visible in the public record, in ensuring that certain matters remained institutional rather than personal. Father Bellini appeared as a witness in a capacity that the court described as "expert witness on organizational structure" and which told the jury everything they needed to know about the Custodes Veritatis without ever naming it.

The jury deliberated for six hours.

Montaldo was convicted on the kidnapping charge. She received eight years.

Her counsel announced an appeal.

Father Bellini, reached for comment by a reporter who had somehow obtained his contact information, declined to comment on the verdict. He said only: "The Church's interest in this matter was always the safety of the archive and the welfare of those involved. We are satisfied that the legitimate authorities have reached a just result."

Which was, Jake thought, the kind of statement that took years of institutional training to produce. Saying everything and nothing at the same time, with perfect sincerity.

He clipped the article. He put it in the case file folder.

He looked at the case file folder.

He put it in the cabinet.

He closed the cabinet.

He went back to work.
