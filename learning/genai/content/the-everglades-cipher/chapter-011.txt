Chapter 11: Preparations at Big Pine Key

There is a particular kind of preparation that happens before a journey whose end is uncertain, and it is different from ordinary packing. Ordinary packing is a logistics exercise. Pre-uncertain-journey preparation is an act of faith — you are deciding what you believe you will need in circumstances you cannot fully predict, and the choices you make reflect not just practicality but character. What do you bring? What do you leave? What do you decide, in the bright light of the morning before departure, that you can do without?

Jake made his preparations on the houseboat before dawn on Thursday. He packed the briefcase with Arturo's materials — the USB drive, the translation document, the notes. He packed his personal gear in the waterproof bag he kept for exactly these situations: two changes of clothes, rain gear, his first aid kit, the knife he'd carried since New York. His recorder. A satellite-capable phone he'd borrowed from Carla Hidalgo through channels she'd pretended not to be part of. A small pair of waterproof binoculars. His legal pad.

He called Nacho at six AM.

"We're coming today," he said. "Arturo, Elena, and me. Can you be ready for a three-day run by tomorrow morning?"

A pause from the Keys end. Not surprise — Nacho had been waiting for this call, he could tell. The pause was the adjustment from expectation to reality, from thirty-one years of preparation to the moment of actual departure.

"Ready by tonight," Nacho said. "I've had La Determinada fueled and provisioned for a week. I've been ready since you called me."

"What's the weather?"

"Window is opening. The forecast shows three days of clean conditions after tomorrow, then a tropical wave. We go tomorrow, we have three days before it gets complicated." He paused. "That's enough. The cavern dive, the archive — three days is enough if everything goes right."

Jake thought about how often everything went right.

"And if it doesn't?" he asked.

"Then we improvise. That's what boats are for." He paused. "One thing. I want to bring someone."

"Who?"

"My daughter. Sofia." A pause. "She's in Miami. Georgetown on summer break. She's been asking about this for three years." Another pause. "She's twenty-two. She's a certified rescue diver, better than I am in the water. If we're going into that cavern, we need the best diver available."

Jake thought about Elena saying she wanted to be there when it was opened.

"It's your boat," Jake said.

"It's my boat," Nacho agreed.

"Bring Sofia. We leave tomorrow morning, six AM."

He ended the call and looked at the houseboat. At the bay. At the city behind it, going about its relentless morning business.

He called Elena. She was awake — she had been awake, he suspected, most of the night.

"We go tomorrow," he said. "Drive to Big Pine Key tonight. Tell Arturo."

A pause. "He'll argue."

"He'll go."

"He'll argue and then go," she corrected.

"Tell him to pack light. Nacho will have everything we need for diving on the boat." He paused. "How is he?"

"Better. He slept eleven hours. He's been at my kitchen table since five AM, working on the final cipher section." A pause. "He says he's close. He says the Phrygian mode resolution — I'm just quoting, I don't entirely follow the music theory — is holding but there are two symbols he can't fully place without being at the island."

"Tell him he'll be at the island in three days."

"He's already planning three days ahead. He was also up at five writing a formal request to the Smithsonian's maritime history department and the Cuban National Archives about what procedure applies to the recovery of pre-Columbian maritime materials in what might be Cuban-adjacent waters." A pause. "He's worried about doing this right."

"Good. He should be."

"Jake." A pause. "The Montaldo people. They know he escaped. They'll be looking."

"Carla has an FBI team on the boat at Dinner Key. If the Carità moves, they'll know."

"If the Carità moves. What if they have a different boat?"

Jake thought about this. "Then we deal with it when we know."

He heard her take a breath. "All right. We'll be there by six tonight."

---

The drive to Big Pine Key from Miami in the late afternoon was the drive that Jake associated with the specific sensation of leaving one world for another. The Overseas Highway south of Homestead was not just road — it was a commitment. You were going somewhere that had water on both sides and no turning back that wasn't a deliberate reversal. The keys got smaller as you went, the continental shelf thinning under you to the coral substrate and the limestone and the ancient reef architecture, and the sky got bigger, and the light flattened to the horizontal gold of late afternoon over flat water.

He drove behind Elena's car, and Elena drove behind the car she had rented, and Arturo was in Elena's passenger seat, and Jake watched the two of them through their rear window occasionally — Arturo's white hair and Arturo's deliberate-by-now-familiar gestures as he talked, animated, the way people talk when they have been silent for too long.

He thought about Carla Hidalgo's words: you're planning to go to the island.

He thought about the formal architecture of the law, which he had spent twelve years in New York building and which had ultimately, at the end, been unable to hold the weight that had been placed on it. He thought about the cases he had worked where the system had done exactly what it was designed to do, had found the evidence, built the case, brought the charges, secured the conviction. And he thought about the cases where the system had failed not through corruption but through limitation — through the gap between what the law could reach and what the world contained.

This case was the second kind.

The archive on Cayo Espiritu was outside any jurisdiction he recognized. The people pursuing it included a private intelligence contractor working for a Vatican-linked organization, a family criminal enterprise with sixty years of operational history, and whatever Calypso Bay Development and Buck Langford represented when you followed the money all the way back.

Against all of this: one man who had spent thirty years on a translation. His daughter who had kept her own knowledge in reserve for years. A Keys captain with a thirty-one-year running promise to his grandfather. And Jake Malone, who had come to Miami to be less than he had been in New York and had, apparently, failed at this ambition.

He had a satellite phone. He had Carla Hidalgo making calls above her pay grade. He had the translation document and the Codex copies and Nacho's grandfather's notes and thirty years of Arturo's scholarship.

He thought: it'll have to be enough.

He pulled into Nacho's boat yard at six-fifteen with the setting sun laying gold over the Gulf side and the no-see-ums doing their evening work and the smell of salt water and diesel and the particular richness of mangrove tidal flat that the Keys have at this hour.

---

Sofia Reyes was twenty-two years old and looked, at first impression, like she had been assembled by two entirely different design teams that had compromised well. She had her father's build — broad-shouldered, compact, the physical ease of someone at home in physical environments — and her mother's face, which Nacho had told Jake once was a Mexican-American woman from the Texas border who had come to the Keys in 1993 and left in 2009 when the Keys and she couldn't reach an agreement about permanent commitment. Sofia had studied at Georgetown for two years, which had given her a specific quality of articulateness that sat interestingly on top of a Keys-bred directness.

She was finishing rigging the dive equipment on the stern of La Determinada when they pulled in. She looked up at Jake with her father's evaluating squint.

"You're the Miami detective," she said.

"Yes."

"You're planning to take my father to an island where multiple criminal organizations have active interests."

"Also yes."

She nodded, with the nod of someone who has processed the risk and made a decision about it already. "I'm the best diver on this boat. We do this in open water with no decompression stops unless necessary. I lead the cavern dive. Those aren't negotiations."

"They're not negotiations," Jake agreed.

She went back to the rigging. He watched her work for a moment — she was efficient and systematic, the equipment check of someone who knew exactly what could kill you and preferred to know it in advance.

Arturo and Elena came up behind Jake. Arturo stopped at the dock and looked at La Determinada with the expression Jake had come to associate with his specific variety of wonder — the calibrated wonder of a man who checks the emotion against the evidence and finds the emotion justified.

"She's a good boat," Arturo said.

Nacho was on the stern talking to Sofia. He heard Arturo's voice and turned. The two men looked at each other across the dock.

They had never met in person. Five years of correspondence, a conference introduction in 2019 that had led to an eight-year research partnership, the sharing of grandfather's notes and expedition records and the developing translation — all of this through letters and calls and emails.

Nacho stepped off the boat onto the dock.

They shook hands. Then — because Nacho was the kind of man whose physical impulses were honest rather than performative — he pulled Arturo into a brief, serious embrace. Arturo, who was the kind of man who received such things with precision, let it happen, and then stepped back.

"It's real," Arturo said.

"It's real," Nacho said.

"You've heard it."

"Three times. It sounds exactly the way Diego described it." Nacho paused. "More than that. It sounds like it's been waiting."

Arturo put on his glasses. He looked at the boat. He looked at Nacho. He looked at Elena, who was watching this with the expression of a scientist observing something she hadn't hypothesized.

"Then let's go find out what it's been waiting to say," he said.

---

They ate dinner in the boat yard's small common area — a picnic table, the cooler, grilled fish that Nacho made with the complete competence of a man who has been grilling fish since adolescence. The conversation moved through the logistics of the voyage (three days south, through the Straits of Florida, skirting Cuban territorial waters, entering the Windward Passage from the east), the technical details of the dive (the cavern entrance at low tide, the chamber depth, the equipment requirements), and then, as the evening got later and the beer count rose, into the history.

Arturo talked about Diego de Almeida the way you talk about someone you have spent decades knowing intimately without ever meeting. He described the voice of the Codex — the quality of Diego's Latin, which was excellent but betrayed, in specific grammatical constructions, an education that had included Arabic and Iberian Jewish scholars. He described the two voyages as Diego had described them: the first in the urgency of the dissolution, the second in the considered patience of a man who had survived the first and had time to do it more carefully.

"What do you think they deposited?" Sofia asked. She had the direct question of someone not concerned with diplomatic framing. "What's actually down there?"

Arturo looked at his hands. "The Templar archive. Not everything — not the complete record of the order's existence, which was extensive and distributed across multiple locations, much of which has been recovered in fragments over the centuries." He paused. "What went with the most important courier on the most urgent voyage, in the weeks after the arrests in Paris, is the material that was specifically dangerous to specific people." He looked up. "The order kept records of everything. It was their institutional culture — everything documented, verified, witnessed. What Philip IV most wanted destroyed were the records of his own dealings with the order, which had been extensive and which included transactions that were, to put it diplomatically, not consistent with his public positions."

"Financial records," Elena said.

"Financial, yes. But also correspondence. Agreements. The kinds of documents that, in 1307, would have meant the destruction of several powerful dynasties if they became public." He paused. "The Church had its own interests. The Templars had been granted extraordinary privileges by a series of popes. The terms of those privileges, and the ways in which the Templars had accumulated information about the Church's affairs in exchange for their services — this was the kind of institutional memory that the Church would very much have preferred to dispose of quietly."

"Seven hundred years of quietly," Nacho said.

"The documents were only dangerous while the people they documented had successors and inheritors," Arturo said. "But some institutions have very long succession lines. The dynamics of 1307 are not entirely irrelevant to the dynamics of today." He paused. "This is what the Custodes Veritatis — if that's what the organization is called — understands. The archive doesn't threaten living individuals. But it threatens institutional narratives. Institutional authority. The stories institutions tell about themselves." He looked at Jake. "Which is why they want it. Not to publish it — to control it."

Jake thought about this. About the woman on the dock in the photograph — Andrea Montaldo — and the three fractured ribs she'd left behind.

"If we reach the archive first," he said, "what happens to it?"

Arturo looked at him directly. "I've been thinking about this for a long time. My instinct, and Constance Fairweather's position, is that it should go to an institution with the resources and mandate to preserve, study, and eventually publish it. The Biblioteca Nacional in Madrid, possibly, or the Vatican Library itself — which is complicated but has the greatest contextual expertise — with conditions. Published findings. Open access to peer review. No institutional suppression of findings." He paused. "There is a protocol for exactly this kind of discovery. Cultural heritage law, the UNESCO conventions. It requires cooperation between the country of the archive's origin — Portugal, possibly Spain — and the country of its current geographic location, which in international waters is complex." He looked at Jake. "I've written to legal experts. I've been preparing a framework. I knew, when the translation was nearing completion, that this question was coming."

"And the island itself?" Jake said.

Arturo looked at him with the expression of a man who has a specific preference and is deciding whether to state it.

"The island has been uninhabited for centuries," he said. "Its ecology — the reef, the coastal structure, the cavern system — is intact because nothing has been developed there. It should stay that way." He paused. "Whatever commercial interests the Santiesteban family may have — I understand them, I don't dismiss the sixty-year wait and the claim of historical engagement — the island should not be developed. If there is any arrangement to be made, it should be conservation. Not resort infrastructure."

Nacho was nodding.

"My grandfather's biggest fear," Nacho said, "when he told me about the island — was not that nobody would find it. He was afraid that whoever found it would build something on it." He looked at his beer. "A man who spent his life on the water knows what happens to places that get built on."

Sofia looked at her father. "The Keys," she said.

"Yes," Nacho said. "The Keys."

Elena was watching Arturo with the specific expression that Jake had been watching her develop over the past few days — the expression of seeing someone clearly, possibly for the first time. Not a stranger. The opposite. Someone she had always known in one dimension becoming three-dimensional.

"You've been building toward this for thirty years," she said.

"Yes."

"The shop. The books. All of it in service of this."

"Not only this. The shop is what it is — it's not a front, it's not a means to an end. I genuinely love those books." He looked at her. "But yes. After 1989, when I found the Codex — yes. The research became a project alongside the shop, parallel to it, and then, over time, primary." He paused. "I didn't tell you because I didn't want to burden you. Because scholars' obsessions are not always inherited safely." He looked at her. "And because a part of me was afraid that if I told you and you thought it was nonsense—"

"I wouldn't have thought it was nonsense," she said.

"You would have been correct to check the evidence carefully," he said. "You are a scientist. I am not. The discipline is different." A pause. "But you would have been gentle about it. You always are." He looked at his hands. "I was afraid of your gentleness."

She was quiet.

"That's an unusual thing to be afraid of," she said finally.

"It is. But gentleness, when applied to something you've built over thirty years, can be the hardest kind of scrutiny." He looked at her. "I needed to be certain first. I'm certain now."

She nodded. Then she reached across the picnic table and took his hand, briefly, the specific gesture of a daughter who has been practicing emotional restraint so long she's gotten good at the controlled version.

"Tell me everything," she said. "From the beginning. The whole thirty years. Tonight."

They were there until midnight, and Jake sat at the far end of the table and listened to Arturo tell it — all of it, the Seville sale, the first careful attempts at translation, the slow decoding of the cipher, the correspondence with Fairweather, the growing certainty, the Santiesteban contact and the breakdown of that relationship, the preparations and the protections and the days in the fish camp — and it was, Jake thought, one of the more remarkable things he had heard.

Not because of the history. Because of the way a man told thirty years of private obsession to his daughter at a picnic table in Big Pine Key, with the Gulf side water visible in the dark and the smell of fish and salt and two-cycle engines from the boat yard, and the daughter listened with her whole attention, the way scientists listen when the hypothesis is finally being explained: looking for the flaws, finding them smaller than she feared.

At midnight they went to their berths on La Determinada. Jake lay in the bow cabin with the boat's movement and the sound of water and thought about the shape of the next three days.

He thought: if everything goes right.

He thought: nothing ever goes entirely right.

He thought: but sometimes it goes right enough.

He turned off the light and let the Keys rock him to sleep.
