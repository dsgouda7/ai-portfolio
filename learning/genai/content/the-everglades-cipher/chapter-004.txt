Chapter 4: Locker 44

The Coral Gables Bus Terminal was a building that had been designed in one era and maintained in another, which is to say that it had the good bones of 1950s civic ambition and the surface patina of chronic underfunding. The main concourse was cool and high-ceilinged with a terrazzo floor worn to a comfortable dullness by seventy years of feet. The storage lockers were in a side corridor off the main hall, fifty blue metal units ranging from luggage-sized to the kind of generous cabinet depth that suggested the architects had been thinking optimistically about the scale of human belongings.

Locker 44 was in the middle row, head height, accessible without bending.

Jake had driven there via three deliberate wrong turns — an old habit for occasions when you want to see who, if anyone, is following you. Nobody was following him, or if they were, they were better at it than the people who typically followed him, who tended to be either amateurs with conspicuous rental cars or professionals who had the sense to use multiple vehicles, and the Coral Gables morning traffic hadn't accommodated the latter.

He stood for a moment in the corridor and looked at the locker and at the other lockers and at the two people he could see from his position: a middle-aged woman with an enormous rolling suitcase loading it into a lower locker, and a young man in a food delivery uniform leaning against the wall looking at his phone. Neither was paying him any attention.

He opened locker 44.

Inside was a soft-sided briefcase of the kind carried by academics and architects. It was green canvas with leather trim, well-worn. Jake lifted it out — it was heavy, perhaps four kilos — and set it on the floor and unzipped it.

Inside were five things.

The first was a USB drive in a small plastic case labeled, in Arturo's handwriting: FOTOS CODEX ALTA RES. High-resolution photographs of the Codex pages.

The second was a bound notebook — thicker than the Moleskine he'd left on his desk. Arturo's handwriting filled it from cover to cover. The first page bore the title: NOTAS DE TRADUCCIÓN — CÓDICE ALMEIDA — A. VASQUEZ-CORTEZ — 2018-2023. Five years of translation notes.

The third was a printed document, approximately thirty pages, stapled in the top left corner. On the cover page: THE ALMEIDA CODEX: A PRELIMINARY TRANSLATION AND COMMENTARY. By Arturo Vasquez-Cortez, MA (Archival Studies), and Dr. Constance Fairweather, D.Phil. (Medieval History), University of Oxford. Unpublished draft, June 2023.

Jake looked at this for a moment. A joint paper. Arturo and Dr. Fairweather had been working together formally.

The fourth thing was a sealed envelope with CARLA written on the outside in block letters. Nothing else. Jake recognized from the weight that there was a small object inside in addition to whatever was written.

The fifth thing was a folded piece of paper, no envelope, addressed simply: Para el detective. For the detective.

Jake sat down on the floor of the locker corridor with his back against the lockers opposite and read the letter.

---

The letter was four pages, handwritten in blue ink on cream paper, in the careful upright hand of someone who learned to write when penmanship was taught as a discipline:

My dear investigator —

If you are reading this, then what I feared has happened, and Elena has done what I asked her to do by going to someone she trusts. I hope she chose well. I believe she will.

I am writing this letter on a Sunday afternoon in my apartment above the shop. It is raining, which I have always found conducive to writing honest letters. The Bocachico is playing on the radio. I have a glass of rum beside me that I am making last, because I find that the quality of thinking deteriorates with the second rum and I need to think well tonight.

I want to tell you what this is about, because you will be going into something without a map, and maps, as I have spent my life learning, are the difference between finding a thing and walking in circles around it.

In 1989 I acquired a manuscript at a sale in Seville. At the time I paid three hundred dollars for it — I was there for other materials and this was an impulse purchase, the kind you make when something in a box catches the light in a particular way and you can't leave it. The seller had no idea what it was. Neither did I, entirely, but I knew it was old and I knew it was in more than one language.

What I eventually understood — and this took twenty years of intermittent work — is that this document was made by a man named Diego de Almeida, a Portuguese navigator and, for a period in his late twenties, a courier for the Knights Templar. He made this document in approximately 1315, eight years after the dissolution of the Order in France, during what was apparently a long period of waiting in Lisbon. It is partly a narrative account of two voyages he made — one in 1307-1308 and one in 1312-1313 — and partly a record of something he was trying to preserve for what he called "the time when it is safe to remember."

The narrative is in a mixture of Latin and Portuguese. Layered over it is the cipher.

The cipher took me another eight years to begin to understand. It uses Arabic month names as a key — something no one would expect from a Portuguese navigator working for a Roman Catholic order. I believe Diego chose this key precisely because of its unexpectedness. The Arab world had the best mathematical tradition of the age, and Diego had evidently been educated by someone with a broad conception of knowledge.

When the cipher resolves, it describes a location.

Specifically: an island in the Caribbean — Diego calls it Isla de los Espíritus, the Island of Spirits — which he reached in 1307-1308 with a party from the Order. At this island, in underwater caverns that he describes as "the vault of the deep place," they deposited something that he calls "lo que debe preservarse," "that which must be preserved." He is deliberately vague about what this is. But from the context — the urgency of the voyage, the identities of the knights he names, the level of care taken — it is clear that this was of extraordinary importance to the Order.

I believe they deposited the Templar treasury. Not the financial treasury — not the order's money, which was distributed, hidden, and eventually discovered and lost over subsequent centuries. But the documentary treasury. The archive. The records that Philip IV of France was most desperate to destroy because they documented things he had done and things others had done that would have meant the end of several powerful dynasties.

Dr. Fairweather, when I shared this with her, told me I was probably correct. She told me this with the expression of a woman receiving bad news she had expected. She said: the problem with this discovery is not finding it — the problem is what happens when powerful people learn it exists.

She was already worried, I think, about the people who had found out about my work. Dr. Fairweather moves in academic circles, but she is not naive about the relationship between scholarship and power. She said she had been contacted — she didn't say by whom, only that the contact was made through indirect channels — by someone asking questions about the provenance of certain Templar documents. She said it felt like someone doing reconnaissance.

The large man with the scar who came to my shop is named — or at least represents interests associated with the name — Santiesteban. Magdalena Santiesteban. I believe she knows about my research because she has her own family history with it. Her grandfather, a man named Ramon Santiesteban, was involved in Caribbean land development in the 1930s. There is a real estate transaction in the archives from 1938 involving an island in the waters between Cuba and Haiti that Ramon Santiesteban acquired and attempted to develop, and then sold, at a loss, two years later. I believe he found evidence of the cache and could not access it, and that his granddaughter has been watching the scholarly world for anyone who might succeed where he failed.

She wants the archive. Not for scholarship. For leverage. For what it contains about the ancestors of the powerful.

There is someone else, too. I am less certain here. But I believe there is a Vatican connection to Dr. Fairweather that she has not been fully honest with me about. The Catholic Church has its own interests in Templar archives — the history of the suppression of the Order is a complex one, and there are elements of it that certain factions within the Church would very much prefer remained unexamined.

The coordinates of the island are embedded in the Codex — not explicitly, but through the cipher, which resolves to a set of measurements that I have cross-referenced with modern cartographic data. The island exists. It is a small uninhabited island in the Windward Passage, south of Cuba and north of Haiti. I have not told anyone its name except Dr. Fairweather.

What I need you to do is find who took me, and what they want to do with the information I have. If I am merely being held somewhere while people negotiate, I am probably alive. If they have determined that my death is simpler than my negotiation, then I am probably not. Either way, what matters is the Codex. It must not fall into the hands of people who want it for power rather than knowledge.

Elena does not know any of this. I have been protecting her — she has her own work, her own life, and the history of men dragging their children into dangerous obsessions is not a history I want to contribute to. If she is involved now, it is because circumstances have made it necessary, not because I chose it. Please take care of her.

The envelope marked CARLA is for Detective Hidalgo. Give it to her. I trust her, which I realize is a significant thing to say about a police officer, but she is honest and she is careful and in this situation honest and careful are worth more than powerful.

The translation document — the one co-written with Dr. Fairweather — is incomplete. We did not finish the final section of the Codex. There is a page, the last page, that neither of us has been fully able to resolve. The cipher on that page is different from the earlier sections — I believe it is a second cipher, a verification cipher, that only makes sense if you know something about the island itself. Something you can only know by being there.

Good luck, investigator. Find whoever has me. And please — if any of this ends badly for me — tell Elena that the bookshop is hers, and that the books are never just books. They are what we remember when we choose to remember.

Arturo Vasquez-Cortez
October 12, 2023

---

Jake read the letter twice. Then he sat for a moment with it in his hands and looked at the wall of the locker corridor without seeing it.

Four kilos of implication.

A medieval archive. The Templar treasury, but of documents rather than gold. People who had been circling the edges of this for years. The Santiesteban family with a 1938 land deal that told its own story. A Vatican connection to the Oxford historian. A cipher that needed geographic confirmation. An island in the Windward Passage.

And Arturo Vasquez-Cortez, who had apparently spent thirty years of his life working toward this and had, in the final approach, been taken by someone who moved faster.

Jake put the letter carefully in the briefcase with the other materials, zipped it shut, and stood up.

He walked out of the terminal into the Coral Gables morning and called Elena.

"I have the briefcase. Are you home?"

"Still at the library. Jake, what's in it?"

"A letter from your father. Some documents. We need to talk."

A long pause. "Is he—"

"It's not that kind of letter. He wrote it in advance, as a precaution. He's — as of when he wrote it, he was fine, and preparing carefully." Jake walked toward the Bronco. "He said he was protecting you."

Another pause. When she spoke again, her voice had a texture to it that was neither grief nor anger but something in between, the specific emotion of learning that someone you love has been keeping something from you for your benefit, and not being certain yet whether to be grateful or furious.

"He's been doing that since I was twelve years old," she said.

Jake got into the Bronco. "Meet me at Café Sonia in two hours. I need to make one stop first."

He drove to Miami-Dade Police headquarters on NW 25th Street, where Detective Carla Hidalgo had a desk in Violent Crimes on the third floor, and he signed in and went up and found her at the desk eating what appeared to be a breakfast empanada with the focused efficiency of someone eating between tasks.

Carla Hidalgo was thirty-eight years old and had the build and demeanor of a woman who had decided, at some point in her career, that she would not perform approachability and that anyone who had a problem with this was welcome to bring it up. She had dark hair with a silver streak above her left temple that had been there since she was twenty-five, and a desk that was organized with the completeness of someone who finds disorder inefficient rather than comfortable. She had a master's degree in criminology from FIU and she had made detective at thirty-two, which in Miami-Dade is fast.

She looked at Jake when he sat down with the expression of a woman who had been slightly expecting this and had prepared a small, spare welcome.

"Malone," she said.

"Hidalgo. The Vasquez-Cortez case."

"Elena Vasquez-Cortez hired you."

"Yesterday."

"I thought she might." She finished the empanada. "What did you find this morning?"

He put the envelope on her desk. "From Arturo. He left it at the Coral Gables Bus Terminal six weeks ago. There's something inside plus whatever he wrote." He watched her pick it up with gloved hands — she kept them in her desk drawer, he noticed, which was either habitual caution or she had been expecting something. "He mentioned you by name in a letter he wrote to me. He said you were honest."

She looked up at him briefly. Then she looked at the envelope. "What else?"

"The missing man knew something about early Caribbean history that several parties have been trying to get hold of. The large Cuban man with the scar — Elena's neighbor described him. Roberto Sanchez thinks the family is Santiesteban. There's an Oxford historian named Constance Fairweather who flew to Miami three days before Arturo disappeared and may not have left." Jake folded his hands. "The fireproof cabinet in the back room of his shop has been cleaned out. Not by the police. Before or after he disappeared."

Carla looked at him with the evaluation she gave most things. "You've been busy since last night."

"He prepared carefully. He deserves careful investigation." Jake stood. "The envelope is yours. What's in it — I'd appreciate knowing, when you can share. I think he knew something about people who don't like to be known about."

Carla picked up the envelope. "You'll stay out of my investigation."

"I'll let you know anything I find."

"That's not the same as staying out of it."

"No," Jake agreed, "it isn't."

He left her to the envelope and drove back south, and thought about Arturo Vasquez-Cortez writing a letter on a rainy Sunday afternoon with the Bocachico playing and a rum he was making last, and writing it calmly and precisely and with care for the people who would have to read it after things had gone wrong.

That took a kind of courage that Jake recognized.

The courage of a person who doesn't believe in their own luck anymore but keeps working anyway.

HISTORICAL INTERLUDE — Lisbon, 1315

Diego de Almeida was thirty-one years old when he began to write down what he had seen and done.

He wrote in the library of a merchant named Gonçalo, who had been his father's friend and his own patron since the age of fifteen. The library was a high-ceilinged room on the second floor of a house near the river, and through its tall windows Diego could see the Tagus running gray and silver on winter mornings, and the ships, always the ships, moving on the water with the patient deliberation that had become the grammar of his life.

He had made the second voyage in 1312-1313 — five years after the first. The Order had by then been formally dissolved across most of Europe; in Portugal, the king had reconstituted the knights as the Order of Christ, which kept the form while pretending to change the substance, and under the cover of this reorganization certain activities had continued that Philip of France and his pope would not have approved. Diego had gone back to the island. He had added to what was there. He had not, this time, been told what he was adding, though he had understood from context that he was delivering something that had been in transit since the dissolution — something that had moved across several countries and several years and several hands and had finally reached its destination by a route that no one who had not been present at each stage could follow.

He sat now with his pen and his parchment and his knowledge, and he wrote. He wrote in Latin and Portuguese, interleaved, in the way he thought — in two languages simultaneously, the way bilingual people often think, with the two idioms enriching each other. He wrote the account of both voyages, the names of the men who had made them, the things he had carried, the place he had gone.

And then he stopped.

He stopped because he understood, as he had understood since Marseille, that the less that was known the longer people lived. He had watched too many men die for knowing things in the eight years since the dissolution. He was not interested in adding to that number, and specifically he was not interested in adding himself.

So he took what he had written and he did something to it.

The cipher was his own invention — or rather, it was a combination of things he had learned from sources that most Latin scholars of his day would not have expected to be in conversation: Arabic mathematics, the numerical symbolism of kabbalah (he had spent time in Lisbon's Jewish quarter, where the scholarship was better than anywhere in Christendom), and a structural key taken from the pattern of Arabic months, because he had known since he was a boy that people looked for patterns in the familiar and ignored the foreign.

He built the cipher in three stages. The first stage scrambled the geography — the coordinates of the island were embedded in what appeared to be a navigation record of a different voyage entirely. The second stage scrambled the identity of the cargo — references to it were replaced with the word "provisions," a word so ordinary as to be invisible. The third stage — the verification cipher, the one that could only be resolved by someone who had been to the island — was built around a physical feature of the place that no document could convey. This feature was something that Diego had noted and that he knew he would remember until he died: the quality of the sound the water made in the cavern under the eastern cliff at high tide.

He embedded a description of this sound, in a form that required having heard it to understand.

He folded the completed document and sealed it with wax and gave it to Gonçalo, with instructions.

Then he went back to sea, because the sea was the place where he had always been least afraid, and because the sea, unlike the land, did not keep records of what it had witnessed.

He died in 1349, in Lisbon, in the great plague that killed a third of Europe. He was sixty-five years old, which for his era was a long time, and he had spent most of it on water.

The document passed through four hands after his death before it was sold, in 1402, to a Castilian merchant in Seville, who found it illegible and kept it in a chest for three generations until his great-granddaughter donated it to a church library in 1511, where it was catalogued as "document of unknown origin, possibly navigation record, Latin/Portuguese mixture, illegible annotations." It stayed in that library for four hundred and seventy-eight years.

In 1989, a Cuban-American bookseller named Arturo Vasquez-Cortez bought it for three hundred dollars at a Seville library sale, carried it back to Miami on a plane, and put it on the shelf in his back room under a lamp.

For the first time in six hundred and seventy-four years, the Codex Almeida was in the possession of someone who was likely, eventually, to understand it.
