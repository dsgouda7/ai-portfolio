Chapter 5: First Dive

The research vessel rocked gently in the morning swell, anchored over the coordinates from her father's most recent logs. Claire checked her equipment one more time: underwater camera, temperature probe, water sampling bottles, sonar receiver. Basic tools for basic science, but more than her father had possessed.

Jake stood at the helm, monitoring weather reports. Vernon Shaw had provided the sampling equipment at cost. Three lobstermen worked alongside, men who had known her father and wanted to help honor his memory. And Maya was documenting everything, camera and notebook in hand, learning to be a witness to discovery.

"Water's at 11.7 Celsius," Claire called out, reading the thermometer. "That's consistent with Dad's readings from last month. Two degrees warmer than forty years ago."

"All that in forty years?" one of the lobstermen, Danny Frost, shook his head. "No wonder the lobsters are moving."

"The Gulf of Maine is warming faster than almost anywhere else on Earth," Claire explained. "The combination of the Gulf Stream shift and atmospheric changes creates unique conditions here. We're seeing ecosystem collapse in real-time."

She lowered the water sampling bottles, collecting at different depths. Each sample would be tested for temperature, salinity, pH, dissolved oxygen, nutrient levels. Standard oceanographic data, but done over time it could reveal patterns.

"Your father used to do this every week," Vernon said. "Same spots, same measurements. He built quite a dataset."

"How much data?"

"Forty years of weekly samples. That's over two thousand data points per location. He worked six locations around the island."

Claire felt a chill that had nothing to do with the ocean air. "That's... that's actually a significant dataset. Properly analyzed, that could show long-term trends that satellite data miss."

"Tom knew what he was doing. He wasn't formally trained, but he understood science. He understood the value of consistency and patience."

The sonar pinged steadily, showing the seafloor 240 feet below. This was deep water for the Gulf of Maine, part of the trench system that made these waters so productive. Claire watched the screen, seeing the irregular bottom topology, the rocky outcrops where lobsters sheltered.

"There," Danny said suddenly, pointing at the sonar. "See that?"

Claire looked. A large mass, moving slowly across the screen. Too large for a fish school, too organized for floating debris.

"Could be a whale," Jake said, but his voice was uncertain.

"Wrong shape. Wrong movement pattern." Claire adjusted the sonar gain, trying to get a clearer image. The mass was elongated, maybe sixty feet long, moving parallel to the bottom. As she watched, it turned sharply, moving with precision that marine animals didn't usually display.

"I'm going to try the underwater camera," Claire said, attaching it to a long cable. "If it's still in the area, we might get visual confirmation."

She lowered the camera, watching the monitor as it descended through increasingly dark water. At fifty feet, ambient light was already dim. At one hundred feet, the camera's LED lights created a small bubble of visibility in the gloom. Fish drifted past, startled by the unusual presence.

At 180 feet, the water temperature suddenly dropped. Claire watched the thermometer reading plummet from 11.7 to 7.3 Celsius in the span of twenty feet.

"That's not normal," she said. "That's a four-degree drop in a very narrow layer."

"Thermocline?" Jake suggested.

"Not at this depth, not this time of year. This is something else." She adjusted the camera angle, peering into the darkness beyond the lights' reach. Shapes moved there, undefined but definitely present.

The camera descended further. At 200 feet, she saw it.

A shape in the water, massive and dark, covered in what looked like marine growth but wasn't. The camera lights reflected off surfaces that seemed metallic, angular in ways that natural forms weren't. As Claire watched, a section of the object illuminated from within, panels lighting with a soft blue-green glow.

"Are you seeing this?" she breathed.

Everyone crowded around the monitor. Maya filmed the screen, capturing the impossible image.

The object moved, rotating slightly. More lights appeared, arranged in patterns too regular to be bioluminescence. The camera caught a clear view of what might be a surface feature: geometric ridges, evenly spaced, deliberately designed.

Then it was gone, moving out of frame faster than the camera could track. The water roiled in its wake, turbulence visible on the monitor. The temperature reading spiked back to normal.

"Did we just..." Danny couldn't finish the sentence.

"We need to get that footage onto multiple devices immediately," Claire said, hands shaking as she rewound the camera. "Maya, upload it to the cloud. Jake, can you copy it to your phone? Vernon, put it on the laptop."

They worked quickly, copying the thirty seconds of footage to every device available. Claire felt the weight of what they had just captured. Visual evidence of something that shouldn't exist. Proof that her father wasn't crazy, wasn't mistaken, wasn't alone in his observations.

"What do we do with this?" Jake asked.

"We analyze it properly. Frame by frame. Get expert opinions on what we're seeing. But we also protect it, make sure copies exist in multiple locations so it can't be disappeared."

"Should we tell people? Post it online?"

Claire hesitated. "Not yet. This is extraordinary evidence, which means we need extraordinary verification. If we post it prematurely, it'll be dismissed as fake, CGI, a hoax. We need to build a case first, document methodology, establish credibility."

"Tom would say the same," Vernon agreed. "He always said proper documentation was everything. Show your work, prove your methods, don't give them any excuse to dismiss you."

They collected more samples, took additional sonar readings, documented everything with meticulous detail. But everyone's attention kept returning to those thirty seconds of footage, the impossible thing captured on camera.

By early afternoon, they headed back to harbor. Claire's mind raced with implications. If what they had filmed was real—and every instinct said it was—then everything about Willowport's environmental situation was more complex than anyone imagined. This wasn't just about warming waters and developer greed. There was something else at play, something that had been here far longer than human settlement.

"What do you think it is?" Maya asked as they approached the dock.

"I don't know. Something built, clearly. Those geometric patterns, the lights. It's engineered. But by whom? For what purpose?"

"Could it be human? A submarine or underwater research station?"

"Not like any submarine I've ever seen. The size is wrong, the shape is wrong, and the way it moved through the water was more fluid than mechanical."

"Alien?" Maya's voice held equal parts excitement and fear.

"That's a big leap. Let's not jump to conclusions. We have evidence of something unusual. That's all we can say for certain."

They docked and secured the boat. Claire carried the equipment, mind already planning the next steps. Analysis of the footage, consultation with image specialists, research into similar phenomena. This would be months of work, maybe years. But it was important work, the kind that could reshape understanding.

A figure waited on the dock. Richard Caldwell, the developer's representative. He was alone, no lawyers or assistants, which made Claire more nervous rather than less.

"Dr. Merritt. I heard you were out collecting samples. Making progress on your environmental assessment?"

"Yes, thank you. Excuse me, I need to get this equipment—"

"I'd like to talk to you. Privately, if possible."

Claire looked at Jake, who nodded slightly. She followed Caldwell to a bench overlooking the water, out of earshot but still visible to her friends.

"I want to make you an offer," Caldwell said. "Seabrook Development will fund your research. Full support, access to the best equipment, connection to top laboratories. All we ask is that you approach the assessment objectively."

"I'm already approaching it objectively."

"Of course. But you understand, I hope, that your father's opinions may have colored your initial impressions. We'd like to ensure you have access to multiple perspectives."

"Are you trying to buy me?"

"I'm trying to support good science. Dr. Merritt, we're not the villains here. We want responsible development that benefits the community. Your research can help us understand how to do that properly."

"For a price. What do you really want, Mr. Caldwell?"

His expression hardened slightly. "I want you to understand that this project is happening, with or without your approval. The question is whether you'll be part of the conversation or an obstacle to progress. Obstacles can be... managed."

"Is that a threat?"

"It's reality. You're a single researcher with limited resources, investigating waters that have been studied extensively by professionals. If you find something unusual, something that doesn't fit established models, you'll need significant backing to be taken seriously. We can provide that backing. Or we can ensure that your work is viewed as biased advocacy from a grieving daughter."

Claire stood. "I don't need your backing, and I don't appreciate your implications. My research will stand on its own merits."

"I hope so. For your sake. These waters can be dangerous, Dr. Merritt. Your father learned that the hard way."

She walked away, hands clenched to stop them from shaking. Jake intercepted her before she reached the others.

"What did he say?"

"He tried to bribe me. Then threatened me when I refused. Very professional." She looked back at Caldwell, who was watching them. "Jake, did my father ever mention being threatened by the developers?"

"Regularly. He reported it to the police twice. Nothing ever came of it."

"Of course not." Claire felt rage cutting through fear. "They're going to try to discredit anything we find. Paint me as an amateur, my father as an obsessed conspiracy theorist, this whole investigation as emotional rather than scientific."

"So we don't give them ammunition. We do everything by the book, document every step, publish every finding. Make it unimpeachable."

They gathered the team at Shaw's Marine Supply, setting up laptops in the back office. Claire transferred the footage to multiple drives, uploaded copies to secure cloud storage, created a detailed log of when and how it was captured.

"Now we analyze," she said, opening video editing software. "Frame by frame. Measurements, enhancements, comparisons to known phenomena."

They worked through the afternoon, isolating individual frames, adjusting contrast and brightness, trying to extract maximum information from the brief footage. Maya researched similar phenomena, finding scattered reports of unusual sonar readings and unexplained lights, but nothing quite like what they had captured.

"There's a marine biologist at Woods Hole who specializes in deep-sea life," Claire said, reading through academic profiles. "Dr. Sarah Chen. She's published on bioluminescence and undiscovered species. I'm going to email her, see if she can look at this footage."

"Will she take it seriously?"

"I don't know. But she knew Dad. They corresponded about his observations. She was one of the few academics who didn't dismiss him out of hand."

Claire composed the email carefully, describing the footage as "unusual visual data from deep-water survey" without sensationalizing. She attached a few clear frames and requested expert analysis.

The reply came within an hour: "Claire—I've been expecting you to contact me. Your father and I were planning a joint research proposal before his death. I'd very much like to see your full footage and discuss collaboration. Can you call me this evening? —Sarah"

"She's interested," Claire said, reading the response aloud. "She and Dad were planning a joint proposal."

"Tom never mentioned that," Vernon said.

"He was waiting to see if it would pan out. Probably didn't want to jinx it." Claire felt a pang of loss. Her father had been on the verge of legitimate scientific validation, and he'd died before achieving it. Now it fell to her to complete what he'd started.

She called Dr. Chen that evening from her father's study, Maya listening on speaker phone.

"Claire, I'm so sorry about Tom," Sarah began. "He was a remarkable man. Self-taught but more rigorous than many professional researchers."

"Thank you. He left me all his data. Forty years of observations."

"I know. He told me about them. We were going to apply for a NOAA grant to do comprehensive analysis, proper study design, the full academic treatment. Then he died, and I assumed the project died with him."

"It doesn't have to. I'm conducting an environmental assessment for the town, and I have access to all his records. I'd like to continue what you started together."

"I'd like that too. Send me what you have. I'll begin preliminary analysis."

Claire sent the footage, the sonar data, samples of her father's temperature logs. Sarah was silent for a long moment after viewing the video.

"That's extraordinary," she said finally. "I need to consult with some colleagues, show this to people who specialize in underwater imaging and marine engineering. But Claire, if this is genuine—"

"It is. I filmed it myself this morning."

"Then you've documented something unprecedented. Something that could reshape our understanding of deep-ocean ecosystems. Or confirm the existence of something entirely non-natural in these waters."

"Non-natural meaning...?"

"I don't know. But those geometric patterns, the regular lighting arrays, the size and movement characteristics. That's not any known marine life. It's either human-engineered, which would mean classified military tech or illegal research station, or it's something else entirely."

"What should I do?"

"Keep documenting. Build an unassailable dataset. And be very, very careful. If this is classified military tech, powerful people will want it kept secret. If it's something else, there are still powerful people who'll want to control the discovery. Either way, you're potentially threatening significant interests."

After the call, Claire sat alone in her father's study, looking at the walls covered in charts and photos and forty years of careful observation. He had known. Had documented and preserved and waited for someone to take him seriously. Now she had the proof he'd never quite captured, and the responsibility of what to do with it.

Her phone buzzed. A text from an unknown number: "Stop investigating. This is your last warning."

Claire screenshot the message, added it to her documentation folder, and kept working. She had been threatened, bribed, and warned. It only confirmed she was onto something real and important. Something worth the risk.

Outside, night settled over Willowport. Boats rocked in their slips, the harbor gradually quieting as fishermen called it a day. Claire worked through darkness, analyzing data, cross-referencing observations, building the case that would validate her father's life work.

Maya brought her soup at midnight. "You need to eat."

"I will. After I finish this analysis."

"Mom. You're obsessing like Grandpa did."

Claire looked at her daughter, seeing worry in her eyes. "Is that such a bad thing? He was right, Maya. About everything. And I'm going to prove it."

"I know. But he died doing this. Stress and obsession and fighting people who didn't want to hear the truth. I don't want to lose you too."

Claire saved her work and closed the laptop. "You're right. I'm sorry. This is important, but you're more important."

They ate soup together in the kitchen, talking about ordinary things: Maya's college applications, her friends back in Boston, the bonfire she'd attended where local kids had been surprisingly welcoming. Normal life continuing despite the extraordinary circumstances.

"Do you regret coming here?" Claire asked.

"No. This is the most real thing I've ever been part of. In Boston, everything felt fake somehow. Performance and credentials and positioning for the future. But here, this matters. What we're doing could actually change things."

"Or get us in trouble."

"Maybe both." Maya smiled. "But that's better than safe and meaningless."

Claire hugged her daughter, feeling grateful for this fierce young woman who had inherited the Merritt stubbornness. They were in this together now, committed to uncovering truth regardless of consequences.

Later, lying in bed, Claire thought about the thing in the water. What it was, why it was here, what it meant for Willowport and the wider world. Tomorrow they would dive again, collect more data, build the case. Sixty days was simultaneously too much time and nowhere near enough.

She dreamed of her father, younger and vital, standing at the helm of the Anna Marie. He turned to her and smiled, gesturing toward the deep water ahead. "Keep going," he said, though his lips didn't move. "You're close. Don't stop now."

Claire woke determined. The weight of tides had pulled her home. Now she would follow those tides into the depths, discover what mysteries they concealed, and bring the truth into light no matter who wanted it to remain hidden.
