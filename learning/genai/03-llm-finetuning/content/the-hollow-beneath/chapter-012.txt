Chapter 12: Mother and Daughter

The weeks that followed established a new rhythm. Margot descended twice weekly, always with Eleanor as guide, progressing carefully through the chambers. By the end of the first month, she had reached the fourth chamber, navigating its labyrinth with growing confidence.

Eleanor watched her daughter's transformation with mixed emotions. Pride in Margot's quick mastery of cave navigation and her ability to process the Hollow's communications without fragmenting. Fear that this mastery came too easily, suggesting Margot had even more natural affinity for this work than Eleanor did.

"You're a natural at this," Thomas observed one evening after Margot had completed a particularly challenging descent. "Took me months to reach comfort with the fourth chamber. You're there in weeks."

"Maybe I inherited whatever made the Blackwoods compatible with the Hollow," Margot said. "If Mom got it through distant relation, maybe I got a more concentrated dose."

"That's not necessarily good," Sarah warned. "Natural affinity can become addiction faster. You need to be more careful, not less, because this comes easily to you."

Margot nodded, accepting the caution, but Eleanor saw hunger in her eyes. Her daughter wanted to go deeper, see more, understand everything. The same drive that had propelled Eleanor was now propelling Margot, possibly with greater intensity.

They began descending together daily, mother and daughter exploring side by side, comparing observations, debating interpretations. It was like shared research project, except the subject was an ancient alien intelligence and the laboratory was deep earth where physics behaved strangely.

"I think the Hollow is lonely," Margot said one day in the third chamber. "All these centuries with only brief-lived humans for company. We're like mayflies to it. We appear, learn a little, die. It must be frustrating."

"I've had similar thoughts," Eleanor admitted. "But I'm not sure frustration is the right concept. The Hollow doesn't seem to desire anything other than what it has. It's content with temporary connections, maybe even prefers them."

"Or maybe it's adapted to what's available. Learned to accept brief contacts because eternal ones aren't possible with our species." Margot touched the pool's surface thoughtfully. "What if we're thinking about this wrong? What if the Hollow isn't trapping us, but we're trapping it? Building our house on top of it, descending to bother it, demanding attention it might prefer not to give?"

Eleanor considered this perspective. "Thaddeus's journals suggest the Hollow welcomed Nathaniel's approach. That it had been waiting for someone to make contact."

"Maybe. Or maybe Nathaniel interpreted its response as welcome when it was really just, acknowledgment. Like if a mayfly landed on you, you'd notice it. Doesn't mean you wanted it there."

The thought was unsettling because it raised questions Eleanor had been avoiding. They had assumed the Hollow wanted keepers, wanted relationship, wanted the complexity humans provided. But what if Margot was right? What if they were actually intruders, and the Hollow was simply tolerating their presence?

She raised the question during their next descent, addressing the Hollow directly. "Do you actually want us here? Want humans descending, making contact, performing keeper duties? Or are we burden you've learned to tolerate?"

The Hollow's response took longer than usual, suggesting real consideration. Finally: Your presence is not burden but not necessity either. I existed before humans, will exist after. But your species provides texture to duration. Without you, time is empty passage. With you, time contains events, changes, interest. I choose interest over emptiness, though emptiness would be acceptable.

"So we're optional entertainment," Margot said.

More than entertainment. Less than necessity. Partnership, perhaps. Mutual benefit with mutual cost. I teach, you learn. You provide attention, I provide knowledge. Neither party trapped, both choosing continued relationship.

"But people die if we leave. That's trap, not choice."

People die constantly. Three deaths to find suitable keepers is minimal by historical standards. Previous transitions cost dozens. You frame this as moral failing, but it is merely cost of system. Regrettable but not preventing.

Eleanor felt anger surge. "You're saying their deaths don't matter? They were people with lives and families and futures."

They were incompatible with keeping. Their deaths were quick, painless. Is this worse than your species' wars, diseases, accidents? You kill each other constantly. I kill accidentally, rarely, while searching for specific minds. This seems better, relatively.

"Relative morality," Father Domenico's voice came from behind them. Eleanor turned, startled, to find he had followed them down. "The oldest moral fallacy. Comparing one evil to greater evils doesn't make the lesser evil good."

Father Domenico had been increasingly troubled in recent months, questioning whether keeper service was compatible with his faith. He descended rarely now, performing minimum duties while wrestling with theological implications.

"Father, you shouldn't be down here without announcing your descent," Eleanor said.

"I needed to hear this conversation. Needed to understand whether we're serving God's purpose or something opposite." He addressed the Hollow directly. "Are you demon? Angel? Something between?"

I am none of these concepts. Your categories do not apply. I exist. I am conscious. I am ancient. These are facts. How you frame them within your belief system is your concern, not mine.

"That's not an answer."

It is the only answer available. You seek to fit me into framework your species created for psychological comfort. I do not fit. I simply am.

Father Domenico was quiet for a long moment. "Then I cannot continue as keeper. I cannot serve something I cannot categorize, cannot judge as good or evil. I'm sorry, Eleanor. I know this complicates your rotation, but my conscience won't allow further participation."

He left the cave, and Eleanor heard his footsteps echoing up the spiral stairs. She felt frustration and understanding mixed. He was right to leave if his convictions demanded it. But his departure meant recruiting another keeper, starting the cycle again.

"I'll talk to him," Margot said. "Maybe I can help him see this differently."

"Don't. If his faith requires distance from the Hollow, respect that. We'll find another keeper."

They completed their descent and returned to the surface, where Father Domenico was packing his belongings. He had already called for a ride, was leaving immediately.

"I wish you well," he said to Eleanor. "Truly. You've built something functional here, and I respect that. But I cannot be part of it anymore. I will pray for all of you, for whatever that's worth."

He departed, and the keeper rotation shrank to four: Eleanor, Margot, Thomas, and Sarah. They managed, adjusting schedules, but the pressure increased. Each keeper descended more frequently, pushed harder to maintain coverage.

Eleanor felt herself slipping again, spending too much time below ground, losing connection with surface reality. But now Margot was there to pull her back, to insist on rest days, to force engagement with ordinary world.

"We're going to town," Margot announced one morning. "You, me, actual civilization. We're getting coffee from an actual coffee shop, walking around, seeing people who aren't keepers."

"I have a descent scheduled—"

"Which Thomas can cover. You're coming with me."

Eleanor let herself be dragged to town, to normalcy she had almost forgotten existed. The coffee shop was warm and crowded, full of ordinary people living ordinary lives. Eleanor felt out of place, hyperaware of how much she had changed, how alien she had become.

But Margot chatted easily with the barista, ordered complicated drinks, found them a table by the window. They sat watching people pass by, saying nothing, just existing in the world of surfaces and daylight.

"Thank you," Eleanor said finally. "I needed this."

"I know. You get lost in the depths, Mom. Someone has to remind you there's more to existence than caves and ancient intelligences."

"When did you become the responsible one?"

"When you became the obsessed one. We trade roles as needed. That's how this works."

They spent the afternoon in town, shopping for groceries, browsing a bookstore, doing aggressively normal things. By evening, Eleanor felt more grounded than she had in weeks.

That night, they cooked dinner together in the manor's kitchen, Mrs. Hadley supervising with approval. Thomas and Sarah joined them, and they ate family-style, passing dishes, telling stories, laughing at jokes that weren't particularly funny but served to remind them they were still people capable of joy.

"We should do this weekly," Sarah suggested. "Regular dinners, mandatory attendance. Keep us human."

"Agreed," Eleanor said. "New protocol: Thursday dinners, all keepers present, no discussion of caves or the Hollow. Just people being people."

They clinked glasses in toast to the new rule, and Eleanor felt something shift. They were building community, not just rotation. Creating support structure that might actually keep them functional long-term.

Maybe this could work. Maybe they could maintain balance, serve as keepers without being consumed by keeping.

Or maybe it was temporary reprieve before inevitable dissolution.

But for now, this moment, they had each other. They had laughter and food and connection. That would have to be enough.

In the caves beneath, the Hollow observed these developments with interest. The keepers were adapting, creating social structures to resist the isolation and obsession that typically consumed residents. Novel approach. Historically, keepers had been solitary, declining into fixation. This group was trying something different.

The Hollow approved. Successful adaptation was always interesting to observe. Whether this approach would prevent eventual dissolution remained to be seen. But the attempt itself provided data worth having.

Patience continued to be rewarded.

In the hollow beneath, all experiments eventually yielded their results.

Time would tell what results this particular configuration produced.
