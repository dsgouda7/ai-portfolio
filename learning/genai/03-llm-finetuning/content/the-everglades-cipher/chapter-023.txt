Chapter 23: The Keys

Nacho Reyes took a group of recreational divers out to Looe Key reef on the first Monday in March, because Monday was when the serious divers came — the ones who had done their reading, who had their certification current, who wanted the reef and not the glamour of it.

He had seven divers. He took them out on La Determinada because the boat that had been to the Windward Passage and back needed to be doing this work, the ordinary work, the regular client work that was what most of life consisted of, and a boat that sat at the dock too long got ideas about being significant that it needed regular correction on.

He briefed them at the anchor point above the reef. He told them what they would see: the elkhorn and brain coral that had been recovering since the 2005 bleaching events, the parrotfish and the surgeonfish and the occasional nurse shark resting under the coral heads, the lobster in the crevices if they were patient. He told them about depth limits and timing and the current, which was running east today, which meant the return leg of the drift would require some effort.

He dropped in last and floated at the surface for a moment, looking down through twenty feet of clear water at the reef below.

He thought: this has been here longer than anything I know about.

He thought: and it will be here after everything I know about is gone.

He descended.

The reef was doing what it always did: being enormously itself. The coral architecture that had taken centuries to build, the fish populations that had survived everything the twentieth century had thrown at them, the sponges and the sea fans and the cleaning stations where fish lined up with the specific trust of creatures that have an arrangement with something and know it will hold.

He drifted.

He had spent thirty years on this reef. He knew individual coral heads the way you know the rooms of your house — by their specific texture, their orientation, the way the light fell on them at different times of day. He knew this reef the way his grandfather had known the Windward Passage: as a place that existed independently of him but which he had learned carefully enough to feel continuous with.

His grandfather.

He thought about his grandfather on most dives, but he thought about him differently now. The specific texture of the thinking had changed, because the thing his grandfather had been trying to tell him — the thing encoded in the story of the island — had been found and verified and placed in the permanent historical record. It was no longer a family story. It was a public fact.

He thought: Ramon Francisco Santiesteban y Vidal did his job.

He thought: his grandfather did his job too. Kept the knowledge. Kept the location. Kept faith with Ramon's account when there was no evidence and no deadline and every sensible practical reason to let it go.

He thought: I kept it too. Found the bookseller. Made the connection. Did the thing.

He thought: the three of us, across three generations, we got this to the right place.

He looked at the reef.

A green sea turtle moved through his field of vision, heading northeast at the unhurried pace of creatures that are simply moving through their world. He watched it until it was out of sight.

He thought about Sofia.

She was in Miami at the university, doing the marine archaeology master's. She had called him on Sunday. She had told him about her conference presentation — The Acoustic Architecture of the Cayo Espiritu Cave System — and how it had gone, which was: well. She had gotten two follow-up requests for collaboration. She had handled the question period with the directness that was her characteristic and which had always made him proud.

He thought about Sofia diving ahead of the boat in the Caribbean dark, because she had understood before anyone else that timing mattered and had acted on it.

He thought: she got that from me.

He thought: I got it from my grandfather.

He thought: and my grandfather got it from watching the water until he understood what it was trying to say.

That was the lineage, really. Not the property or the money or the status or any of the things that families usually meant when they talked about lineage. The practice of paying attention. The practice of acting when action was what the moment required. The practice of waiting, when waiting was what the moment required, and being patient without being passive.

He reached the deepest part of his drift, twenty-five feet above the base of the reef, and turned to look east where the ocean floor dropped away into the blue void of the Atlantic. He looked at that void for a moment — the specific dark blue of deep water, which was not frightening to him but which was what it was: the thing that was beyond knowing, beyond the reach of the light, beyond the reef's careful architecture.

He thought: beyond is always there.

He thought: you can't go past the edge of what you know, but you can know it's there.

He turned back toward the reef and the divers and the anchor and the boat.

He came up at the end of the drift with the current, which had cooperated, and climbed aboard, and looked at his divers' faces, which had the specific expression of people who have been given something by the sea and are still feeling what it was.

He thought: I know that feeling.

He thought: good.

He pulled the anchor and turned north toward Big Pine Key.

The water was blue and the sky was clear and the Keys were doing what the Keys do: existing in their specific way, the thin strip of land between the Gulf and the Atlantic, the place where Florida runs out and the ocean begins, the place where, if you know what you're looking at, you can see what the continent will eventually become.

He drove north.

He thought about the letter he had received from the University of Miami the previous week, from a researcher named Dr. Patricia Chen who was studying Cuban-American maritime culture and wanted to interview him about his grandfather's 1941 expedition. He had been reluctant at first — reluctant in the way of private people who are not accustomed to being studied. But then he had thought: this is how the record is made.

He had written back and said yes.

He drove north.

The water was clear. The bridges were exactly what they were. The herons were in the mangroves.

He drove home.

Nacho kept the logbook.

He had always kept it — the running log of every trip, every dive, every observation. Weather, current, visibility, what the reef was doing, what the fish were doing, what the clients asked about and what they saw. He had been keeping it since he took over the charter business from his uncle in 1994, and he had three shelves of logbooks in the back office of the dive shop, dated and indexed, the accumulated record of thirty years of paying attention.

He added an entry for the March Monday dive.

He wrote the coordinates, the depth profile, the current direction, the visibility (75 feet, excellent), the species list from the dive. He noted the turtle heading northeast. He noted that the elkhorn coral at the 8-meter station had two new colonies extending from the base that he hadn't seen on the February survey, which meant recovery was continuing.

He wrote: Sofia called Sunday. Presentation went well. Two collaboration requests.

He wrote: Thought about grandfather. The job is done and the record is made.

He closed the logbook.

He looked at the shelf of logbooks. Thirty years of entries.

He thought about what that was: a record. Not of anything famous. Not of anything historical, mostly. Just the record of what the reef was doing, year by year, dive by dive, in the care of a man who had paid attention.

He thought: this is also an archive.

He thought: this also matters.

He turned off the shop light.

He walked down to the dock and stood on it in the evening. The Gulf was moving in the specific way it moved in March: the calm that came before the spring wind season, the light long and low and doing extraordinary things with the water.

He looked west.

The sun was going down behind the mangroves. The sky was doing what Florida skies do at sunset: turning itself into something that nobody in their right mind would believe unless they were looking at it.

He looked at this.

He thought: this is also real.

He thought: the archive and the reef and the family story and the turtle and the logbook and the sunset.

He thought: all of it is real.

He stood and watched the sun go down.

Then he went inside and had dinner and went to bed at nine, because the next morning there was a six a.m. departure with a four-person dive group that wanted to see the juvenile nurse sharks at the Sand Island shoal, and you didn't want to be tired for that.

The work was always there.

That was the thing about the work: it was always there.

He slept well.
