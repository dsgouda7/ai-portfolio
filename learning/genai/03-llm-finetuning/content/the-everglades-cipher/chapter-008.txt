Chapter 8: Magdalena

The compound in Coral Gables occupied a corner lot of half an acre, screened from the street by a wall of twelve-foot ficus that had been allowed to grow to opacity over twenty years of deliberate neglect-by-design. From the street, you could see the upper floor of the house and the red tiles of the roof, and nothing else. The house had been built in the twenties in the Mediterranean revival style that colonized South Florida in that era — arched windows, barrel-tile roof, loggia on the second floor with cast-iron railings — and had been maintained with the investment of serious money: not the money of someone trying to impress but the money of someone trying to last.

Magdalena Santiesteban did not entertain in the Coral Gables house. She had a smaller property in Coconut Grove that she used for social occasions, and an apartment in Brickell that served as a working office. The Coral Gables house was family — the house her grandfather had built and her father had grown up in and where she had been raised and where she lived now in the way that people live in the houses of their lineage: with a proprietary comfort that could never be fully replicated in any property acquired later.

She was forty-five years old. Jake had found photographs in newspaper archives — business section, several charity events. In the photographs she was always positioned slightly off-center, which he thought was intentional, the deliberate spatial choice of someone who understood that power is more convincing when it doesn't perform itself. She was dark-haired in the older photographs, now showing early silver at the temples that she made no effort to conceal. Her face was the kind of face that photographs don't quite capture because its power is in movement and attention — in person, Jake suspected, she would have the specific presence of someone who makes the room organize itself around her without appearing to have tried.

He had not met her. He did not intend to meet her. Not yet.

He had been watching the compound from a parked position on the cross-street for forty-five minutes on Thursday morning when a car left the side entrance — a black Tesla, not a Suburban, more discreet — with a single occupant that his long lens confirmed was a woman. Silver at the temples, dark hair, dressed for a working day.

He followed at three cars' distance.

She went to the Brickell apartment building, parked in the underground garage, was in the building for three hours. He couldn't follow without being clocked — the security at the building's entrance was professional, not decorative — so he sat in a coffee shop across the street and worked from his phone.

He had spent two days building a picture of Magdalena Santiesteban from public records, business filings, news archives, and two conversations he'd had with people who knew the Miami business world well enough to speak with useful vagueness.

Magdalena had taken over the family's operations when her father Ramon Jr. had a stroke in 2011. She had a degree in international business from the University of Miami, 1998, which Jake found interesting — she had grown up in the shadow of these operations and had chosen to stay rather than escape, which suggested either a specific kind of family loyalty or a specific kind of ambition, and probably both. She had been married once, briefly, to a Venezuelan architect named Guillen who had returned to Caracas in 2005 and was no longer part of the picture. She had one child, a daughter named Sofia, who was nineteen and at Georgetown studying international relations.

The legitimate businesses she ran were genuinely substantial: Caribe Logistics was not a fiction. It moved real goods — agricultural products, consumer electronics, pharmaceuticals licensed for distribution — through real supply chains. The company had two hundred employees and had made a profit every year since 2008. The non-legitimate interests — the facilitation fees, the movement of certain goods that required no documentation, the financial services rendered to parties who preferred not to use banks — were, as far as Jake could determine from the outside, conducted with the same professionalism.

She was the third generation of a family enterprise. She had inherited not just the business but the patience — the sixty-year patience of people who had known about Cayo Espiritu and had waited for the right moment.

The right moment had apparently arrived.

Jake thought about Arturo Vasquez-Cortez, somewhere in this city or beyond it, and about the specific pressure that would be applied to a man who had spent thirty years in love with a piece of history. Magdalena Santiesteban would not be crude about it. She would be as precise as the family enterprise had always been. She would apply exactly enough pressure to extract what she needed and no more than that, because unnecessary damage was inefficient.

The question was: what was she willing to pay for the island's exact location?

And what was she willing to pay for Arturo's silence afterward?

These were not the same sum. And in Jake's experience, the delta between them was where the real danger lived.

He was still sitting in the coffee shop with these thoughts when his phone rang. A number he didn't recognize — Miami area code.

"Malone," he said.

A pause. A woman's voice, precise and controlled. "This is Magdalena Santiesteban."

Jake said nothing.

"I was told you were parked across from my Brickell office this morning," she said. "I'm not calling to threaten you. I'm calling because I think we may want the same thing."

He said: "What's that?"

"Arturo Vasquez-Cortez safe and found." A pause. "And the correct disposition of what he discovered."

Jake kept his voice neutral. "You have him."

"I do not have him." The way she said it was flat and direct, the way a person says something that is factually true and is slightly irritated by the implication that it might not be. "Alejandro Reyes-Castillo had him. Past tense. He no longer does."

Jake sat forward. "Explain."

A brief pause, as if she were deciding how much to explain. "Four nights ago, the location where Arturo was being held was accessed by a third party. Arturo was taken. Alejandro's operation was — disrupted. I currently do not know where Arturo is, and I would very much like to know."

Jake processed this. "A third party took him from you."

"Alejandro — my representative — made a decision without my full authorization to take proactive steps to secure Arturo's cooperation. He believes he was acting in the family's interests. He was wrong about the method and wrong about the timing and, as it turns out, wrong about the security arrangements."

Jake thought about this. "Who took him from you?"

"That is what I'm trying to determine." Her voice was precise without being tight. "The people who took him are not from this city. They moved professionally and they left nothing. They also left Alejandro with three fractured ribs and a concussion, which suggests they were not interested in conversation."

Jake thought about Father Giorgio Bellini. The Custodes Veritatis. The Vatican connection that Fairweather had mentioned.

"How did you get my number?" he asked.

"Roberto Sanchez. He told me you were working for Elena. He said you were thorough." A pause that might have contained something like respect, or its more businesslike cousin. "I know who you were in New York. I know why you came to Miami. I'm not interested in your history. I'm interested in finding Arturo before the people who have him extract what they want from him and then make a decision about his continued existence."

Jake looked at the street. A parking enforcement officer was writing a ticket for a car two spaces up. The ordinary frictions of the city.

"If you're in contact with Roberto Sanchez," he said carefully, "then you know about the university library."

"The Codex Almeida. Yes. I know it's there. I don't want it." She paused. "I want the island. The location. With the full translation, the island becomes accessible to a properly resourced expedition. The documents in the cavern — what they are, their historical value, the implications — these are matters for scholars. I have no use for fourteenth-century politics. I have use for a Caribbean island with existing infrastructure possibilities." A pause. "And I have use for being the party that resolves this situation cleanly, before it creates complications I can't manage."

Jake thought about the implications of this conversation. She was telling him several things: she had lost Arturo to another party. She was capable of working with him on finding him. She was willing to separate the island's commercial possibilities from the archive's scholarly value. And she was trying to close a situation before it escalated beyond her management.

"You know that Arturo won't tell you the island's name," Jake said.

"I assumed as much. Which is why I didn't give Alejandro authorization to take him. Alejandro was impatient." A slight edge came into her voice on that last sentence. "My family has been patient for sixty years. I was prepared to be patient a little longer."

"What does Alejandro know? About the island's location."

"He knows the region — Windward Passage, south of Cuba. He knows it's a small uninhabited island. He does not know the name. Only Arturo and apparently Dr. Fairweather know the specific island." She paused. "I have no interest in harming Dr. Fairweather. I'm aware of where she is. I'm aware that she has the information. I'm also aware that she won't share it with anyone she doesn't trust, and that getting it from her by force would create — international complications. British academics don't disappear quietly."

Jake said: "What do you need from me?"

"Find Arturo. Return him safely. In exchange I'll provide you with everything Alejandro's operation had gathered about the people who took him. Which is more than you currently have." A pause. "And when the island question is eventually resolved by scholars and appropriate parties, I would appreciate a conversation about its practical possibilities. A conversation in which I am at the table."

Jake thought for a long time.

"I'm not your agent," he said.

"I'm not asking you to be. I'm asking you to find a man who is in danger and to operate without constraints I might otherwise create." Another pause. "I can make your work easier or harder. I'm offering to make it easier."

Jake said: "Send me what you have about the people who took him."

"My lawyer's office will send it this afternoon. Direct to your email." She had his email. Of course she did. "Mr. Malone. Find him quickly. The people who have him are not patient the way my family is patient."

She ended the call.

Jake sat for a long time with his phone in his hand and the ordinary morning traffic of Brickell moving past the coffee shop window.

He had just been recruited by the woman whose organization had kidnapped the man he was looking for, to find the man from the people who had counter-kidnapped him.

Only in this city, he thought.

Only in this city.

---

He called Carla Hidalgo.

"Arturo isn't with the Santiesteban operation anymore," he said, when she picked up.

A long pause. "Elaborate."

He elaborated. When he finished, she was quiet for several seconds.

"She called you directly," Carla said.

"She did."

"That's a signal. She's trying to get ahead of something. If she's worried enough to contact you directly, the third party she's describing is something she takes seriously." A pause. "The Vatican connection."

"Fairweather mentioned an organization called the Custodes Veritatis. A Jesuit named Father Giorgio Bellini."

"I know that name," Carla said immediately. "Or I know the category. The FBI Miami field office has a file on individuals who represent themselves as agents for religious organizations but operate with resources inconsistent with their stated roles. Bellini was on a list I saw in the context of an art theft investigation three years ago. He's been connected to the recovery of three separate historical documents that were returned to Vatican custody." A pause. "Recovered, not stolen. The distinction matters because in two of the three cases the previous holders of the documents were—" She paused. "—persuaded to relinquish them."

"Persuaded how?"

"The art theft file doesn't go into operational details. But in one case the previous holder ended up in a hospital in Barcelona with injuries consistent with a fall that the Spanish police did not fully investigate." She was quiet. "These are not Vatican librarians. These are people who operate in service of institutional interests with institutional resources."

"They've taken Arturo," Jake said.

"If it's them. We're not certain."

"Magdalena Santiesteban is certain enough to call me about it."

"She could be misdirecting you."

"She could be," Jake agreed. "But what she told me aligns with what Fairweather told me about Bellini's interest in the research. And the operational description — professional, moved quickly, left nothing — matches what you just described."

A pause. "I need to make some calls. Federal level. This is officially beyond my authority." She sounded frustrated by this, which was the sound of a good cop hitting jurisdictional limits. "Stay reachable. Don't do anything until I've made the calls."

"Carla," Jake said. "How long?"

She was quiet. "Tonight. I'll have something tonight."

He sat in the coffee shop for another ten minutes after ending the call, watching the street. The Vatican, or something operating in its name, had taken a retired Cuban bookseller because he held the key to an archive that might contain documents inconvenient to the institutional history of the Roman Catholic Church.

This was, Jake thought, going to require a very different kind of conversation than the ones he'd been having.

He finished his coffee and drove to Coconut Grove.

---

On the houseboat, he read the materials Magdalena Santiesteban's lawyer had emailed while he was driving. The lawyer was named Marcus Webb, the firm was Webb & Associates on Brickell, and the email contained three attachments.

The first was a brief summary — four paragraphs — of what Alejandro Reyes-Castillo's operation had gathered prior to the incident. Jake read this twice. Alejandro had held Arturo at a property in Hialeah — a commercial property, light industrial, the kind of space that is simultaneously private and ordinary. Arturo had been — the summary was clinical but Jake parsed it — under house arrest conditions rather than physical restraint. Fed, comfortable, not harmed. Being negotiated with, though the negotiation had apparently been one-sided and increasingly frustrated.

Arturo had not told Alejandro the island's name. In four days of conversation. Which told Jake things about Arturo that he already suspected: the man had the courage of his convictions.

The second attachment was a series of photographs. The Hialeah property, photographed from outside. The approach routes. And then — photographs taken by Alejandro's team of the people who had arrived and taken Arturo. They were good photographs, clear enough.

Four individuals. Two men and a woman, plus a driver. The men were both large, Mediterranean-looking, dressed functionally — dark clothing, professional operational style. The woman was in her forties, wearing a dark jacket, short hair, standing at what appeared to be a slight distance from the others as if she was directing rather than participating.

In the last photograph, as she was getting into a vehicle, she turned slightly toward the camera. Jake looked at this photograph carefully.

He sent it to Carla Hidalgo by text, no message.

Her response came back in four minutes: a single name.

Andrea Montaldo. Previously active NOC for an Italian intelligence service, currently independent. Three known contracts in the past seven years, all involving retrieval of cultural or historical property. One contract in service of a buyer identified in a Portuguese police file as a representative of Vatican interests.

The third attachment was the vehicle registration for the van the four individuals had arrived in. It was registered to a shell company in Delaware, but the insurance on it — and this was the kind of detail that a Santiesteban-level intelligence operation could actually run down — traced to a policy brokered through a Miami-area insurance agent who had two other clients that were companies with no discoverable principal officers and addresses at the same registered agent in Delaware.

Jake ran the addresses through his own databases.

One of the companies was a Florida LLC that owned a single property — a forty-two-foot boat with a Florida marine registration, currently documented as docked at a marina in Coconut Grove.

Not his marina. Another marina. Less than two miles from where he was sitting.

Jake looked at this for a long time.

Then he put on his jacket and his sneakers, the ones with the thick soles that didn't make noise on dock boards, and went out into the evening.


---

The Dinner Key Marina in Coconut Grove was larger and busier than Jake's home marina, the kind of place with two hundred slips and a fuel dock and a restaurant and enough boat traffic that one additional person walking the dock boards attracted no attention.

He found the boat at slip 184. A Hylas 46, built in the early nineties, maintained well but not ostentatiously. Named — and this Jake found interesting — Carità. The Italian word for charity.

He stood on the dock across from it for ten minutes. The boat was dark. No lights aboard, no movement.

He walked to the marina office and had a conversation with the dock master about availability for long-term berthing. The dock master, who was bored and willing to talk, told him the Carità had been in the marina for eleven days, crewed by two people — a man and a woman, both Italian he thought, both friendly enough but private. They'd been out twice, day trips, came back by evening both times.

Eleven days was the day after Dr. Fairweather had arrived in Miami.

Jake thanked the dock master and walked back to his car.

He called Carla.

"The boat," he said. "The Carità. Dinner Key Marina, slip one-eighty-four. It's connected to the same registered agent as the van."

A pause. "You went there."

"I went there."

"Malone—"

"I know. I know. But Arturo is on that boat or on a property connected to it." He paused. "Probably not on the boat now — too visible. But they're using the boat for logistics. If they move Arturo — possibly to a ship, possibly to a plane — they'll use the marina as a staging point."

A long silence.

"I can request a harbor patrol presence," Carla said slowly. "Discreet. If the boat moves—"

"They'll know if there's surveillance."

"I know." She was quiet. "Let me think. Give me until morning." A pause. "Malone. Don't go near the boat again tonight. I mean this."

"I heard you."

"Hearing and obeying are different things with you."

"Tomorrow morning," he said.

"Tomorrow morning," she agreed.

He sat in the parking lot for a while after the call, looking at the direction of the marina in the dark, thinking about Arturo Vasquez-Cortez on a boat called Charity.

Then he drove home and wrote everything down in the yellow legal pad and sat on the deck of the Reluctant Witness in the dark, and thought about the shape of the thing, which was getting larger and more complicated and more dangerous with each conversation.

Medieval archives. Intelligence contractors. Vatican money. The ghost of a 1307 Templar flight from Paris.

And somewhere in the middle of all of this: a man in his late sixties who had opened a bookshop on Calle Ocho and spent thirty years of his life working quietly toward a moment that someone else had been waiting sixty years to exploit.

Jake looked at the water.

He thought: the sound the water makes in the cavern at high tide. The voice of the saint. What does it sound like? A low, rhythmic resonance, musical, almost breathing.

Diego de Almeida heard it in 1307-1308 and found it worth encoding. Nacho's grandfather heard it in 1941 and called it beautiful and disturbing. Three expeditions and thirty years later, Nacho had heard it too, and had described it as the most specific thing he had ever heard — particular and irreplaceable, the way certain pieces of music are particular and irreplaceable.

Jake thought: that's worth finding.

Then he thought: Arturo needs to stay alive to help find it.

He put down the legal pad and went below to sleep.

The city moved around him in the dark, indifferent and enormous, and somewhere in it, a boat called Charity rode at its mooring and waited for whatever came next.
