Chapter 12: The Straits of Florida

They left at six AM on a Friday morning, which was not ideal for leaving a marina dock in Big Pine Key, because Friday morning is the beginning of the weekend convoy south toward Key West, and La Determinada had to navigate the channel traffic of sport fishers and dive boats and the occasional early-sailing cruiser with the patience and attention that Nacho applied to all channel navigation: methodically, without cursing, and with the specific equanimity of a man who has been doing this longer than most of the other boats in the water have been built.

Once they cleared the channel and turned southeast with open water ahead, the morning opened up in the way that blue-water Florida mornings open up: the light became horizontal and gold, the chop developed its proper rhythm, and the boat settled into its stride.

Jake stood at the stern rail for an hour with coffee and watched South Florida recede. He had a specific relationship with departures. He had left New York in a way that had felt necessary and possibly permanent, and he had come to Miami and made it something like home without ever quite completing the act of arriving. He was still, in some sense of the word, in transit. The houseboat was appropriate: a dwelling that floated, that was moored but not fixed, that contained all his necessaries but could, in principle, be moved.

He thought about this because movement always made him think about it, and the open water between Key West and Cuba was as specific a kind of movement as he knew — the committed movement of a vessel underway, with the continental shelf dropping away beneath the keel and the weather coming from all directions and no reversing without a full course change.

Nacho ran the boat from the flying bridge. He had offered to put Jake at the helm for sections of the run, and Jake had declined, not from lack of confidence — he had handled boats on occasion — but from the understanding that La Determinada was Nacho's instrument and the passage was Nacho's expertise, and there was a principle, learned over decades of detective work, about not taking over other people's competences.

He watched the Keys sink behind them and then disappear, and then there was nothing in any direction except water and sky, and this — this was the specific thing about the open water that the city could not provide and that Jake had, over twelve years of houseboat living, come to depend on in a way he would not have expected.

Arturo was below, working. He had set up his materials on the salon table — the USB drive loaded into a borrowed laptop, the photographs of the Codex pages at maximum magnification, his translation notes — and he was working with the concentrated intensity that Jake had begun to recognize as his normal operating mode when he was close to something. He drank coffee at intervals and occasionally muttered in Spanish and occasionally wrote something in his notebook, and otherwise he was entirely absent from the social life of the boat.

Elena was on the foredeck with a book — something biological, technical, the kind of reading that most people would not choose for an open-water passage, which Jake thought was exactly right. She was on the foredeck because she wanted sky and she wanted air and she did not want to watch her father work, which would inevitably become watching over his shoulder, which she had identified as a behavior to avoid. The book was her way of being present but separate.

Sofia was in the water — briefly, while they were still in the protected lee of the lower keys — doing what turned out to be a free-dive check of the hull and running gear, which she did with the specific efficiency of someone who views equipment inspection as a professional obligation and not a chore. She came up with a brief report: port side zincs were sixty percent consumed and should be checked before the dive, nothing else concerning. She delivered this to her father with the tone of a colleague and went below to change.

Then there were hours of the Atlantic.

---

The Straits of Florida is the forty-mile-wide deepwater passage between the southern tip of the continental United States and the western coast of Cuba. It is where the Gulf of Mexico and the Atlantic Ocean meet, and this meeting is not a gentle one — it produces a current system, the Florida Current (the northern extension of the Gulf Stream), that runs north and east at a rate of three to four knots, and this current makes the passage interesting for any vessel going south, because three to four knots of current against you means you are making less speed than you think, and more fuel, and more time.

Nacho had planned for this. He ran south and slightly west, fighting the edge of the current rather than its center, and he tracked their speed over ground on the chartplotter with the attention of a man who knows the arithmetic of fuel and time.

They were three hours out when Jake's satellite phone rang.

It was Carla Hidalgo.

"The Carità is gone," she said.

Jake's hand tightened on the phone. "When?"

"Last night. Harbor patrol said she cleared Dinner Key at oh-three-hundred. Coast Guard Sector Key West picked her up on AIS at oh-four-thirty, bearing south-southeast on the Straits passage." A pause. "They lost the AIS signal at oh-seven-hundred. She turned off the transponder."

"They're following us."

"The boat's been tracking your passage time, not your specific route. But in the Straits of Florida there's only one passage south. They know where you're going." She paused. "The FBI Miami field office has officially taken jurisdiction on the Montaldo connection, which is good news in the long run and not immediately useful."

"How far behind are they?"

"If they left at oh-three-hundred and you left at oh-six-hundred, they have a three-hour head start. They're probably faster than La Determinada — the Hylas is an ocean passage boat, designed for speed over long distances."

Jake thought about this.

"They'll reach the island before us," he said.

"Possibly. Unless—" A pause. "Unless their course isn't direct. If they don't have the exact island location—"

"If Montaldo got anything from Arturo during the fish camp days, she knows to look in the Windward Passage region. If she's talked to anyone with Caribbean knowledge, she knows the island candidates." He paused. "Do you have any way to interdict?"

"Coast Guard jurisdiction extends through the Straits. I'm calling the Sector Key West commander now. But once she's in international waters south of Cuba—"

"Then it's just boats," Jake said.

"Then it's just boats." A pause. "Be careful."

"You keep saying that."

"Keep making it necessary." She paused. "One more thing. Buck Langford's office received a call yesterday from an attorney representing Calypso Bay Development. The attorney asked about the status of marine infrastructure permits for a listed but unnamed island property in the Windward Passage region. The permitting office flagged it because the property details matched a blank on a pending environmental impact waiver that had been submitted without specific location data six months ago."

Jake sat down on the stern bench. "They were pre-permitted."

"Calypso Bay has been building the regulatory framework for development on an island whose specific location they hadn't yet confirmed. Based on the family's family history — they knew it was in the Windward Passage, they knew it was accessible, they had the expedition records from 1941 — they built the permits around a general description, waiting for the specific location to be confirmed." A pause. "Which means the development plan for the island is already drafted. They just needed Arturo's translation to fill in the address."

Jake looked at the water. The blue-green of the mid-Straits. The Gulf Stream running beneath them.

"This has been planned for sixty years," he said.

"Since Ramon Santiesteban," Carla agreed. "Three generations of patience."

"And now someone is three hours ahead of us in a fast sail boat."

"Yes."

"And we have three days before a tropical wave shuts down the passage."

"Yes."

Jake thought.

"Tell the Coast Guard Sector Key West that we have one confirmed Italian national operating under a false cover identity in U.S. waters, in connection with an active FBI investigation, operating a vessel that has suppressed its transponder in U.S. territorial waters. That's a clear maritime law violation. They can request verification of the vessel's identity and that buys time."

"I can do that." A pause. "Jake. Even with that — by the time the Coast Guard locates a vessel that's turned off its AIS—"

"It buys time," Jake said. "That's all I need."

He ended the call and went up to the flying bridge.

Nacho was watching the chartplotter. He looked at Jake once, reading his face.

"Problem," Nacho said.

"The Carità is three hours ahead of us. Fast passage boat. They know the general region. They don't have the specific island."

Nacho looked at the chart. He was quiet for a moment, the quiet of a man consulting thirty years of water knowledge.

"In the Windward Passage, approaching from the north, there are seven islands that match the general description of what I know about Cayo Espiritu from the historical records," he said. "If they don't have the exact name and they're searching, they'll try the most likely candidates first. The ones that are largest, most accessible, most obviously have historical structure."

"Which isn't Cayo Espiritu."

"Cayo Espiritu is the least obvious one. It has no obvious landing beach — the only approach is through the reef passage on the northwest side, and you have to know where the passage is. The reef would put them off for at least a day if they don't know the route." He looked at Jake. "If they start with the obvious candidates, we might not be three hours behind."

"And if they've talked to someone who knows the passage—"

"Then we're three hours behind." Nacho looked at the horizon. "I know a shortcut. It's not on any chart. It's a passage through the Cay Sal Bank that you use when you know it — takes four hours off the southern approach." He paused. "It's shallow in places. You need to know the depth marks."

"Do you know them?"

Nacho looked at him with the expression of a man who has been asked whether he knows the way to his own kitchen.

"Okay," Jake said. "Take the shortcut."

---

They passed through the Cay Sal Bank at noon, in water that went from deep blue to the specific pale green of extreme shallowness and back to blue in the course of an hour, navigating by Nacho's charts and memory and the color-reading that comes from thirty years of Keys seamanship. The bottom was visible at points — sand and scattered coral at three meters, the kind of depth that would put most vessels aground if they tried to transit without knowledge.

Sofia was on the bow watching the water ahead and calling depths by hand signal. Nacho responded to each signal with a small adjustment of course that to a passenger looked like nothing but was, Jake understood, the difference between passage and shipwreck.

Elena came to stand near him at the stern rail during the bank passage.

"He's been worrying about the last cipher section all morning," she said. She meant Arturo, who had not emerged from below.

"Is he close?"

"He thinks so. He says the Phrygian mode hypothesis holds, but the verification symbols — the ones in the last line of the cipher text — he can't place them without hearing the sound." She paused. "He keeps saying: I need to hear it. Like a musician who can read a score but needs to hear the orchestra to be certain about the performance."

"That's what Diego built," Jake said. "A lock that requires being in the room."

"Yes." She was quiet. "My father built his entire professional life around the skill of reading things at a distance. Archives, documents, history — decoding things from far away. And the one thing he's been working toward for thirty years requires him to be physically present." She paused. "I don't think that's coincidental. I think Diego de Almeida made a very specific point about what kind of knowledge can be trusted."

Jake looked at the pale green water.

"What kind?" he asked.

"The kind that requires presence," she said. "The kind that can't be replicated by a document or a transmission or an account. The kind that only exists because you were there." She paused. "It's the same principle in science. The reproducible experiment. You don't trust a result you can't reproduce. Diego's verification cipher is the equivalent: you don't trust the translation unless you can reproduce the key. And you can only reproduce the key by being in the specific place."

Jake thought about this. "He built a method of authentication that resists forgery."

"He built a method of authentication that resists forgery across seven hundred years of technological change," she said. "Because a document can be forged. A cipher can be cracked with enough computational power. But a specific acoustic resonance in a specific geological formation at a specific tidal condition — that can't be faked in the abstract. You have to go there."

The water around them went back to deep blue.

"He was smart," Jake said.

"He was brilliant," she said. "And he was scared. And scared brilliant people build remarkable things."

They came out of the Cay Sal Bank into open water and Nacho opened the throttle and La Determinada put her shoulder into the sea and went south.

---

That afternoon Arturo came up from below. He had the expression of a man who has been working on a problem for several hours and has reached the point where the problem needs input from outside his own head.

He sat down next to Jake at the stern and looked at the ocean.

"In the last section of the cipher," he said, without preamble, "Diego uses a term I've been unable to definitively translate. The term is 'vox maris profundi.' The voice of the deep sea." He paused. "In the Latin of his era and region, 'profundus' has two distinct uses. It means deep in the spatial sense — deep water, deep cave. But it also means deep in the temporal sense — ancient, of long standing." He looked at Jake. "If it's the temporal meaning — and I think it is, because Diego uses it in contexts that suggest time rather than depth — then the phrase is not 'the voice of the deep sea' but 'the voice of the ancient sea.' Or more exactly: 'the voice of what the sea has been for a long time.'"

Jake absorbed this.

"What's the distinction?" he asked.

"The distinction is significant for the cipher. If the acoustic key uses the ancient quality of the sound — its age, its unchanged persistence, its geological permanence — rather than just its physical depth or loudness, then the modal identification I've been working on is insufficient. The key isn't just what mode the resonance is in. It's something about the resonance that makes it sound old." He looked at his hands. "Which is a concept I'm having difficulty formalizing. Because sound doesn't age. The physics of a resonant cavity is constant if the cavity doesn't change."

"But Diego thought it did," Jake said.

"Diego thought it communicated something about time," Arturo said. "Which was either poetic or literal. And the cipher requires literal." He paused. "Nacho. When you heard the sound — you described it to me years ago as specific. What else did you say? What was the quality of it?"

Nacho spoke from the helm without turning. "It sounded like something that had been there for a very long time. Not old the way an old building sounds old — echoing, diminished. Old the way the reef is old. The way the Caribbean is old." He paused. "It sounded like it had been making the same sound before there were people to hear it."

Arturo closed his eyes briefly. "The Phrygian mode," he said slowly. "In Arabic music theory — the mode Diego would have known from his sources — there's a specific variation called Phrygian dominant. It uses a raised second scale degree. This gives it what Arabic theorists described as a quality of 'primordial,' of something that predates civilization." He opened his eyes. "Not just old. First. Before."

"Is that the key?" Jake asked.

"I think it might be," Arturo said. "I need to hear it to be certain. But the modal structure I haven't been able to fit — the two unresolved symbols — I think they indicate the Phrygian dominant specifically. Not the regular Phrygian. The one that sounds before everything else."

He went below again.

Jake looked at the ocean around them — the deep Caribbean blue, the long horizon, the absolute absence of land in any direction. Three generations of Santiesteban patience. A medieval Portuguese navigator's careful encoding. A sixty-year wait and a thirty-four-year translation.

He thought: we're going to be there soon.

He thought: God, I hope we're not three hours too late.
