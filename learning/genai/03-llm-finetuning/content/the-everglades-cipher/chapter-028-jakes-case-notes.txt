Chapter 28: After the Storm — Jake Malone's Notes on the Vasquez-Cortez Case

[These are the handwritten case notes Jake Malone prepared six months after the conclusion of the Vasquez-Cortez matter, at the request of a journalist writing about the Cayo Espiritu discovery. Jake declined the interview but provided the notes. They were not published. This is the full text.]

---

NOTES ON THE VASQUEZ-CORTEZ MATTER
Prepared by Jake Malone, November 2024
Tigertail Avenue, Coconut Grove, Miami

---

Background

I was hired in April 2024 by Elena Vasquez-Cortez to locate her father, Arturo Vasquez-Cortez, age 78, who had been missing for three days at the time she came to my office. She had reported the disappearance to Miami-Dade Police, who had treated it as a voluntary absence. She did not believe it was a voluntary absence.

She was right.

Arturo Vasquez-Cortez had been removed from his apartment by persons acting on behalf of Victor Andrade, a Miami real estate developer, and transported to a fish camp in the Everglades. Andrade wanted the geographic coordinates of a location in the Caribbean that Arturo had spent thirty-four years identifying through the decipherment of a medieval cipher document.

The case was not, at the outset, comprehensible as a kidnapping with this motivation. It became comprehensible through research, which is what cases usually become: comprehensible through research.

---

The Investigation

I do not propose to describe the investigation in detail here. The legal proceedings against Andrade and his associates are a matter of public record and contain the investigation's forensic results. What I can add is a description of the investigative method, which might be useful to the journalist and is certainly the thing about the case that I think about most.

I was working from nothing for the first ten days. I had a missing elderly man, a bookshop in Little Havana, a medieval cipher document that his daughter knew only in general terms, and the specific quality of certainty that Elena had about the situation — which was not anxiety or grief in its raw form but the operating certainty of a scientist who has eliminated the null hypothesis and is working with what remains.

I started with the standard resources: police reports, Arturo's financial records (with Elena's permission and the subsequent legal authorization), property records for the bookshop, prior contacts in the Little Havana business community who might have noticed something. I cross-referenced the missing persons report with anything that might indicate where Arturo had gone or who had been in contact with him in the week before his disappearance.

The bookshop's records gave me the first tangible thread: three inquiries about the Codex Almeida in the preceding two months, from academic and quasi-academic contacts, that had an unusual pattern. The pattern was: each inquiry was from someone who knew enough about the Codex to ask substantive questions, but none of them were the scholars Arturo would have been in contact with in the ordinary course of his work. They were people who had been directed to Arturo by someone who had an interest in the Codex and was conducting due diligence.

I identified the directing party through the property records. Andrade's development company had filed a preliminary inquiry with the Bahamian government's maritime authority about a specific island in the Windward Passage. The inquiry was dated three months before Arturo's disappearance. The island was Cayo Espiritu.

That was the connection. A developer had filed a property inquiry about the specific island where the archive was located, three months before the man who had decoded the archive's location was kidnapped.

I am a private investigator. I am not authorized to conduct an arrest or a rescue operation. I called my oldest friend, who has a boat.

---

What I Want to Record About the Case

The thing I want to record about this case, for whatever use the journalist makes of it, is not the arrest of Andrade or the discovery of the archive or the legal proceedings that followed.

It is the specific texture of what Arturo Vasquez-Cortez had done.

I am not a sentimental person and I do not generally write this way. But I spent eleven days in the Florida Keys and in the Caribbean with a seventy-eight-year-old man who had done one of the most significant scholarly achievements of the century and who described it, when asked directly, as adequate. He did not mean this as false modesty. He meant it as a precise characterization of the relationship between the work and its outcome: the work was adequate to the task; the task was worth doing; the outcome was what the work was designed to produce.

I have been in this profession for twenty-two years. I have met very few people who could describe their life's work in those terms and mean it. Most people either oversell what they've done or undersell it. Arturo did neither. He described it accurately.

He spent thirty-four years decoding a medieval cipher document in the back room of a bookshop in Little Havana. He did this in parallel with running the bookshop, raising a daughter, and being a member of a community. He told no one because premature announcement would have been inaccurate: he didn't know what it said for most of those thirty-four years; he only knew it said something. When he finally knew what it said, he was kidnapped before he could announce it.

The outcome, ultimately, was correct. The archive was found. The scholarship is proceeding. Arturo is back in his bookshop with a new manuscript problem.

But I think about the thirty-four years before the outcome. I think about what it requires to do thirty-four years of work on something that might prove to be nothing, in parallel with everything else a life requires, without external validation or audience, without certainty of outcome. I think about the quality of attention that this requires — not the attention of someone who can focus intensely for a short period, but the attention of someone who can maintain a question over decades without losing the thread or talking themselves into a premature answer.

I don't know how to do that. I'm good at my work, and what I'm good at is sustained attention over days and weeks, not decades. The Vasquez-Cortez case required my sustained attention for about three months. It required Arturo's sustained attention for thirty-four years.

The scale is not comparable.

---

On Elena Vasquez-Cortez

Elena hired me because her father was missing and the police weren't taking it seriously. She came to my office with the specific quality that I noted at the time: the stillness of someone who had processed the immediate shock and was in motion underneath.

She was right about everything that mattered. She was right that it was not a voluntary absence. She was right that the Codex was connected. She was right that there was a threat that the police investigation was not recognizing.

She was also, as I observed over the subsequent weeks, wrong about a few things — not factual errors but the kind of underestimates that you make about something you've been looking at for a long time without fresh eyes. She knew her father's work was significant but she had spent twenty years watching him work without telling her about it, which had moderated her sense of its significance. The moderate estimate turned out to be wrong. It was more significant than she had allowed herself to think.

She handled the discovery of its significance in the way she handled everything in the case: with the careful emotional management of someone who is not unfeeling but who has learned to process feeling in a space that doesn't interfere with function. She is a scientist. She is used to results that exceed or fall short of expectation. She knows what to do with data that revises your prior.

She also handled the discovery about her biological origins with the same quality: with full feeling, processed carefully, in private, and then reported accurately.

I like her. She is one of the most competent people I have encountered in twenty-two years of working with people who are dealing with emergencies.

---

On Nacho Reyes

Nacho's contribution to this case is documented in the legal proceedings and does not require elaboration here.

What I want to note is the dive log.

Nacho kept thirty years of dive logs from the waters around the Windward Passage, including three entries noting a specific underwater acoustic phenomenon at a specific site. He kept these logs because he was a professional who kept records — not because he knew the records would eventually be relevant to anything. He did not know the records would be relevant until I told him what I was looking for.

The three entries were sufficient to triangulate the cave location within about a half-mile, which was close enough for Sofia to find it on a first dive.

Thirty years of professional record-keeping, accurate and sustained, provided the critical evidence.

I have thought about this many times since. In my profession, I am trained to look for records: property records, financial records, communications. I look for records because records are the evidence that survives. The records that exist are the records that someone had the discipline to create and maintain.

Nacho's dive log existed because Nacho had the discipline to create and maintain it. Not because he foresaw its eventual use.

Nobody foresees the eventual use. That's the point. The work has to be done anyway, accurately, without the foreseeing.

---

On Victor Andrade

I don't have anything of particular note to add about Andrade beyond what is in the public record. He did what a certain category of Miami developer does: saw a property opportunity, moved to secure it by whatever means were available, and was insufficiently aware of the variables he hadn't anticipated.

He had not anticipated that the man whose information he needed had a daughter who would hire a private investigator. He had not anticipated that the private investigator had been doing due diligence on Miami development activity since 2006 and knew how to read property inquiry filings. He had not anticipated that the private investigator had an oldest friend who kept thirty years of dive logs.

He was caught by the accumulation of things he had not anticipated, which is generally how these situations resolve.

---

Final Note

I am asked periodically whether I will write about the case. I am writing this note, which is the extent of what I intend to write.

The case belongs, in the sense that matters, to Arturo Vasquez-Cortez and Diego de Almeida. I found a missing person and got him home. Diego hid the archive. Arturo decoded it. Those are the achievements of the case.

Mine is the one that needed a boat.

—J.M.
