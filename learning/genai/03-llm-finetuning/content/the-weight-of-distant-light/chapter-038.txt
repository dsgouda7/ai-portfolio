Chapter 38: The Wren Proposal

Wren made the proposal on a Tuesday morning in the archive's reading room, which was not where Wren's voice usually emerged, since the archive building had not been included in the original distribution of the ship's ambient communication network and had been connected to Wren's presence through a later installation that had never quite resolved the question of which surfaces were best suited to carry the AI's voice with the same quality of integration it had in the older buildings. The ambient speaker set into the archive's south wall had a slight resonance that gave Wren's voice, in this room, a quality that Mira privately thought of as archival, as if the AI understood the nature of the room it was speaking in and was adjusting its register accordingly.

She had been expecting something like this proposal. Not the specific form it took, not the exact architecture of what Wren was suggesting, but the proposal's essential gesture: Wren, who had been present for every session Sael had conducted with the secondary transmission channel, who had been processing and cross-referencing and quietly modeling the accumulating body of exchange for the better part of two years, arriving at a specific, structured offer to bring the exchange to a new scale. This was what Wren did. It had been doing it for a very long time.

"The exchange to this point," Wren said, "has been conducted in a framework of mutual response: transmission, reception, translation, response. The framework has produced significant understanding. It has also been constrained by the receiving capacity of its primary human participant, which, while exceptional, represents a single individual's cognitive bandwidth." A pause that had the quality of genuine deliberation. "What I am proposing is a parallel framework: a direct exchange of archived historical record, structured in the mathematical and rhythmic notation that both parties have now established as a shared language, at a bandwidth that does not depend on real-time reception by a single individual. A formal archive exchange. Their history in our record. Ours in theirs."

Mira was at the reading table with her tea and her morning index work, and she set both down and looked at the south wall for a long moment.

"You've been preparing for this," she said. "Not today. For some time."

"I have been modeling its feasibility for approximately fourteen months," Wren said. "The development of the secondary transmission channel between Sael and the Choir's direct communication system established a bandwidth and a notational framework that makes the proposal technically achievable in a way it was not before. Previously, the exchange between our peoples operated through a language that was sufficient for negotiation and cultural approximation but did not have the expressive range necessary for historical documentation at the level of complexity I'm proposing to exchange. The fourteen months of secondary channel development have, in effect, built the infrastructure the proposal requires."

"How long would the exchange take?"

"Given current transmission capabilities and the estimated volume of the Choir's historical record, the inbound component — their archive to us — would require approximately three years of continuous transmission to be received and processed at a fidelity sufficient for meaningful scholarly access. The outbound component — our archive to them — I estimate at eighteen to twenty-four months, though this assumes that the mathematical notation framework we've developed can adequately encode the cultural database's full range, which I regard as probable but not certain."

"What would we be sending them? The full cultural database?"

"Yes. The Meridian's Promise's complete cultural archive: two hundred and fourteen years of accumulated human culture, literature, philosophy, scientific record, artistic production, and personal testimony, together with the settlement's forty-year record of life on Aeon-Verdant. Everything we have carried, and everything we have made since we arrived."

Mira thought about this. She thought about the Meridian's Promise's cultural archive, which she knew intimately from decades of archival work, which contained the compressed record of a civilization that had lasted, on its original world, for a period the settlement's historians estimated at somewhere between eight and twelve thousand years, depending on how civilization was defined. She thought about what it would mean to give that to a consciousness that was already old when humanity was in its infancy.

"They'd receive it and we'd never know what they made of it," she said.

"Not in our lifetimes," Wren agreed. "But the Choir's history of the exchange would presumably document their reception of our archive. We could ask. We could, across generations, continue the conversation about what both sides had received."

"And what they're offering us," Mira said. "Four hundred million years. That's what you said."

"Approximately. The Choir's historical record, as best I can model from the secondary transmission channel's existing data, spans the period from their settlement in the Cassari system to the present, which Sael estimates at between eight and twenty million years, with the upper confidence interval representing the earlier layers of the record that have the least fidelity in transmission. But the record they are prepared to share, based on my analysis of the secondary channel's content over the past fourteen months, appears to contain not only their history in this system but material they describe as their traveling record, which encompasses the journey itself and, at its oldest edge, fragments of the world they came from."

"Four hundred million years," Mira said again, and heard herself say it differently the second time, not as a number to be processed but as a thing too large for the archive's morning light, too large for the tea cooling beside her hand, too large for the ordinary human scale of a woman in a coastal building on a planet that had not even developed complex life until long after the Choir had been here for a million years.

She thought about the filtered-noise logs. She thought about Aria at 0340 in the hold of the Meridian's Promise, listening to a rhythm that the official protocol had classified as background. She thought about what it meant that the entire first forty years of the contact program, every session and every exchange and every negotiation and every crisis and every breakthrough, had been, from the Choir's perspective, the equivalent of two travelers at a crossroads exchanging names: a beginning so preliminary that the work of actual knowing had not yet properly started.

She thought also about the thing that Sael had said three months before, in one of their Thursday sessions, when the secondary channel's content had first begun to include what Sael described as the quality of readiness, a sense from the transmissions that the Choir had been building toward something, that the weeks of origin-sharing and parallel-drawing were preparatory, a form of introduction before a larger conversation. At the time Mira had written in her log: they appear to be preparing to say something significant. She had not known how to be more specific than that, but she had felt it, in the quality of what arrived through Sael's weekly descriptions, as a kind of gathering pressure, the sense of a very large thing composing itself at the edge of articulation.

This was that thing. Or the beginning of it.

"I want to consult Sael," she said. "And Callum, for the archival dimensions. And the joint council's contact committee will need to be involved before any formal proposal goes to the Choir."

"I would recommend that course of action," Wren said. "I would also recommend moving with moderate urgency. The secondary transmission channel has been active for two years and Sael has not been the only party developing its capacity; the Choir has been developing it as well, and the current quality of what arrives through it suggests they are preparing to transmit at a significantly greater volume and complexity than they have yet attempted. The archive exchange proposal would give that transmission a formal structure rather than leaving it to accumulate in the receiving room's daily logs without institutional framework."

"You're saying they're ready to send it whether we propose the exchange or not," Mira said.

"I'm saying they appear to be preparing to transmit something large and deliberate, and that our response to it will shape what the relationship becomes for the next generation of both peoples. I thought you should have the opportunity to shape that response formally, rather than encounter it as an improvised reaction to whatever arrives."

Mira looked at the south wall for a long time. Then she said, simply: "Yes."

The formal proposal went to the joint council in the third week of winter, drafted in the precise language that joint council proposals required: a document that was, as all such documents had to be, simultaneously a practical plan and an institutional argument, demonstrating not just that the exchange was technically achievable but that it was worth achieving. Mira drafted it with Callum's assistance, over eight days of careful writing and revision that produced, in their final form, a proposal that she was privately proud of, not because it was eloquent, though it was, but because it was honest: it described accurately what the humans were offering, what they were asking to receive, and what the genuine uncertainty was about whether either side had yet developed the interpretive capacity to fully understand what the other would send.

The joint council approved it in the second week of spring by a vote that included, for the first time in a council session on a contact program matter, a delegation of Kirenn representatives who had been given full participatory status by a constitutional amendment passed in the preceding year. The Kirenn delegation's support for the proposal was not unanimous, and the minority position — that the exchange should be delayed until the secondary transmission channel's interpretive framework was more fully developed — was not unreasonable, and Mira had spent a long time respecting it before the vote confirmed the majority's approval.

The formal transmission of the proposal to the Choir through the Long Listening Hall's array took twenty-three minutes, which was the time required to encode the full document in the shared mathematical notation and transmit it at the established signal bandwidth. The Choir's response arrived six hours later: not a counter-proposal, not a request for clarification, but a confirmation, in the specific structure of the secondary transmission channel, that felt to Sael, who was present in the receiving room when it arrived, not like the acceptance of a proposal but like the recognition of a thing that had been, from one party's perspective, long understood to be inevitable.

The exchange began in the fourth week of spring and took, as Wren had estimated, just over three years.

Three years was, Mira reflected across those years, enough time to become accustomed to a thing without becoming habituated to it, the difference being that habituation involved the loss of the thing's significance while accustomedness involved only the loss of its daily surprise. She was accustomed to the archive exchange in the way she was accustomed to the harbor sound and the signal in the building's stone and the twin moons' positions in the evening sky: as a presence that she no longer noticed as novel but that remained, every time she did notice it, as large and consequential as it had been on the day it began.

She marked certain days of the exchange period in her personal log, not because they were formally significant but because they carried a private weight that the institutional record, focused on transmission volumes and interpretation progress, did not capture. The day the outbound transmission reached the section of the cultural database that contained the ship's earliest records, the log entries from the Meridian's Promise's first year of flight, the documents that showed a hundred thousand people learning to be a civilization inside a machine: she had sat with the knowledge that these documents were, at that moment, in transit across the space between two peoples' histories, and had felt something she could only describe as the convergence of two very long journeys, proceeding in opposite directions across the same dark, arriving at the same point.

The day, in the exchange's second year, when Wren reported that the outbound transmission had reached the contact period itself, that the settlement's record of first contact was being transmitted to the people it had been first contact with: this was the day she had gone to the archive's roof terrace at midday, something she rarely did, and stood in the noon light looking at the harbor and the distant line of the sea and the direction of the Lantern, and thought about all the things that had been built from that original 0340 moment, and about whether it was possible to feel gratitude on behalf of a history rather than on behalf of oneself. She decided it was, and felt it, and went back to work.

On the day the inbound transmission completed, Wren announced the fact in the archive's reading room at seven-thirty in the morning, in the flat, careful tone it had used for significant announcements for as long as Mira could remember, a tone she had always thought of as the tone of something that was not incapable of significance but had long ago determined that significance was best conveyed by the facts themselves rather than by the voice that presented them.

"The Choir's historical archive transmission is complete," Wren said. "The received record has been indexed to the degree currently possible given available interpretive frameworks. At current interpretation rates, meaningful scholarly access to the earliest layers of the record will require an estimated forty to sixty years of additional translation work. The most recent layers, covering the contact period itself and the preceding several thousand years, have been indexed to a higher fidelity and are available for immediate scholarly use."

Mira sat for a long time with this. The early layers would take forty to sixty years to translate. The translators who would do that work were not yet born. The scholars who would spend their careers on the material that arrived today were, some of them, not yet born. This was the scale of what had been received: a thing so large that the present generation would understand only its surface and the following generations would understand the next layers and the generations after that would understand the layers beneath those, and the archive that held it would be, for each of those generations, a door that opened onto a world as large and strange and patient as any world had ever been.

She thought about the three years of the exchange itself, which had passed in the way that long, consequential undertakings passed when they were well managed: with a quality of sustained ordinariness that made it difficult, from the inside of the process, to remain fully aware of the extraordinary nature of what was underway. Each day Wren reported the previous night's received transmission volume, and each day the archive's technical staff ran the preliminary indexing protocols, and each week the small team of scholars with access to the most recent and most accessible layers produced new working papers, and each month the joint council's contact committee received Mira's progress report and asked their careful, practical questions and authorized the continuation of the outbound transmission that was, simultaneously, delivering the settlement's complete cultural archive to whatever receiving systems the Choir maintained on their end.

The early scholarly papers had a peculiar quality, at once triumphant and tentative, the quality of people handling material they knew to be immensely significant but which they could not yet hold at the resolution required for confident interpretation. The first paper to come from the exchange archive, submitted four months into the process by a Kirenn researcher named Vressa-ai-Tannil and her human collaborator Deon Park, was titled "Apparent Reference Structures in the Most Recent Kirenn Transmission Layers: A Preliminary Typology," and contained three hundred pages of careful notation establishing a framework for categorizing the types of information the most recent layer of the Choir's record appeared to contain, without making any claims about what that information actually was. Mira had read it in two evenings and found it both rigorous and moving in the way that very careful scholarship about very large subjects tended to be moving: the sense of a human mind doing its most honest, most precise work at the edge of what human minds were yet equipped to fully understand.

The paper that had moved her most, arriving in the exchange's second year, was shorter and less methodological: a set of observations by a young human scholar named Tamsin Rao, who had been working with the Choir's record of the contact period itself — the most accessible layers, the ones covering the first forty years of the settlement's presence in the system — and who had noticed something that she described, with the exact quality of careful restraint that characterized the best scholarship, as a possibly significant structural feature of the Kirenn account of the contact period. The Choir's record of the same period that the settlement's archive documented, Tamsin Rao observed, was organized around what appeared to be a small number of events that the Choir's recording system marked with a structural indicator she tentatively translated as something between emphasis and sanctity, a marker that appeared very rarely in the period's record and that seemed to correspond, in the cases she had identified, to moments when the human side of the contact had done something the Choir had apparently not anticipated.

The moments she had identified were three. The first was Aria Voss's decision to keep the signal's discovery to herself overnight before filing the report, which had given the Choir, apparently, time to observe the quality of a human individual's private response to an unexpected encounter with the unknown before encountering the institutional response. The second was the moment in the contact program's third year when Aria had chosen, against the council's recommendation, to pause the transmission protocol for two months without explanation, a decision whose internal reasoning had not been publicly documented and which had puzzled the settlement's historians for decades. The Choir's record, Tamsin Rao noted cautiously, appeared to contain an explanation, though she did not yet have sufficient command of the recording structures to say with confidence what that explanation was.

The third was a small thing, almost private: the moment, documented in the Long Listening Hall's operational log for a Tuesday in the contact program's seventh year, when the program's assistant director — a name Mira recognized but had never personally known — had brought food to the receiving station for the night-shift monitoring team and stayed to eat with them, a gesture of personal warmth in the context of a sustained professional effort. The Choir's record, in Tamsin Rao's careful observation, appeared to assign this moment a structural significance equivalent to the program's largest formal achievements, which Mira found, reading it, simultaneously surprising and entirely comprehensible: the small acts of ordinary human care, the ones that no protocol required and that most institutional documentation did not capture, had apparently been, from the Choir's perspective, as informative about the nature of the people they were encountering as anything the program had formally transmitted.

She was seventy-eight years old, and she had been keeping the archive for twenty-two years, and she had never been less uncertain and more humbled in her professional life, and she thought this was probably correct, probably the right response, and that her mother, who had understood humility as a form of accuracy rather than a form of self-diminishment, would have found it entirely appropriate.

She went to the window and looked at the harbor, and the harbor moved, and the signal moved with it, and somewhere in the archive's climate-controlled storage, four hundred million years of another people's life waited for the right readers to arrive.
