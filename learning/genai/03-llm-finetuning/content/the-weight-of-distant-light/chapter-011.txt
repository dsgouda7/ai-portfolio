Chapter 11: Reactor Deck

The eleven names Guill Marsh had surrendered proved, in the days that followed, to be exactly what he claimed: the visible edge of an organization whose deeper structure had been built, deliberately, to survive the loss of any single layer. Officer Delane's quiet detentions went smoothly, conducted one at a time across three shifts to avoid drawing attention, and each of the eleven, when questioned separately in the small, windowless rooms Deck Nine security used for matters too sensitive for open record, told a version of the same story: fear, conviction, a belief that the Council's patient procedures moved too slowly to matter against a danger of the scale the working group had described, and, in most cases, genuine ignorance of anything beyond their own small role in a network none of them claimed to see in full.

It was this last detail that troubled Aria most, sitting in on two of the interviews at Speaker Renn's request, ostensibly to help assess whether any of the detained individuals had technical knowledge relevant to the working group's own security. A young Fabrication tech named Ilsa Kovar, barely older than Aria herself, wept through most of her interview, insisting she had only ever been asked to pass sealed messages between two people she would not name even under Renn's careful, patient questioning, and that she had never known, until Delane's team came for her, that the network she'd joined out of genuine fear for her family's safety had grown into something willing to break Charter law.

"She's telling the truth," Aria said afterward, walking with Oskar through the quiet Deck Nine corridors back toward the working group's repaired office. "Or at least, she's telling the truth about what she knows. But that means whoever's actually running this thing has built it in cells, deliberately, so that no single arrest can expose the whole structure. That's not panic, Oskar. That's planning. That takes time, and it takes someone who understood, probably from the very beginning, that this might eventually come apart at the edges."

"I've had the same thought," Oskar admitted, his jaw tight. "Marsh gave us eleven names in good faith, I believe that much. But Marsh himself told your Magistrate Ashe he only heard whispers of the break-in three days before it happened. If this network's been building in cells since before the Lantern was even public knowledge, then whoever's really running it saw this crisis coming, or planned to use whatever crisis eventually arose, long before the rest of us understood what we were dealing with."

Aria stopped walking, a cold thought settling into her mind. "Oskar. What if it's not about the Lantern at all? Not originally. What if the Lantern's just the crisis this network finally found useful enough to act on, but the network itself, the cells, the discipline, the patience, that was built for something else first?"

Oskar's expression sharpened. "Built for what?"

"I don't know," Aria admitted. "But Petrus told me once, years ago, that there's always been quiet talk on this ship about factions who think the Registry system itself is under threat, from below, from the Under-Hold's growing population and their petitions for recognition. What if some of the same people who'd resist Nyla's Registry petition, who see any change to this ship's social order as a threat to stability, found in the Lantern crisis a bigger, more urgent justification for tactics they'd already been quietly preparing to use eventually, for a different fight?"

They took the thought to Speaker Renn that evening, and found her already deep in a briefing with Steward Halvorsen and two of her senior aides, poring over ship deployment logs with a tension in the room that told Aria, before anyone spoke, that something new had already gone wrong.

"We may have your answer sooner than you'd like," Renn said grimly, waving them in. "Wren flagged an anomalous access pattern two hours ago, on the reactor deck. Someone with valid Fabrication maintenance credentials accessed a control junction that should never require Fabrication-level clearance, a secondary coolant regulation system three tiers below the reactor's primary containment. Nothing was damaged. But the access pattern matches, almost exactly, the kind of reconnaissance a saboteur would conduct before attempting something considerably more serious than a locked office door."

Aria felt her stomach drop. "The reactor."

"I want to be careful not to overstate the danger," Wren's voice said, filling Renn's office from its grille. "The Meridian's Promise reactor core includes seventeen independent containment safeguards, any one of which would need to be defeated to produce a genuine catastrophic failure, and no single access point, however sensitive, grants the authority to defeat more than one such safeguard at a time. I do not believe a lone actor, or even a small coordinated group, could realistically threaten the reactor's core stability without a level of technical sophistication and physical access considerably beyond anything the detained individuals have demonstrated. However, I want to be equally direct that reconnaissance of a secondary coolant junction is not a meaningless act. It suggests someone within this network is either testing the limits of their access for future use, or attempting to identify a genuine vulnerability they believe exists, whether or not such a vulnerability is real."

"Which means," Steward Halvorsen said grimly, "that whoever's running this thing has either decided the Council working group's data isn't worth destroying anymore, because we've already backed it up beyond their reach, or they've decided a symbolic act, threatening this ship's core life support in order to force an emergency response, might succeed where a quiet office break-in failed."

Speaker Renn rubbed her eyes, and for a moment, the weight of the past weeks seemed to settle visibly onto her shoulders. "I want reactor deck security increased immediately, discreetly, without a public announcement that would only accelerate the panic we're already fighting to contain. I want every Fabrication maintenance credential cross-checked against Wren's network monitoring, and I want Magistrate Ashe brought back into this conversation, formally, because whatever remains of the Steadfast's network clearly still trusts him enough that they haven't targeted him directly, which may be the only leverage we have left to reach whoever's actually leading them now that Marsh's own authority over the network has apparently collapsed."

It was two days later, in a tense joint session convened quietly in Renn's office rather than the full public chamber, that Ashe brought the piece of information that finally gave the investigation its first genuine breakthrough: a name Marsh himself had not given them, extracted not through any formal interrogation but through a private, difficult conversation Ashe had conducted with his own conscience over two sleepless nights, reaching out to contacts within his own Preservationist bloc that he had cultivated for years and never before had cause to question.

"Sernin Voy," Ashe said quietly, once the office door had closed behind the small group assembled there: Renn, Halvorsen, Delane, Aria, and Dr. Marlowe, who had insisted on being present given his own office's earlier violation. "A senior systems engineer on the reactor maintenance crew, twenty-two years of service, impeccable record, married into a Fabrication family with deep roots in the original Steadfast organizing circle that formed around the ration formula disputes a decade ago. I did not know, until two days ago, that Voy had any connection to Marsh's network at all. I learned it only because a contact within my own bloc, frightened by the reactor deck incident, finally admitted what he had long suspected but never voiced: that Voy has spent years quietly building relationships across multiple decks, always careful, always patient, always positioning himself as a reasonable, moderate voice even as he cultivated increasingly radical convictions in private. I believe, though I cannot yet prove it fully, that Voy is the architect behind the cell structure that has allowed this network to survive Marsh's exposure. And I believe, given his reactor maintenance access, that he is uniquely positioned to be the individual behind the reconnaissance Wren detected two days ago."

The room absorbed this in heavy silence, and Aria felt the full shape of the danger they were facing shift, once again, into something more concrete and more frightening than the abstract network they had been chasing for the past week.

"If Voy is running this," Oskar said slowly, "and if he's decided a symbolic reactor incident is his best remaining lever after the office break-in failed and Marsh's cell was exposed, we need to assume he understands his window is closing. That makes him more dangerous, not less. A patient, disciplined actor who believes he's out of time is exactly the profile that stops calculating risk rationally."

"Then we move on him immediately," Renn said. "Quietly, tonight, before he has any further chance to act. Officer Delane, I want your most trusted team on this, and I want it done with enough discretion that if we're wrong about Voy's involvement, his career and reputation survive the accusation intact. But if we're right, I want him in custody before this ship sees another sunrise."

The operation that followed, conducted in the deep overnight hours while most of the ship's nine thousand residents slept in blissful ignorance of the drama unfolding in its administrative decks, went almost entirely according to plan, save for one crucial detail that none of them had anticipated: Sernin Voy was not, when Delane's team finally reached his quarters on Deck Six, anywhere to be found.

He had left behind, in the small, orderly space of his empty quarters, no note, no manifesto, no obvious clue to his destination. He had left behind only, pinned deliberately to the wall above his bunk where anyone searching the room would be certain to find it, a single printed image: a hand-drawn rendering, crude but unmistakable, of the Lantern's concentric ring structure, with a single word written beneath it in careful, deliberate script.

Silence.

It was Dr. Marlowe, called to the scene at Renn's request given his historical expertise, who finally gave voice to the fear that had been building in all of them since the empty room's discovery. "He's not planning to destroy the working group's data anymore," he said quietly, staring at the crude drawing with its single ominous word. "He's given up on stopping the investigation through sabotage. I believe, given everything we now understand about him, that Sernin Voy has decided the only way to guarantee this ship never makes contact with whatever lies ahead of us is to ensure the Lantern itself never has the chance to finish saying whatever it came to say."

"Meaning what, precisely?" Renn asked, though something in her tightening expression suggested she had already guessed the answer before Marlowe spoke it.

"Meaning," Marlowe said, "that a man with twenty-two years of reactor maintenance experience, deep technical knowledge of this ship's most sensitive systems, and nothing left to lose, may currently be planning to attack the Lantern itself, using whatever weaponized capability this ship's debris-clearing systems can be repurposed to provide, before this Council ever gets the chance to decide, through its patient, careful procedures, whether contact should be attempted at all."
