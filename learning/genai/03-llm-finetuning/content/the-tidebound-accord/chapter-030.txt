Chapter 30: Contrary Voices

The voyage to the outer islands took three days on the courier vessel that ran the eastern correspondent route, and Sera spent most of it on deck in the wind regardless of the weather, watching the archipelago resolve and dissolve around them as they moved through it. This was one of the things she had missed most acutely in three years at the Academy — not the open sea itself but this: the specific experience of the archipelago as a collection of distinct places, each island with its own quality, its own smell on the wind, its own relationship to the tide-veins beneath it. In Deepwater Crossing the islands blurred together into the general category of city-ness, and the tide-veins beneath the Academy's campus had been so worked over by centuries of practice that they felt smooth and characterless, like a river path worn to bare mud. Out here the veins had their own personalities: the stone-cold solidity of the deep northern islands, the warm brackish quality of the shallower southern chain, and here in the east, as they moved further from the central islands, something older and more deliberate, like the difference between a road and the original path through old-growth forest that the road had eventually been laid over.

Tam traveled with her. He had not precisely explained why he was coming personally rather than sending correspondence, but Sera understood it had something to do with the nature of what they were going to see. The void-touched weavers needed to be interviewed by someone with direct experience of the phenomenon, and the communities needed to be addressed by someone with the institutional weight to be taken seriously. She was too young and too junior for the second requirement, and Tam was not young enough for certain kinds of agility that the situation might demand. They made, she thought, a reasonable complement.

They had eleven interview appointments across six islands, scheduled by the correspondent network over three weeks of planning. The plan was four days out and four days back with a week in the islands, visiting communities in pairs of proximity where possible. Tam had a bad knee that objected to damp weather, which the outer archipelago provided in abundance, and he managed this without complaint by a system of strategic pausing that Sera had learned to read as the signal for a rest break.

Shellhaven was first.

The island was low and windswept, its fishing community clustered at the base of the only significant elevation, the eastern cliff that blocked the worst of the winter storms. The houses were built low and thick-walled in the way of communities that had been managing severe weather for many generations, their gardens protected by stone windbreaks that had been assembled and reassembled so many times the stone itself had developed a kind of weathered wisdom, each piece fitted to its neighbor with a precision that came not from design but from the accumulated adjustment of decades. The harbor was small and busy, the boats older than the boats in Deepwater Crossing's harbor but maintained with a care that said these communities could not afford to replace what could be repaired. The community's Academy correspondent, a woman named Lira who did double duty as the island's record-keeper and who had been managing the correspondence route for twenty years, met them at the dock with an expression that communicated both relief and worry in roughly equal proportions.

"Three more reports since I sent the last dispatch," she told Tam as they walked up from the harbor. "Two of them are from the same family — the Greenstays, over on the north shore. Both water-weavers, both had episodes within a day of each other." She paused. "And there's something else. Cors Brinestep — the first one I reported — she's had a second episode."

Tam stopped walking. "The same character as the first?"

"She says different. The first one was just the darkness, she says. The second one — she describes something she's having trouble putting words to. She says it felt like a conversation she was not a participant in. Like being a room where a conversation was happening that had nothing to do with her but was happening through her."

Sera and Tam looked at each other briefly. Through her, not around her.

"The fragments are getting clearer," Sera said quietly.

"Yes," Tam said. He looked at Lira. "We need to see Brinestep first."

Cors Brinestep was a compact, weathered woman with the permanently salt-roughened hands of someone who worked the nets regularly regardless of her weaving practice. She received them in her house, which was tidy and smelled of fish oil and bread, and answered their questions with the careful precision of someone who has been thinking about an experience she doesn't understand and has decided that accuracy is more useful than a comfortable narrative.

The first episode she described in familiar terms: the dark space, the depth, the brief presence that noticed her. Sera asked her questions about the character of the recognition she had felt — whether it had a quality of searching, of being found — and Brinestep looked at her sharply and said: "You know what it is."

"I know something about it," Sera said. "What was the second episode like?"

The second one was different in ways that Brinestep struggled to verbalize but that Sera found, as she listened, had a clear pattern. The spatial ambiguity was the same but less dominant, as though the void was less relevant to the second episode's purpose than it had been to the first. The presence was more specific: not vast and fragmented but focused to a kind of point, a concentrated direction. And what had passed through her — what she called the conversation happening through her — she described as a quality of orientation, of direction-finding, as though she had been briefly used as an instrument of navigation.

"Navigation," Sera said.

"I know it sounds strange," Brinestep said.

"It doesn't," Sera said. "Did you have any sense of what was being navigated toward?"

Brinestep thought for a long time. Outside, the wind off the cliff was audible, the particular sound of wind in the architecture of a low stone community — not howling but working, pressing itself through the gaps in the wall-stones with a quiet purposefulness that was almost conversational. The sound of an island managing its weather.

"Toward something particular. Something it had been trying to find for a long time," Brinestep said finally. She looked at her hands. "It didn't feel hostile. That's the thing I keep coming back to. The first one was frightening because it was strange and I didn't understand it. The second one — I still didn't understand it, but it didn't feel hostile. It felt like..." She paused. "Like something old and very tired trying to go home."

The Greenstay interviews confirmed the pattern: both weavers described similar episodes, the darkness and the presence, and both used language about direction and searching. The father, older and more formally grounded in technical weaving terminology, described it as the fragments running a triangulation. Multiple access points, brief contacts, building a map.

A map of what?

By the third island, Sera had begun to suspect.

The community of Highwater Ridge on the island of Stonepoint gave them their first encounter with Polder Ashcroft, who they had known they would encounter eventually because his name had been appearing in the correspondence reports for six months as a growing local presence. He was waiting at the community hall when they arrived — not for them specifically, they understood quickly, but because the community hall was where the island's public disputes were conducted and he was currently conducting one.

He was perhaps fifty, with the heavy-shouldered build of someone who had spent decades in physical labor and the particular quality of conviction that came from having found, relatively late in life, a framework that explained everything he had experienced and everything he feared. He was a stone-weaver, which Sera noted, because stone-weavers were often particularly resistant to the idea that the void could interact with their element — their practice was founded on solidity, resistance, the certainty of mass and weight. The void was antithetical to all of that, in theory. But theory and experience did not always align, and the void-touched incidents had included stone-weavers in their number, which Ashcroft had apparently found personally disturbing in ways that had calcified into something more public.

His supporters numbered perhaps thirty in the hall, which held closer to a hundred people, but the thirty had the specific energy of people who had rehearsed their arguments recently and were ready to use them.

"The void-touching has nothing to do with fragments of some ancient story," he was saying, when Tam and Sera came in. His voice had the carrying quality of someone who has used it to be heard over wind and weather. "It's the weavers themselves. They're losing control. They're touching things they shouldn't touch, opening doors they should keep closed, and then the Academy tells you it's nothing to worry about and sends investigators to confirm there's nothing to worry about. Ask yourself who benefits from that story."

This last line he directed at Tam and Sera, who had become visible as newcomers as soon as the door opened.

Tam handled this with the patience of someone who had been in a great many contentious public conversations over seventy years. He did not attempt to dismiss Ashcroft or the crowd's anxiety. He acknowledged that the reports were real and were being taken seriously, and that the Academy investigation was specifically aimed at understanding what had happened to the affected weavers, not at minimizing their experience. He said this with the particular quality of an institution taking responsibility rather than defending itself, which Sera watched the audience respond to: not with immediate conversion, but with the slight shift that indicated the framework had become slightly less settled, slightly more open to revision.

Ashcroft was not moved.

"Resonants," he said. "That's the next thing they'll tell you. The resonants at the Academy are responsible for this, because they're the ones who work with the void. All five tides — no one should be touching all five tides. Kerra Valmont said so herself, and then she spent fifty years teaching people to do exactly that." He looked at Sera directly. "How old are you?"

"Nineteen," she said.

"Third year?"

"Yes."

"And you're here to tell us that everything is under control." His voice was not hostile, which was somehow harder to manage than hostility would have been. He was reasonable in his unreason, which meant he could not be dismissed.

"No," Sera said. "I'm here to understand what happened to your weavers, because what happened to them has also happened to me, and I need to know whether we're dealing with the same thing."

This produced silence. Ashcroft looked at her. She held his gaze.

"You were void-touched," he said. "A resonant."

"Yes."

"And the Academy is investigating its own student for being void-touched."

"The Academy is investigating an anomalous void-tide phenomenon that is affecting weavers across the archipelago, resonant and single-tide both. I'm part of the investigation, not the subject of it." She paused. "Your weavers are not in danger from the episodes themselves. The phenomenon we're investigating is not harmful to the people it passes through. I'm not saying it isn't alarming — it is alarming. But the alarm should be directed at understanding what is causing it, not at the weavers it is happening to."

Ashcroft regarded her for another long moment.

"It hasn't hurt anyone yet," he said, and the yet carried weight. "But it frightened people. It frightened people who had no reason to be frightened, who went about their work in good faith and got taken somewhere they didn't consent to go. That's not a small thing."

"No," Sera agreed. "It's not."

Something in the quality of the room shifted again. Not full resolution — Ashcroft was not the kind of man who reached resolution quickly, and the concerns he was channeling were not unreasonable even if his proposed explanations were wrong. But the confrontational edge softened slightly into something more like wary mutual assessment.

They spent three hours in Highwater Ridge, interviewing two void-touched weavers, speaking with the community leader about the incident documentation, and navigating three more brief encounters with Ashcroft's supporters who had individual questions and concerns that were, in several cases, quite reasonable.

On the walk back to the harbor, Tam said quietly: "He's going to be a problem."

"He's going to get worse before the situation becomes clear," Sera said. "The fragments are still moving. More weavers are going to be touched before we have any ability to stop it. And each new incident is going to feed his argument."

"Yes." Tam walked with his characteristic deliberate pace, managing the uneven harbor road. "What he said about Kerra, that she said no one should touch all five tides — he's wrong about that. She never said that. But he's representing a real anxiety that existed in her time and has never been fully resolved. People accepted resonants, but they never fully stopped wondering whether the acceptance was wise."

"And now something is using resonants as its preferred navigation system," Sera said.

Tam looked at her. "You are not responsible for the fragments' preference," he said, in the tone he used when he was correcting a conflation he had noticed her making internally even though she had not said it aloud.

"I know."

"Knowing it and not carrying it are different things."

She thought about this for the remainder of the walk, and through the evening meal at the island's single inn, and through the night, listening to the wind off the water and the sound of boats in the harbor. She thought about Ashcroft's supporters, their anxiety genuine and their interpretation wrong, and the very particular difficulty of the situation in which the people best positioned to solve a problem were also the people whose existence contributed to the fear surrounding it.

Kerra Valmont had navigated this her whole life — being a resonant in a world that found resonants alarming, trying to demonstrate through action and restraint and careful institution-building that the fear was misplaced. She had done it over decades, with patience that Sera found, trying to imagine it, genuinely awe-inspiring.

Sera did not have decades.

She thought about the fragments' mapping behavior — the triangulation the Greenstay patriarch had described, the orientation that Brinestep had felt — and what it implied about where the fragments were building toward. She had a hypothesis, still unverified, still needing the kind of evidence that only deeper investigation could provide. But the hypothesis fit: the void-touched episodes were happening most densely in the outer islands, in communities that had been through difficult seasons, in weavers whose emotional states had been amplified by sustained hardship. And the outer archipelago, at its easternmost extent, held the oldest geology in Vaetheris — stone so old it predated the current understanding of how the tide-veins had formed. Old stone. Old veins. Old places where the boundary between ordinary void-tide and the deeper void was thinner than it was elsewhere.

The Hollow King had not arisen in Deepwater Crossing or in the prosperous central islands. He had arisen at the edge of things, where the world's deep structure was closest to the surface.

She would need to go deeper into the void to confirm this.

She would need to go deeper than she had gone, carefully and with Tam present, and look for the architecture of the gathering that she suspected was already taking shape.

She lay awake listening to the harbor and thought about the long arc of three hundred years, and the woman who had done what she could and then sealed away the rest for whoever came after her, and what it meant to be whoever came after.

Then she thought about what Brinestep had said — that it felt like something old and very tired trying to go home — and what it would mean to stand in the path of that, and what it would mean to choose to.

She did not arrive at any conclusions. She fell asleep eventually, and her dreams were quiet, which was its own kind of answer.
