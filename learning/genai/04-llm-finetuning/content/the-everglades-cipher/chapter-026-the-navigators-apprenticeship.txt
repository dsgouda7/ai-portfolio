Chapter 26: The Navigator's Apprenticeship (Diego de Almeida, Porto, 1289-1303)

The Tagus in winter was a different river than the Tagus in summer.

Diego de Almeida had been told this by his uncle, Fernão de Almeida, who had been a pilot on the Lisbon-Cádiz run for thirty years and who knew the river the way pilots know rivers: as a continuous practical problem whose solution changes with the season, the tide, the wind, and the state of the bar at the river mouth. Fernão had also told Diego that a man who could read one river could learn any river, and that a man who could not read the river in front of him could not be trusted in any water.

Diego had been twelve years old when Fernão told him this. He had been standing on the Ribeira do Porto, the riverside district of Porto, watching a carrack work its way upriver to the Customs House quay with the particular slow difficulty of a large vessel in shoal water, making short tacks in a narrow channel with a current running against it. The river was grey-green and opaque in November, and the carrack's crew was doing things with the rigging that Diego could not yet name and was watching very carefully in order to learn.

He had learned quickly. This was the first thing Fernão had told him about himself, with the specific directness of an uncle who had no particular investment in flattery: Diego learned quickly, which was not the same as being clever, which was not the same as being wise, and the order of those three things mattered.

Diego understood, even at twelve, that this was a graduated compliment.

He began his formal navigation apprenticeship with Fernão at thirteen, aboard the Santo António, a medium-sized carrack that carried salt fish south to Cádiz and cloth north to Porto from Lisbon, with occasional longer voyages to the Atlantic islands. The work was hard in the physical sense and not difficult in any other sense: he learned to read the water by reading the water, which meant looking at it continuously and correlating what he saw with what he subsequently observed the vessel doing. He learned the compass by sailing by it in conditions where he could verify his position by known landmarks, and then in conditions where he could not. He learned the portolan chart by working from it on a board in the chart room while Fernão asked him questions about the coastline ahead.

He was good at it. He knew he was good at it without that knowledge becoming complacency, because Fernão was very good at it and Fernão's standard was the standard Diego measured himself by, and by that standard he was still a student.

He sailed with Fernão for six years, until Fernão's death in 1295 from a fever contracted on the Cádiz run. After Fernão's death, Diego sailed as second pilot and then as pilot on vessels he found through the Porto guild network, working the Atlantic coast from Galicia to Morocco, learning the shoals and currents and seasonal wind patterns of each stretch of coast with the systematic completeness that Fernão had taught him was the only adequate approach.

He was engaged in this work when the Templar contact found him.

---

The Templars had a preceptory in Porto, as they had preceptories in every city of consequence in Portugal. The Porto preceptory was primarily administrative — it managed properties and collected revenues from the surrounding district — but it maintained a small permanent staff of permanent personnel, including a navigator on retainer for the occasional maritime operations that the Order required.

The navigator on retainer in 1299 was a Genoese named Marco Cigala, who died in a fall from a quay in Viana do Castelo in October of that year under circumstances that were officially accidental and privately considered otherwise by the Order's internal investigators, who had their reasons for skepticism.

The preceptory needed a replacement. Brother Afonso, the Porto preceptor, had been told by his contacts in the guild network that there was a navigator named Diego de Almeida who was methodical, discreet, and known to be reliable in difficult conditions. Brother Afonso knew what discreet meant in the guild network's vocabulary: someone who could be trusted not to discuss the work he was paid to do. This was the most important qualification.

The meeting was at the preceptory's administrative offices, which occupied the ground floor of a substantial house near the cathedral. Brother Afonso offered him a retainer and a stipend, explained that the work would be irregular and would require availability on short notice, and declined to explain in advance what specific operations the work would involve. Diego accepted.

He was twenty-three years old. He had never been a man who required a complete explanation before beginning work. What he required was a clear problem and adequate tools.

---

The first work the Order gave him was straightforward: a voyage from Porto to La Rochelle with a cargo of documents, returning with a cargo of persons — three senior Templar officers who needed to travel from the French Atlantic coast to the Portuguese Atlantic coast without being observed. The documents were in iron-bound boxes. The officers were quiet men who asked no questions and who were, from the navigator's perspective, ideal passengers: they did not interfere with the ship's management and did not require entertainment.

The La Rochelle run became regular. Diego made it six times in three years, always with sealed cargo in one direction and passengers in the other, and never with a complete understanding of what the cargo contained or why the passengers needed to travel without record. This did not trouble him. He understood that the Order operated in a world of institutional politics that required, occasionally, the movement of things and persons at the margin of official knowledge. He was not being asked to do anything he considered wrong. He was being asked to navigate, which was what he did.

In 1302, the nature of the cargo changed.

The boxes that came aboard at La Rochelle on the October voyage were different from the previous cargo: heavier, more numerous, more carefully secured. The officers who supervised their loading were more senior than any he had worked with before, and they were tense in a way that was different from the ordinary tension of a significant operation. They were careful in the way that people are careful when they are not certain that carefulness will be sufficient.

Diego watched this and said nothing. He sailed the vessel south to Porto. The boxes were unloaded and moved to the preceptory's storehouse under heavy guard.

Three weeks later, Brother Afonso told him that there would be a new mission. It was different in character from anything he had done before, and Afonso could not describe it fully because the full description would require security clearances that Diego did not yet have. What Afonso could tell him was that it would require extended Atlantic sailing — not the coastal run but the deep Atlantic — and that Diego's answer would determine whether he received the clearances and the full description.

Diego said yes.

---

The clearances took two months, which was how long it took for the senior Templar officers in Lisbon to make their assessment of him and for their assessment to be transmitted up the chain of command to whoever made this category of decision. During those two months, Diego was not told what he was waiting for, and he did not ask.

He used the time to study charts.

The preceptory's chart room contained the best collection of portolan charts he had seen outside the official pilots' archive in Lisbon. There were Atlantic island charts that he had not previously known existed, and a set of charts covering the far Atlantic whose origin he could not determine but whose draftsmanship was extraordinary — finer and more accurate than anything in the guild's collection, with annotations in a hand he recognized as Arabic or perhaps Persian, describing currents and wind patterns in the open Atlantic west of the Canaries.

He spent the two months reading these charts.

When the clearances came, and with them the full description of the mission, he understood what he had been reading. The wind patterns described in the Atlantic charts were the wind patterns he would need. The currents were the currents he would follow. The route was already in the charts, if you knew how to read them.

He spent another month with the charts before he was satisfied that he knew what he was doing.

Then he went to Afonso and said: he was ready.

---

The full description of the mission took three hours to explain.

The Templar Order was in crisis. The French king was hostile and his hostility was moving toward action. The Order's senior leadership in Paris had concluded, through intelligence sources that Diego was not identified to but that were clearly extremely good, that action was imminent — not in months but in weeks or days. The crisis had been anticipated for years; the preparation for it had been underway for two years. Diego was the operational element of that preparation for the Portuguese and Iberian branch of the operation.

The materials to be moved were the Order's most sensitive archive: the financial records of the European intelligence network, the correspondence with European courts, the operational records of the Order's intelligence relationships, and the personal documents of the most senior officers — the materials whose possession by the French crown would, in the wrong hands, be dangerous to living persons and to still-operating networks.

These materials had been gathered at the Porto preceptory over the preceding six months, arriving in the irregular shipments Diego had been carrying south from La Rochelle. They were now in the Porto storehouse: approximately two hundred iron-bound boxes, varying in size from a man's armload to something requiring two men to carry.

The mission was to take these boxes to a location entirely beyond the reach of any European authority, seal them in a form designed for long-term preservation, and encode the location in a document that could be recovered by a future reader without institutional support.

The location had been selected by Brother Afonso and the senior Templar officers who had designed the mission. It was an island in the Western Ocean, far beyond the Atlantic islands, requiring a voyage of a type that had not been undertaken by any European institution on record.

Diego looked at the chart Afonso spread on the table.

He looked at it for a long time.

Then he said: he could do it. He had been reading the wind charts for two months. He knew the route. He needed a specific type of vessel, a specific crew, and a specific preparation time.

Afonso said: how long.

Diego said: four months, to prepare properly. Less, if necessary.

Afonso said: two months.

Diego said: three.

They agreed on three.

---

The preparation occupied every waking hour of the following three months.

He needed a vessel that could carry a substantial cargo on a deep ocean passage of unknown duration, in conditions that the Atlantic charts described but that no one he knew had personally experienced. The vessel needed to be seaworthy, provisioned for a minimum of sixty days beyond its Atlantic island staging point, and manned by a crew that was competent enough to be useful and discreet enough to be trusted.

The vessel was a medium nau of about 150 tons burden, acquired from a Lisbon merchant who had no particular attachment to it and needed money, officially sold to a trading company that was a Templar front. Diego spent six weeks going over every plank, fitting, and rope with the master shipwright he trusted. The vessel was renamed Nossa Senhora da Paciência. Our Lady of Patience.

The crew was eight experienced men: three sailors from the guild network whom Diego knew personally, a ship's carpenter whose discretion was guaranteed by the nature of the guild's internal knowledge economy, and four Templar personnel who were not primarily sailors but were physically capable and trained in basic seamanship. Diego was the pilot and master. His second was a young Portuguese sailor named Gaspar Rodrigues, who was twenty-one, quick-minded, and had already shown the specific quality Diego valued most: the ability to be wrong, notice it, and correct without requiring that the error be explained to him.

He told the crew what he told them in categories determined by what they needed to know to do their work. The sailors knew they were going on a deep Atlantic voyage with a sensitive cargo. They did not know the destination or the nature of the cargo. They would know the destination when they needed to navigate to it. They would not know the nature of the cargo, which would be sealed in the hold for the duration.

He told Gaspar more than the others, because Gaspar was going to be the second pilot if anything happened to Diego, and the second pilot needed to be able to complete the navigation without him.

He told Gaspar the destination and the route. He showed Gaspar the wind charts and the current descriptions. He walked Gaspar through the navigation plan, day by day, for the estimated sixty-day crossing. He made Gaspar repeat the plan back to him three times until Gaspar had it without reference to the charts.

He told Gaspar nothing about the cargo.

Gaspar did not ask.

This was the quality Diego valued in him.

---

They departed Porto on a Tuesday morning in early November 1307.

Diego stood at the helm and watched the city recede behind him. The Douro's mouth was choppy with the fresh northwesterly that was filling their sails and pushing them south toward the Canaries. The Nossa Senhora da Paciência handled well: steady, not particularly quick, but the right vessel for the purpose.

He would not see Porto again for eight months.

He thought about Fernão, who had taught him to read the river. He thought about the Tagus in winter, grey-green and opaque, and the carrack making its slow way upriver with its crew doing things with the rigging. He thought about being twelve years old and watching and learning.

He was twenty-eight now. He had a vessel under him and a chart in his head and a destination that no Portuguese navigator had previously been to and returned from. He was not certain that he would succeed. He was certain that he had done everything that could be done to prepare, and that certainty was what he had.

He looked south.

He sailed.

---

The Canary Islands took eleven days to reach, running south before the northwesterly until it gave way to the northeasters that prevailed in the lower latitudes. The islands were familiar: he had been here twice before, on the longer Atlantic voyages from Porto, and the anchorage at La Palma was known to him.

He spent three days at La Palma, taking on water and provisions for the crossing. The local pilot who met them at the anchorage gave him the standard information about conditions to the west: the trades were blowing steady, northeast at about fifteen knots, which was what the charts had predicted and what he had planned for.

He asked, carefully, whether the pilot had knowledge of the route to the far western islands — the islands described in certain charts as lying beyond the horizon of the normal Atlantic passage. He phrased the question as a scholarly inquiry rather than a navigational one, on the theory that a pilot who was not carrying a specific cargo would have more reason to speak freely.

The local pilot looked at him for a moment. Then he said: there were stories. Sailors who had run before the trades for too long and found land where the charts showed only ocean. These were sailors who had returned, which was the important qualification. The ones who had not returned had also, by definition, not reported.

He said: of the ones who returned, what did they find?

The pilot said: low green islands. Warm water. Birds that were different from the Atlantic birds. Current that ran west and then north. The ones who had gone further said the land was continuous and large, but none of the ones who had gone that far and returned had gone very far.

Diego thanked him. He paid for the provisions. He spent that evening with the charts, checking his calculations one final time.

In the morning, they left La Palma and turned west.

The trades took them immediately: a steady northeast wind at exactly the speed the charts described, and the vessel running before it with the ease of a vessel being given exactly what it needed.

Diego stood at the helm and felt the trades on his face and thought: the charts were right.

He had been hoping the charts were right. Knowing and hoping are different things.

He knew now.

He sailed west.
