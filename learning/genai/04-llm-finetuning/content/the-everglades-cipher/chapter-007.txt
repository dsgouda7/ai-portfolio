Chapter 7: The Senator's Handprint

Senator Buck Langford had what his campaign literature called a "big Florida handshake" — two-handed, firm, lasting one beat longer than socially necessary, accompanied by direct eye contact and the name of the person being shaken, used twice in quick succession, as in: "Jim! Jim, great to have you." Nobody in Florida politics had used this technique for thirty years without being trained into it, and everyone knew this, and everyone found it effective anyway, which said something about the persistent triumph of form over content in political culture.

Jake Malone found it irritating.

He was not in the senator's presence. He was watching a recording of the senator's last public appearance, a ribbon-cutting for a new marina development on the Miami River, which he had pulled from the Channel Seven News archives through his contact at the station, a producer named Felicia who owed him a favor from a missing teenager case in 2021. The ribbon-cutting had taken place five weeks ago. In the recording, Senator Buck Langford stood between two men Jake recognized from property records as officers of a company called Calypso Bay Development, LLC.

Calypso Bay Development was a Florida corporation, incorporated in 2019, with registered offices in Tallahassee and an operating address in Brickell. Jake had spent the previous evening running searches and had developed a modest dossier:

Calypso Bay Development, LLC had made six major land acquisitions in South Florida and the Florida Keys since 2020, totaling approximately $280 million in real estate. Of those acquisitions, four had received state environmental permits on an accelerated timeline compared to similar applications. Two of the permits had been for areas previously designated as sensitive coastal habitat.

The company's two principal officers were Charles Dupree and Melinda Taft, neither of whom Jake could find any background on prior to 2018. They appeared, in effect, to have been created in 2018 — no prior business filings, no professional history, no social media presence older than 2018. This was the signature of people either using legal name changes or, more commonly, company-level cutouts — names attached to a structure rather than real individuals who might be traced.

The company's financing was interesting. The acquisitions had been funded through a combination of bank loans (from three different Florida-chartered banks, all of which received state-backed deposits) and a private equity partner called Antigua Capital Partners, registered in Antigua and Barbuda. Antigua Capital Partners had a single Miami-based agent, a law firm called Horowitz and Penn that appeared in three of the four permits' documentation.

Horowitz and Penn had Buck Langford's former chief of staff as a partner.

Jake had built this over three hours with public databases and a property records subscription and his own knowledge of how Florida development money moved. It was not evidence of anything criminal. It was the shape of something criminal — the outline you could see if you knew where to look.

He called Carla Hidalgo.

"Calypso Bay Development," he said.

A pause. "Where are you going with that?"

"You know the name."

A longer pause. "It's been on the periphery of two separate FBI field office investigations in the past four years. Nothing I'm involved in. They're clean on paper."

"The Santiesteban family's logistics interests and Calypso Bay's real estate interests overlap how?"

She was quiet long enough that he knew she was deciding how much to share. "There's a theory — not mine, the FBI's — that Caribe Logistics is used to move money from Santiesteban's less legitimate interests through the development projects. The real estate absorbs the capital, the development creates value, and the returns are clean. Calypso Bay may or may not be the vehicle."

"And Langford's involvement."

"Langford's involvement is in the permits. He sits on two key committees. He has been consistently helpful to development interests in sensitive coastal areas. Whether that's corruption or policy is—" She paused. "—the question."

"The island," Jake said. "Cayo Espiritu. If the Santiesteban family found the archive there and wanted to monetize it, they'd need to develop the island. Build infrastructure. And they'd need permits for that."

A long silence.

"That's a speculative chain," Carla said finally.

"Yes. But it's a complete chain." Jake looked at his notes. "They can't access the archive without Arturo's help with the final cipher. If they get that help, the next step is mounting an expedition to the island. An expedition of that scale — boats, divers, possibly construction — needs logistics, which is Caribe. And if they decide to capitalize on the location itself — tourism, resort, whatever — they'd need Langford's committee for the permits." He tapped the desk. "The family has been waiting for this for sixty years. When they finally have the complete information, they'll have everything in place to act on it. They've been building the infrastructure for the endgame."

"Malone," Carla said carefully. "If this is right — all of this — then what the Santiesteban family has built is not a crime of opportunity. It's a sixty-year enterprise. Which means it's very deeply embedded in this city's business and political structure."

"I know."

"Which means moving against it—"

"I know."

"You'll be very careful."

"I'll be careful," Jake said, which was not the same as very careful and they both knew it.

He ended the call and thought about the senator.

Buck Langford had been in the Florida state senate for eighteen years. Before that, three terms in the state house. His father had been a developer. His son was a developer. His daughter worked for the law firm that handled the Santiesteban logistics interests' compliance filings. He had the deep, comfortable alignment of a man who had never understood that conflict of interest was a thing.

Jake's problem with the senator was not evidence. The problem was access. Langford was not the kind of person you got to through a cold approach, and Jake did not have the standing or the cover to approach him indirectly. He needed a better angle.

He thought about this for twenty minutes.

Then he called Elena.

"Your father's research," he said. "The draft paper — the one with Fairweather. Does it mention Calypso Bay Development? Or any specific company or individual who had tried to acquire the island before?"

"I've been reading it," she said. "Hold on." He heard pages. "There's a footnote — wait. In the second section, the historical context section, there's a reference to a '1939 expedition to the Windward Passage region by an unspecified private party.' And a note that says: 'documentary records of this expedition are held in the private archives of the Santiesteban family, Miami, and were made available to the researcher through a third-party intermediary.' The footnote cites a personal communication, 2021."

Jake stared at the ceiling. "The Santiestebans showed Arturo their grandfather's expedition records."

"Apparently. Or someone connected to them did."

"Why would they do that?"

"Maybe—" She paused, thinking. "Maybe they thought sharing what they had would get them access to what he had. A collaboration. They give him the historical expedition records, he gives them the translation."

"Except he didn't give them the translation."

"He didn't give them the complete translation," she corrected. "He may have given them enough to establish the relationship and then—" She stopped. "And then changed his mind."

"Or understood what they intended to do with it," Jake said. "He wasn't interested in a resort development on a historically significant site. He was interested in the archive itself."

"He cared about the documents," she said. "Not the — whatever other value the location might have. Yes."

Jake looked out the window. The Brickell towers in the afternoon sun looked like someone had been playing with the light settings. "The intermediary," he said. "Who arranged the introduction between Arturo and whoever in the Santiesteban organization shared the expedition records?"

"The footnote doesn't say. Just 'personal communication, 2021.'"

"I need to find who that was."

He thought about it for a moment. An introduction between a bookseller and an organized crime family, arranged in 2021. The kind of introduction that gets made in certain specific Miami circles — business, culture, philanthropy. The overlap between those worlds and the world of Caribbean antiquities research.

He thought about who he knew who moved in those circles.

Then he thought of a name he hadn't expected.

He called a number he hadn't called in eight months.

It rang four times. Then:

"Captain Ignacio Reyes." The voice was weathered and warm, the specific warmth of a man accustomed to putting passengers at ease on rough water.

"Nacho," Jake said. "It's Jake Malone."

A pause, then a laugh. "Miami Jake! What are you doing calling the Keys at this hour? Something's wrong."

"I need to come down to see you. Tonight if possible."

"Something is definitely wrong." The warmth was still there but something more serious had come up beneath it. "Does this have anything to do with the Windward Passage?"

Jake sat very still. "Why would you ask that?"

A long pause from the Keys end. The sound of water and maybe wind, the sounds you get when someone is standing on a dock.

"Because I was going to call you next week," Nacho Reyes said slowly. "I've been hearing things. About a bookshop in Little Havana. About someone who was close to something. And then the bookshop owner disappeared, and I've been sitting here on my boat for a week wondering who to call."

Jake said: "I'll drive down tonight."

---

He left Miami at four-thirty, after stopping at the houseboat for a change of clothes and the briefcase from the locker, and drove south on US-1 through the specific geography of the Florida Keys, which begins in earnest at the Card Sound Bridge and which is unlike any other landscape in North America. It is landscape as argument: the argument that what the land is doing here is getting out of the way of the water, which has always been the more interesting element anyway. The mangroves crowd the edges and the sky comes very low over flat water and the bridges connect a chain of islands that seem, in the particular horizontal light of a late afternoon, to be barely holding on.

Jake had driven this road many times. He found it calming in a way that Miami was not and that New York had never been. The Keys had a deliberate quality to them — the speed limit dropped and so did the pulse — and the towns on the keys, as you moved south through Key Largo, Islamorada, Marathon, Big Pine, had the particular character of places where people came to simplify their lives and usually half-succeeded.

Key West was forty-five minutes further, but Nacho Reyes operated out of Big Pine Key, about halfway down, from a dock and a small boat repair yard he had owned since 1994. Jake pulled into the yard at seven-fifteen as the light was failing and the no-see-ums were beginning their evening work.

The yard was organized chaos: two boats up on stands in various states of repair, a fuel dock, a small office structure with a light on, and a long dock where three vessels were tied. The largest was a forty-two-foot dive charter boat, white hull, named La Determinada.

Ignacio Reyes was sitting on a dock piling with a beer in his hand, watching the last of the sunset.

He was fifty-nine years old and looked it in a way that was specifically Key West rather than specifically Miami — the weathering, the squint lines, the gray in the thick hair that had gone rogue in several directions and made no apology for it. He had the build of a man who did physical work outdoors: broad, well-used, the hands of someone who tied knots and pulled ropes and dove in water at various temperatures. He wore a faded blue shirt with the sleeves cut off and shorts that had seen years of salt water and was barefoot on the dock.

He was a man who looked absolutely at home in his location, which was either the product of a life well-chosen or a life that had compressed everything else out.

He saw Jake and got up from the piling with the ease of a man whose body still did what he asked of it.

"Miami Jake," he said. He extended a hand. The handshake was the real kind — firm, brief, not Buck Langford's political maneuver. "Come aboard. I'll get you a beer."

"I need to talk first," Jake said.

Nacho looked at him. Something shifted in his expression — the easy hospitality giving way to something older and more careful, the expression of a man whose guard had been relaxed for a while and was now coming back up.

"Come aboard," he said again. "We'll talk on the water."

They took the smaller of the dockside boats — a center-console twenty-four-footer — out into the Gulf side channel, where the light was nearly gone and the stars were just beginning, and where the flat dark water gave the sensation of being entirely removed from everything that happened on land.

Nacho cut the engine and let them drift.

"How do you know about the Windward Passage?" Jake said.

Nacho drank from his beer. He had given Jake one too, and Jake held it without drinking it.

"I've been looking for Cayo Espiritu for thirty-one years," Nacho said. Not boasting — stating. The way you state a fact you've made peace with.

Jake looked at him.

"My grandfather was on Ramon Santiesteban's 1941 expedition," Nacho said. "He was the dive lead. He was a free diver — this was before scuba, before any of it, he held his breath and went down. He was the one who wrote the note in the expedition report about the sound the water made." He looked at the dark water. "He told me about the sound when I was six years old. He described it very precisely. He said it was the most beautiful and disturbing thing he had ever heard in a lifetime on the water." Nacho paused. "He said: go back, Nacho. When you're old enough and you have the equipment and the time. Go back and listen to the saint's voice and see what the saint is keeping."

Jake was quiet.

"He died in 1979," Nacho continued. "I was twelve. I've been on the water since I was fourteen. I got my captain's license at nineteen. I've run dive charters for thirty years. And I've made, I think, six expeditions to the Windward Passage in that time. Looking." He turned the beer can in his hands. "I know the island. I know the cavern. I've heard the sound."

Jake sat forward. "You've been to the cavern."

"Three times. The first time was 1995. I was — optimistic then. I thought I'd go there with some scuba gear and come back with a treasure chest." He gave a short, self-deprecating laugh. "The cavern is accessible. The sound is real — exactly as my grandfather described. There are underwater chambers behind the main cavern, and they contain something. I can see it — or could, with the visibility on a clear day with good light. Shapes that are the wrong shape for rock. Long rectangular shapes. The kind of shape that is boxes or containers." He looked at Jake. "But the chambers are at twelve meters at the nearest point and deeper further back, and the current in and out of the cavern at high tide is dangerous. I've never been able to get in."

"You've never told the Santiesteban family this."

"The Santiesteban family doesn't know I exist," Nacho said. "My grandfather worked for Ramon Santiesteban but Ramon died in fifty-six and the connection to the family — my grandfather left the expedition work, came back to the Keys, fished. He kept the story to himself except for telling me." He looked at Jake. "I've told three people in thirty years. You're the fourth."

"The bookseller in Little Havana—"

"Arturo Vasquez-Cortez. I met him in 2019 at a conference — a maritime history conference in Miami. Someone introduced us because we were both interested in medieval navigation records. We talked for two hours. He told me he had a document. I told him I had a location. We decided to work together quietly."

Jake stared at him. "The 2021 personal communication in the research paper."

Nacho looked puzzled. "What paper?"

"Arturo's translation paper. There's a footnote about a third-party intermediary who shared expedition records."

Nacho went still. "That's not me. I've never had access to the Santiesteban family's expedition records. What I had was my grandfather's private notes — his own copy of what he wrote on the expedition, which he kept separately from whatever he submitted to Santiesteban." He frowned. "If Arturo got the family's records from a third-party intermediary, that's someone else entirely."

Jake thought about this. Someone with access to the Santiesteban family's private archives. Someone who shared those records with Arturo in 2021.

"Do you know a man named Alejandro Reyes-Castillo?" Jake asked.

Nacho's face changed slightly. "Of him. Not personally. He's Magdalena Santiesteban's man. Operations." He paused. "He's the one who visited the bookshop."

"He's the one who took Arturo," Jake said. Or gave the order. He didn't say this aloud. "I need everything you have. Your grandfather's notes, your expedition records, everything you've told no one for thirty years."

Nacho looked at him for a long time. The flat water around them was very dark and very still.

"What's your play?" Nacho said. "You find the bookshop owner. You recover the document. Then what? Who does the information go to?"

"It goes to being properly studied," Jake said. "Not to someone's development project or blackmail portfolio."

Nacho looked at the water. "That's a nice idea. This world doesn't usually accommodate nice ideas."

"No," Jake agreed. "It usually doesn't."

They sat there in the dark water for a while.

Then Nacho said: "I'll show you everything. On one condition."

"What's the condition?"

"When it's safe to go to the island, I take you there. In my boat. I've been waiting thirty-one years. I'm not sitting this out."

Jake thought about this for perhaps two seconds.

"Agreed," he said.

They went back to the dock and Nacho made them eggs and rice in the small apartment above the boat yard office, and Nacho showed Jake his grandfather's notes and his own expedition logs, which were thorough and systematic, the records of a man who had approached this the way he approached everything on the water: as a problem of navigation and patience.

At midnight Jake drove back north on US-1 with the stars overhead and the water on both sides, and thought about what he now had: a historian with the island's name. A treasure hunter with the island's location confirmed by experience. A missing man who knew how to decode the final cipher, currently in the hands of people who understood none of this and were trying to extract it by force.

He had the shape of the thing now. What he needed was Arturo.

He picked up his phone and called Carla Hidalgo. She picked up on the second ring, which told him she hadn't been asleep.

"I know where the island is," he said. "And I know someone who's been to it."

A long pause.

"That's not everything," she said.

"No," he agreed. "But it's enough to start pulling on."

He told her about Nacho. She listened without speaking. Then:

"I need to formally request FBI cooperation on the Santiesteban aspect of this. This is beyond my jurisdiction." A pause. "It was always beyond my jurisdiction but now I have justification to make the request."

"How long will that take?"

"Two, three days for the initial response. More for coordination."

"Arturo may not have two or three days."

A long silence. "I know that, Malone." Her voice was flat and controlled, the voice of a professional who has learned to manage the distance between what she can do and what the situation requires. "I'll expedite. In the meantime—"

"I'll find him," Jake said.

"I know you will," she said. "That's what worries me."

She hung up. Jake drove north in the dark, and thought about a man in a room somewhere in Miami, or maybe not in Miami anymore, who had a sound in his head — the voice of the saint — that a medieval navigator had heard seven hundred years ago and encoded in a document that had waited in a chest in Seville for four hundred and seventy-eight years for the right person to find it.

And thought about whether he was going to be in time.
