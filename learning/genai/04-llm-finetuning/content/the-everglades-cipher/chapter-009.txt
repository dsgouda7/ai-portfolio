Chapter 9: Tommy Okeechobee Reads the Land

There were places in South Florida that did not belong to the century they were in. The Everglades were the largest of these places, and the most insistent about it. Two million acres of slow water and sawgrass that moved on their own schedule, which was not human time and which was, if you were patient enough and honest enough, the time that mattered.

Thomasina Okeechobee — Tommy, to the people who knew her well enough to use the name — was seventy-one years old and had lived all but three of those years within thirty miles of the Tamiami Trail, in the Miccosukee territory along the northern edge of the Everglades. The three years she had not been there she had spent at the University of Florida, studying environmental science on a scholarship, and she had come back with a degree she rarely mentioned and knowledge she used constantly — not as credentials but as context, the way a person who understands the chemistry of water can hear more in a river than a person who sees only a river.

Jake had met Tommy two years ago on a case that had started with a missing teenager and ended in the Everglades, and in the course of that case he had acquired the specific respect for her that comes from watching someone read a landscape you can barely see. She had found a footprint in sedge that should not have contained a footprint and told him what it meant and been correct. She had told him which channel the boat had come from and been correct. She had told him the teenager was alive because the alligators in the southern section were moving away from a particular location and been, again, correct.

He had called her because of a detail in Magdalena Santiesteban's email that was wrong.

The Hialeah property where Arturo had been held — Alejandro's operation had given the address in the summary. It was in industrial Hialeah, close to the Miami-Opa-locka Airport. A light-industrial building, anonymous, hundreds of its kind in the area.

But in the photographs of the Hialeah property, taken by Alejandro's team, there was a detail that Jake's eye had snagged on and then passed over and then come back to: a vehicle in the background of one photograph, partially visible behind a corner of the building. It was a Florida Fish and Wildlife Commission truck. White, official, parked.

There was no reason for a FWC truck to be near a light-industrial building in the middle of Hialeah on a Tuesday.

Unless the truck was not connected to the building.

Unless it was connected to something else in the photograph's frame.

He had looked at the photograph with the zoom function of his phone at maximum and then with a magnifying glass borrowed from the dry cleaner downstairs from his office, and what he had seen — barely visible but visible — was that the FWC truck was parked not beside the building but on the other side of a fence line, in a dirt lot that was, by the vegetation at its margins, not Hialeah but something edging toward the canal country southwest of Hialeah.

And in the dirt lot, behind the truck, partially visible, was the edge of an airboat.

An airboat meant water access. Canal access, at minimum. Potentially Everglades access.

He had called Tommy at six in the morning.

---

She was waiting for him at the turnoff on the Tamiami Trail, leaning on her truck, drinking from a thermos, watching the early morning light do what it always did to the Everglades, which was to make everything absolutely clear and absolutely mysterious at the same time. She was a compact woman with gray-streaked black hair that she kept in a braid, wearing work pants and a long-sleeved shirt against the heat-before-the-heat that you feel in the Everglades in autumn, which is different from the direct sun heat but no less serious.

She looked at the photograph he showed her on his phone.

"Miccosukee territory runs up to that canal network from the south," she said. "That lot is right at the edge of where FWC runs their water patrol staging. But FWC doesn't run operations from Hialeah-side lots. They use the dedicated launch sites further west."

"Could someone use that lot to access the canal system without FWC knowing?"

"You could use it. It's not posted. There's no gate on the north end." She looked at the photograph again. "What are you looking for in the canals?"

He told her. Not everything — not the Codex and the island and the medieval history — but the relevant operational facts: a man taken from a Hialeah building four days ago by professional people using professional transport. A boat called the Carità at Dinner Key Marina. A gap between where Arturo was taken from and where Jake believed he was now being held.

Tommy handed him back his phone. She looked at the morning light on the sawgrass.

"There are three structures in the canal country between Hialeah and the trail that shouldn't be there," she said. "We've been reporting them for two years. Fish and Wildlife knows. The county knows. Nobody does anything because the access is complicated and the structures are old and nobody is sure who owns the underlying land."

"What kind of structures?"

"Old fish camps. From the fifties and sixties, when people could still build in the margins. Some of them had improvements — pilings, covered platforms, rough interior. Some of them have been used more recently than they were built." She looked at him. "One of them is on a lot that shows up in the county records as private land. The owner of record is a Florida LLC that was dissolved in 2015."

Jake looked at her. "You've already looked into this."

"I've been looking into the illegal structures for two years. The LLC — when I tracked it back, it had a registered agent that I couldn't trace further. But the agent address was a law firm on Brickell."

Jake thought about Webb & Associates on Brickell.

"If I describe the structure," he said, "can you take me to it without using the main access points?"

She looked at him with the expression of someone deciding how much to let herself be recruited.

"There's a condition," she said.

"Of course there is," Jake said.

"The structures — whatever's in them, whoever was using them — the land beneath them is Everglades margin. It should be restored. When this is over, whatever deal or settlement or conviction happens, the land restoration goes on the table." She looked at him steadily. "This isn't a negotiation. This is me telling you what my involvement costs."

"Done," Jake said.

"You're not authorized to promise that."

"I'll promise it as part of any agreement I help build. You have my word."

She looked at him for another moment.

"Get in the truck," she said. "I'll drive."

---

The Everglades, seen from the air, appears flat and uniform, an undifferentiated expanse of green extending to the horizon in every direction. Seen from the water — from a flat-bottomed boat, or wading, or from the low vantage of someone moving through the sawgrass — it is nothing like this. It is an ocean of distinct microenvironments, each operating by its own rules. A stand of cypress. A hammock — a slight elevation, inches above the surrounding water table, enough to support an entirely different ecology of trees, ferns, orchids, things that need to be above the flood. A slough, a slow river with no visible banks, moving south at a walking pace with the specific gravity of water that has far to go.

Tommy moved through this landscape with the ease of someone who has never been lost in it. She knew the canals by name — not the official FWC designations but the Miccosukee names, some of which had no exact English translation and described qualities of water or light or season that English hadn't needed a word for. She moved the flat-bottomed skiff she'd launched from a site three miles from the staging lot, poling rather than motoring, which was slower and quieter.

Jake sat in the bow and watched the landscape and thought about Diego de Almeida moving through the Atlantic in a caravel in 1307, navigating by the stars and by knowledge he kept in his head, carrying something worth more than his life in the hollow of a vial around his neck.

After forty minutes on the water, Tommy stopped poling.

"There," she said.

Jake looked. At first he saw only the usual architecture of the canal margin: mangroves tight to the waterline, sawgrass behind, a cypress stand further back. Then he saw the structure.

It was built on pilings, as old structures in the Everglades were, with the main platform about four feet above the water at current level. Screened walls, now mostly intact though clearly old. A corrugated metal roof. A dock extension on the water side with two cleats, both newer than the structure they were attached to.

And tied to the near cleat: a small aluminum skiff, the kind used for shallow-water navigation.

Someone had been here recently. The skiff's deck was wet and the tie-off lines were clean.

Jake looked at Tommy.

"I need to go in," he said quietly.

"There might be people."

"There might be. Stay here."

"Jake." She said it the way you say a person's name when you want them to hold still. "There's a second dock. Around the south side. I've seen it before. If there's a second boat — if there's someone watching the water side and you come in from the north—"

Jake understood. He nodded.

He stepped off the bow into the water. It was warm and knee-deep and smelled of the specific organic richness that is both compost and life simultaneously. He moved along the structure's north side, keeping below the dock line. The sawgrass at the margin cut his arms and he ignored it.

He went around the south corner.

There was a second dock. And on the second dock, sitting in a plastic chair with his hands resting on his knees and his face turned slightly away, was a man.

He was looking toward the canal's south end. He was a large man in dark clothing. He had the posture of someone on watch — not relaxed, but not actively alert either. The specific posture of someone who has been doing this for many hours and is in the phase where vigilance and fatigue have reached an equilibrium.

He had not heard Jake because Jake had learned, in twelve years as a detective and before that in too many situations he preferred not to detail, how to move on water without noise.

Jake was not interested in the man on the dock.

He was interested in the door behind him.

The door to the structure's interior was a plywood panel on hinges that had been replaced recently — the hinges were new hardware in an old frame. He waited until the man on the dock was looking left, toward the canal, and then he went up the south side pilings with the care of a man who does not weigh as much as he did at thirty-five, and pushed the door open.

The interior was dim and smelled of old wood and recent human habitation. A cot with a sleeping bag. A small table with a camp stove and food. An LED lantern, currently off. Two bags, the kind that suggest transit rather than camping.

And on the cot, awake, his eyes adjusting to the additional light that came with the open door:

Arturo Vasquez-Cortez.

He was wearing the same clothes he'd disappeared in, by the look of it — a guayabera, cotton pants, bare feet. His face had the specific gauntness of a person who has not eaten well for several days, and his glasses were missing, which seemed to be affecting his ability to locate Jake precisely. He squinted toward the doorway.

"Who's there?" he said, in Spanish.

"My name is Jake Malone," Jake said quietly in English. "Your daughter hired me. Can you walk?"

A very long pause.

"My shoes," Arturo said, with the tone of a man who has been waiting a long time to ask for a specific thing. "Do you have my shoes?"

Jake had his shoes in the bag from the Coral Gables station. He had brought the briefcase.

"Yes," he said.

Arturo sat up on the cot. "Then let's go," he said, with the dignity of a man who has been waiting a long time and is not going to waste the wait by being slow about leaving.

---

They moved quickly, which in the Everglades meant deliberately and with attention paid to the water. The man on the south dock had not heard them leave — Tommy's poling was that quiet, which was the skill of decades. Jake sat in the bow with Arturo in the middle, and Arturo's hands were trembling slightly, not from cold or fear but from the physiological residue of sustained high-alert stress being abruptly released.

He said nothing for five minutes. Then he looked at Jake.

"The people who took me from the other place," he said. "They were professional. Italian, I think — the two men spoke to each other in Italian. The woman spoke English with an accent I couldn't place. Eastern European, possibly."

"We know," Jake said.

"They didn't hurt me. They were — clinical. They kept me in the fish camp, which is deeply uncomfortable but not— They told me they needed my cooperation and that I would be returned safely to my family once I provided it." He paused. "I didn't provide it."

"You're remarkable," Jake said.

Arturo looked at him. "I'm a stubborn old man. There's a difference." He was quiet for a moment. "The woman — the one in charge — she knew about the Codex. She knew about Fairweather. She knew about the island's general location. What she didn't know — what she was trying to get from me — was the name of the island."

"Cayo Espiritu," Jake said.

Arturo looked at him sharply. "How do you know that?"

"Fairweather told me. She said you trusted her to tell whoever Elena trusted."

A pause. "Constance is thorough." He was quiet. "Is she safe?"

"She's at the Biltmore."

"Good." He looked at the passing landscape — the sawgrass, the cypress, the flat water that seemed to extend forever and that Tommy poled through with the familiarity of someone moving through their own living room. "Beautiful," he said quietly, looking at the Everglades. He said it with the specific wonder of a man who has been looking at plywood walls for seven days and is grateful for the return of beauty.

Jake looked at him. He thought about the letter. The careful preparations. The rum he was making last. "Your daughter is in Miami," he said.

Arturo's face changed. Not grief, exactly — the thing that moves through the face of a person who has been holding something back for a long time and suddenly doesn't need to.

"She knows?" he said.

"About the Codex. About the research. About most of it." Jake paused. "She also found the document in the drawer. Years ago. She didn't ask, but she knows."

Arturo was quiet for a long time.

"She's known about the adoption," he said slowly. "For years."

"Yes."

He nodded. The nod of a man putting down something heavy. "I should have—" He stopped. "No. I made the best decisions I could with the information I had. She was twelve. She was— she needed stability, not another mystery to carry." He looked at the water. "She deserved to know. I know that. I just kept finding reasons."

"You can tell her yourself," Jake said. "When we get back."

Arturo looked at him. Something in his face cleared, the specific clearing that happens when a person who has been uncertain about survival remembers that survival is still a possibility.

"Yes," he said. "Yes, I can."

Tommy poled them out of the canal system and into the access channel and toward the launch site, and when they reached land Jake called Elena first, then Carla Hidalgo, then — after a moment's consideration — Magdalena Santiesteban.

"I found him," he said to Magdalena. "He's safe."

A pause on her end. "The people who had him—"

"They don't have him anymore."

"Where is he?"

"Somewhere safe," Jake said. "We'll talk in a day or two. When he's had time."

"The island," she said.

"We'll talk," Jake said again.

He ended the call and looked at Arturo, who was sitting on the tailgate of Tommy's truck with his shoes on — his own shoes, from the briefcase from the bus station, which he had put on with the specific satisfaction of a man who had been thinking about a pair of shoes for seven days.

"How did you know where I was?" Arturo asked.

"A photograph your captors' opponents took," Jake said. "There was a FWC truck in the background. And a woman who knows the Everglades well enough to read the difference between where a truck should be and where it was."

Tommy was loading the skiff back onto the trailer with the efficiency of someone who has done it many times. She looked up at Arturo.

"The land under that fish camp," she said. "Everglades margin. What happens to it matters."

Arturo looked at her. He had the expression of someone recalibrating based on new information — a characteristic that Jake was already identifying as one of the man's most useful qualities.

"You're Miccosukee?" Arturo said.

"Yes."

"Your people have a relationship with this land that predates everyone else's claim to it."

"Yes," Tommy said simply.

He nodded slowly. "Whatever happens with the island and the archive — the land restoration of the sites used in this situation — that's a reasonable condition." He paused. "I'll make it part of any agreement."

Tommy looked at him for a moment. Then she looked at Jake.

"He's what you said," she said.

"He is," Jake agreed.

They drove out of the Everglades margin on the Tamiami Trail as the sun hit noon intensity and the light went flat and white over the sawgrass, and south of the road the wilderness went on doing what it had been doing for ten thousand years: holding still, moving slowly, waiting with the patience of something that has outlasted everything that ever tried to drain it.
