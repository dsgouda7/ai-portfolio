Chapter 19: The Woman Who Waited

Magdalena Santiesteban received Arturo Vasquez-Cortez in the garden of the Coral Gables compound on a Thursday morning in December, six weeks after the events in the Windward Passage.

The garden was formal in the Spanish colonial manner: a central fountain, four quadrants of trimmed hedge, bougainvillea on the walls in the specific violent red-orange that bougainvillea achieves in South Florida when it has found good soil and full sun. The fountain was old limestone, water-stained at the base, and made a sound that was older than the house around it — the sound of water moving over stone, which is one of the oldest sounds there is.

Magdalena was eighty-one years old and she was sitting in a straight-backed chair at the table nearest the fountain, because the fountain made it easier to hear the conversation and harder for other people to hear it from the perimeter. She had been doing this since she was twenty-three, when she had first started conducting meetings in this garden, and she had never once felt the need to explain why she chose the seat she chose.

Arturo came through the gate at nine precisely. He was followed by no one. He had agreed to come alone when she requested it, and he had done so, which she noted without comment.

He was smaller than she had expected. Photographs — even surveillance photographs, which she had — were always misleading in one direction or the other, and this man had been built up in her mind by sixty years of family narrative until he had acquired a scale that reality would inevitably shrink. He was compact, precise, with the bearing of a man who had spent decades at a desk doing fine work, and who had the calm of someone who had resolved something long unfinished.

She stood when he entered the garden. She was not required to do this. She did it because it was correct and because she respected him.

"Dr. Vasquez-Cortez," she said.

"Mrs. Santiesteban," he said. "Thank you for seeing me."

"Thank you for coming without your detective."

"He offered. I asked him not to come." He paused. "This conversation didn't need a detective in it."

She appreciated this. She gestured to the chair across the table. He sat. Her assistant brought coffee, which in this house meant proper Cuban coffee in demi-tasse cups, served on a tray with a small glass of water and a piece of pound cake, because hospitality was not a formality in this house, it was a practice.

Arturo tasted the coffee. He looked at her with the expression of someone who is reading a text carefully before speaking.

"Your grandfather's name was Ramon," he said.

"Ramon Francisco Santiesteban y Vidal," she said. "He was born in Havana in 1902. He came to Miami in 1938."

"He heard the sound."

"Yes."

"He understood what it was."

"He understood that it was significant." She paused. "He was not a scholar. He was a businessman and a sailor. He didn't know what the sound meant academically. He knew it meant something was there." She paused. "He also knew he couldn't get to it. The technology of 1941 didn't include what was needed."

"The resonance is produced by water moving through a passage that narrows before it opens into the inner chamber," Arturo said. "The acoustic effect requires specific tide conditions and a specific approach angle. It could be reproduced experimentally if you knew what you were looking for." He paused. "But from the outside of the cliff face, without foreknowledge, it would sound like a natural phenomenon."

"He described it as the island singing," she said. "That was the phrase he used. He said: the island was singing, and it was the most beautiful sound he had ever heard."

Arturo was quiet for a moment.

"Diego de Almeida described the same phenomenon in his Codex," he said. "He used the Latin word — cantus — and then crossed it out and substituted a Portuguese word, canto. Song." He paused. "Seven hundred years, two men from completely different eras and backgrounds, hearing the same thing."

"Music," she said.

"Music," he confirmed.

She drank her coffee. The fountain spoke its old syllable in the background. The bougainvillea moved very slightly in the morning breeze.

"What's in the archive?" she asked. "The full answer, not the press release."

"The press release is accurate but incomplete," he said. "The Philip letters are the most historically significant item and they'll receive most of the attention. But the archive is, in its totality, a record of an organization in the process of preserving its most important materials under extreme time pressure. There are financial records — the Templars' banking documents, which are valuable for understanding medieval finance. There are maps — navigational charts from the early fourteenth century, some of which predate anything currently in public collections. There are personal documents: letters, diaries, account books. The human texture of the organization." He paused. "And there are theological and philosophical documents that the Order considered important enough to protect at the same level as the Philip letters." He looked at her. "Some of those documents are going to be controversial. The Templar rule had a genuine mystical tradition that the Church's prosecution characterized as heresy. Whether it was heresy or heterodox practice within an acceptable range is a question that the Church is going to have to formally address."

She thought about Father Bellini.

"The Vatican knows what's in it?"

"Father Bellini has had preliminary access to Sofia Reyes's photographic survey. He's relayed that to his principals." He paused. "The Vatican's position, as represented by Bellini, is that the documents are a legitimate part of Catholic history that deserve scholarly study and context. They are not, officially, treating this as a threat."

"Officially."

"The unofficial position is more complicated. But Bellini is genuine. His interest in preservation over suppression is real." He paused. "The framework that was negotiated has teeth — it's not just a gentlemen's agreement. Multiple governments and academic institutions are parties to it. The archive is too widely known now for any single institution to control."

"Including yours."

"Including mine." He met her eyes. "I don't control it. No one does. Which is how it should be."

She studied him.

"My grandfather spent the last thirty years of his life believing he had found something important and not being able to make anyone understand it," she said. "My father spent twenty years pursuing the island by legal means — development, then conservation — because he thought the only way to protect something in this world was to own it. And I spent—" She paused. "I spent sixty years being the caretaker of a secret that my family had paid enormous costs to keep."

"I know," Arturo said.

"I'm not telling you this to make an accusation."

"I know," he said again. "You're telling me so I understand what the acknowledgment means."

She looked at him.

"Yes," she said.

He reached into the bag he had brought and placed a document on the table. It was printed, formal, on letterhead that she recognized as that of the University of Miami, Department of History. It was signed by six people.

"The historical context section of the formal discovery paper," he said. "The published version will appear next month. Section four is titled: Prior Discovery and Preservation of Knowledge of the Cayo Espiritu Site, 1941-2024." He waited while she read.

She read slowly. She read the paragraph about Ramon Francisco Santiesteban y Vidal, who in May 1941 had conducted an independent expedition to a site in the Windward Passage, had located the acoustic phenomenon that had been described in primary source navigation documents, had correctly assessed its significance as evidence of an enclosed archaeological deposit, and who had, for the subsequent thirty years of his life, preserved and transmitted knowledge of the site's location and significance to his family, enabling the eventual successful excavation.

She read the footnote that named her father and herself as the successive caretakers of that knowledge.

She put the document down.

She looked at the fountain.

"Ramon would have liked this," she said, finally.

"I thought he would," Arturo said.

"He was a vain man," she said. "In the good sense. He wanted to be remembered accurately." She paused. "He would have liked being in a footnote in a legitimate academic paper."

"He's in the text," Arturo said. "The footnote is your father and you."

She looked at the paper again.

"Sixty years," she said.

"Yes."

"Do you know what it costs to keep a secret for sixty years?"

"I kept one for thirty-four," he said. "I have some idea."

She looked at him. She felt, for the first time in this conversation, the specific adjustment that comes when you encounter someone who actually does have some idea, who has not produced a sympathetic formulation but the thing itself.

"It costs relationships," she said. "You can't explain why the island matters without explaining everything. And explaining everything makes people think you're—" She paused. "Unhinged. Obsessed. Living in a story."

"I had the same problem," Arturo said. "No one who didn't know the Codex could understand why it mattered. And I couldn't show the Codex to anyone who I wasn't certain of." He paused. "I lost three friendships in the second decade. People who thought I was wasting my life in a beautiful library."

"Were you?"

"No." He paused. "Though I would have been, if the Codex hadn't been real."

She picked up her coffee.

"The seat on the committee," she said. "I want to use it."

"That's what it's for."

"I want to ensure that the expedition and the archive's study include contributors from the Cuban academic community. Not as a political matter — as a historical one. The Atlantic and Caribbean networks that moved these documents included people from every part of the Spanish colonial world. Those histories are not secondary."

"I agree completely," Arturo said. "My colleague at the University of Havana is already part of the excavation team. He's been involved since the day after the discovery. His formal co-authorship is in the paper."

She nodded.

"There's one more thing," she said.

He waited.

"The island," she said. "Your document and the conservation framework treat it as a cultural heritage site. That's correct. That's right." She paused. "But I want something included in the heritage designation language that my father tried to get included in the development proposal thirty years ago and couldn't get. A specific reference to the ecological significance of the reef system on the eastern face." She paused. "The reef is not incidental to the site. It's what made the site survivable — the reef protected the cavern mouth from storm surge damage. Without the reef, the archive doesn't survive the centuries. The reef's preservation is part of the archive's preservation story." She paused. "I want that in the designation language."

Arturo looked at her with the expression of a man who is recognizing something unexpected and good.

"I'll make sure of it," he said.

"Your word is enough," she said.

"I'll make sure of it anyway," he said. "Because you're right and because it should be in the record."

She looked at him.

She had expected a scholar. She had been prepared for someone who was brilliant and narrow — someone who cared about documents and not about what surrounded them. She had been prepared for the specific blindness of the dedicated expert.

She had not been prepared for this: a man who had spent thirty-four years on one thing and had come out of it seeing more clearly, not less.

"Would you like more coffee?" she asked.

"Very much," he said.

She called for the assistant. They sat by the fountain in the December morning, which in Miami means brilliant sun and low humidity and the specific quality of light that the city produces in winter: clear, warm, without the summer's weight, like a city that has taken a breath.

They talked for another hour.

They talked about the archive and about Ramon and about the reef and about what it meant to spend a life in service of something that you could not be certain was real. About the courage that required. About the specific loneliness of that kind of faith.

They talked about their fathers, both of whom had kept things that mattered. About what it meant to receive that kind of inheritance.

They didn't talk about Alejandro Reyes-Castillo. There was nothing there to talk about that the courts weren't already handling. And she had made her own peace with that, in her own way, which was a private matter.

When he left, she walked him to the gate herself.

He shook her hand.

She held it for a moment longer than the formal duration.

She said: "Thank you for not giving up on it."

He looked at her. He said: "Thank you for keeping the knowledge alive."

She let go of his hand.

She watched him walk to the car.

She went back to the garden.

She sat by the fountain and let it speak in its old, uninterrupted voice, the voice of water over stone, the voice that had been speaking in this garden since before she was born, since before her father, since before her grandfather, who had come to this city from Havana in 1938 with his particular vision of what mattered and his stubborn insistence on being right about it.

She thought: he was right.

She thought: we were right.

She thought: it's enough.

She looked at the bougainvillea on the wall. She looked at the sky. She looked at the fountain. She sat very still.

Then she called for her assistant and asked for the schedule for the afternoon, because there was more to do, there was always more to do, and she had not spent sixty years being patient in order to stop working when the patience paid off.

She worked until seven.

She went to bed.

She slept better than she had slept in years.
