Chapter 2: La Luz de los Libros

He drove to Little Havana at seven in the morning in a 1998 Ford Bronco that had been several colors over its working life and had settled on the current rust-and-tan as a kind of existential compromise. Jake Malone had owned the Bronco for nine years. He had replaced the alternator twice, the transmission once, and the passenger seat entirely after a case three years ago that had ended with someone's blood on it, and he didn't keep the Bronco because it was practical or because it represented something about his character. He kept it because it was honest. It ran when it wanted to run, and when it didn't want to run it didn't pretend otherwise, and after a marriage and a career that had ended in mutual exhaustion and some spectacular dishonesty on all sides, he found the Bronco's limited emotional range something close to restful.

Calle Ocho in the early morning is a different street than it is at noon. At noon it is color, sound, music, the living theater of a neighborhood that has been performing its own character for sixty years and knows every line. At seven in the morning it is stone and shadow and the smell of café and the sound of Spanish spoken at the register of private conversation — the register used between people who know each other well enough not to perform.

La Luz de los Libros occupied the ground floor of a narrow two-story building between a botanica and a Cuban café, and it had the kind of physical presence that old bookshops accumulate the way good teachers accumulate authority — not through design but through accretion. The façade was hand-painted signage in navy and gold that someone with skill had done sometime in the eighties and touched up since, the gilt letters reading LA LUZ DE LOS LIBROS and beneath them, in smaller script: Libros Raros, Antiguos y de Ocasión. Rare, Antique and Second-Hand Books.

The metal security gate was pulled halfway across the door. Not locked — pulled. As if someone had pulled it and then been interrupted.

Jake stood outside for a moment and looked at this. The café next door — Café Sonia, according to the hand-lettered sign in the window — had its lights on. Through the glass he could see a woman of approximately sixty wiping the counter with the particular ferocity of someone who wipes counters to avoid thinking.

He went into the café.

The woman behind the counter had the build of someone who had spent decades moving heavy things without complaint and the expression of someone currently deciding whether another person was worthy of her time. She looked at Jake for four seconds with dark, evaluating eyes.

"You're the investigator," she said. Not a question.

"Elena called ahead?"

"She called late last night. Said you'd come in the morning. You want café?" She was already reaching for the espresso handle.

"Please," Jake said.

He settled onto the nearest stool. The café was small — six stools at the counter, three tables against the wall, photographs of Havana covering the wall behind the cash register in a dense mosaic that was itself a kind of archive. He looked at it while Sonia Rios made his coffee.

"I've been trying to remember everything I can," she said, placing the cup in front of him. It was a proper café cubano, dense and sweet. "Since he disappeared I keep going back through the last weeks. What I saw, what I heard."

"Tell me," Jake said.

She leaned on the counter. Her hands were strong and flour-dusted and she hadn't bothered to wash them before starting the coffee, which said something about her sense of priorities.

"Arturo has been my neighbor for twenty-four years," she said. "He came in here every morning. First customer. Eight o'clock, sometimes seven-fifty. He would have the same thing: café con leche and one croqueta. Ham. He would sit at the corner table and read for twenty minutes before he opened the shop. He read everything — novels, histories, sometimes old newspapers. He had this way of holding a newspaper, folded to one quarter, like a man on the subway. I always thought it was a Havana thing. My father held a newspaper the same way."

"In the weeks before he disappeared. Did anything change?"

"Everything changed," Sonia said, with the directness of someone who has been waiting to say this. "Three months ago, four maybe — around the time the heat broke, so September, October — he started coming in at different hours. Sometimes seven, sometimes nine-thirty, once he didn't come in until noon. His reading changed. Instead of novels it was always documents. Xeroxes, printouts, old photographs of manuscript pages. He spread them on the table and worked at them with a magnifying glass." She tapped the counter. "Arturo is not a man who rushes. He is methodical. Quiet. He was never rushed before. After September he had the energy of a man running out of time."

"The man who visited him. The large Cuban man with the scar."

Sonia's expression tightened. "Twice. First time was a Wednesday, about six weeks ago. He pulled up in a black SUV — those big Chevrolet ones, the Suburban — and parked right there in the no-parking zone like he was exempt from the law. He went straight into Arturo's shop without knocking. That bothered me. You knock when you go into a bookshop. There's a courtesy to it." She paused. "He was in there about an hour. When he left he got into the Suburban and drove away without looking left or right. Like a man who's used to space being cleared for him."

"The second visit?"

"Two weeks after the first. Same car, same no-parking. But this time there was a driver who stayed with the car. And this time, after about thirty minutes, I heard Arturo's voice." She said this with something that wasn't quite distress but was close to its border. "Arturo doesn't raise his voice. In twenty-four years I've heard him angry exactly once, when someone came into his shop and was rough with a book. He values quiet the way some people value money. So when I heard his voice through my wall — through a concrete wall, mind you — I went to the doorway of my shop."

"What did you hear?"

"Not the words. But the tone. He was refusing something. Very clearly. Repeatedly. The large man spoke less — lower, harder to hear — but it was the kind of voice that's used to being the last word in a conversation." She looked at her flour-dusted hands. "They were standing just inside the shop door. I could see through the window — the blind was up. The large man was close to Arturo, not touching but close. And Arturo had his arms at his sides and his chin up. The way my grandfather used to stand when the government told him something he wouldn't accept. Dignified but immovable."

"What happened?"

"The large man left. Arturo watched him go. Then he saw me in my doorway. He didn't say anything — he just gave me a small nod, the way you nod to someone after a doctor's appointment when you want to say 'I'm all right' without the whole complicated truth." Sonia picked up a cloth and began working the counter again. "A week after that he was gone."

Jake drank his coffee. "Did you see the large man clearly enough to describe him further? Height, weight?"

"Very tall. Over six feet. Maybe six-two, six-three. Heavy, but not fat — the weight of someone who was once very strong and still carries it. Gray suit both times. The scar under his left eye, like you said Elena told you. It was old — healed white. And he wore a ring on his right hand. A large signet ring, gold, with something on it. I couldn't see the detail from where I was."

"The Oxford professor," Jake said. "The woman who came from England. Did you see her?"

Sonia tilted her head. "Arturo mentioned her. Dr. Fairweather. He called her a 'necessary irritant,' which from Arturo is practically affection. He said she was brilliant but that she had an agenda he hadn't entirely worked out."

"Did she come to the shop?"

"Once. Three weeks ago, I think. An Uber dropped her off. Tall English woman, sixties, white hair, dressed like someone who takes the train rather than planes. She was in the shop for four hours. When she came out she walked straight to the corner and called someone on her phone. She looked upset — not crying, not dramatic, but focused upset. The kind of thing that happens when you've learned something that changes your plans."

Jake closed his notebook. "One more thing. The shop — Elena has the key. Is the security gate locked now?"

"No." Sonia looked toward the window. "I left it the way it was when I found it Saturday morning. Half-open, the way it was when Arturo—" She stopped. "I didn't touch it because it seemed like you shouldn't touch evidence. I know how it sounds, a half-open gate for a week."

"It sounds careful," Jake said. "Thank you."

He paid for the coffee — she tried to refuse, he left it anyway — and went next door to La Luz de los Libros.

---

The shop smelled of paper and leather and the deep basement note that is the particular perfume of old books, which is itself the smell of time breaking down into its components: wood pulp, ink, leather tannins, the slow oxidation of something that once contained information. Jake had spent a good portion of his career in rooms that smelled of this, not because he had any special relationship with literature but because the old and the hidden tend to accumulate in such places, and he had spent his career with the old and the hidden.

He stood in the doorway for a moment and looked.

The shop was densely organized in the manner of a mind that thinks in categories. There were labeled sections in hand-lettered Spanish: Historia, Ficción, Ciencias, Filosofía, Geografía, Lo Antiguo, and a back section simply labeled Lo Especial which was screened from the main room by a hanging curtain of dark blue velvet. Floor-to-ceiling shelves. A sliding ladder on brass rails, the kind that spoke of serious intentions toward the upper shelves. The main working area — the desk — was a partners' desk of old oak that faced the door and was covered in the controlled density of active scholarly work: open books, magnifying glass, a brass ruler, several photographs of manuscript pages, and a pair of reading glasses sitting beside a coffee cup that had grown a faint halo of rust-colored residue in the bottom.

The coffee cup was what bothered him. A man who cleaned his coffee maker and put it away before disappearing had not left a dirty coffee cup on his work desk. You clean one, you clean the other. The residue in the cup suggested coffee that was made, partially drunk, and abandoned — not set down deliberately, but set down in the middle of something that interrupted it.

Jake put on latex gloves he kept in his jacket pocket — an old habit from New York — and moved to the desk.

The manuscript photographs were on top. Twelve pages, photographed with a high-quality macro lens on a dark background that made the parchment glow amber. The writing was in multiple hands, or possibly one hand at different periods of aging. Jake could see what appeared to be Latin interspersed with a denser symbolic text — not letters, exactly, but a combination of geometric forms and what looked like modified Greek characters. To his left eye, which was the one he trusted, it looked very old and very deliberate.

He didn't pick them up. He photographed them in place with his phone.

Next to the photographs was a notebook — Arturo's own, a lined Moleskine of the kind that serious researchers use, with the elastic closure pulled back. Jake photographed the open page. It was dense with notes in a small, slanted hand, mixing Spanish and Latin, with occasional English words underlined. He could pick out several phrases:

...confirma la ruta del suroeste, pos. 1309-1312...

...Tomar → Marseille → Lisboa → Madeira → hacia el oeste...

...D. de A. = mensajero, no comandante — pero conocía el escondite...

...la isla de los espíritus no es ninguna de las islas conocidas en los registros colombinos...

...clave del cifrado: nombres de los meses en árabe árabe → correspondencia con símbolos...

Diego de Almeida: messenger, not commander. Knew the hiding place. The island of the spirits — not any island in the Columbus records.

Jake photographed every page of the Moleskine that was visible, then gently lifted the cover to see if there were pages beneath. There were none. The last entry in the notebook, on the right-hand page, ended mid-sentence:

Si Fairweather tiene razón sobre las coordenadas entonces el sitio es accesible, pero solamente desde—

If Fairweather is right about the coordinates, then the site is accessible, but only from—

Only from what. The sentence ended at the bottom margin, continued presumably on the next page, but the next pages were gone. Not blank — the notebook had been written in up to some undetermined point and then the pages were missing. Not torn. Cut. The margins were clean as paper bought new.

Jake looked at this for some time.

Someone had been here before him. Not necessarily before the police — the police may have seen it and documented it as irrelevant, not knowing what they were looking at. But someone with knowledge of what the notebook contained had removed pages.

He went behind the curtain of dark blue velvet into Lo Especial.

The back room was different from the front. Where the front shop was organized for customers and browsing, this room was organized for a single purpose. There was a table with a light box for examining documents. An adjustable lamp. A humidity and temperature gauge on the wall — it read 68°F and 45% relative humidity, the parameters of archival storage. A fireproof cabinet with a combination lock, standing open, and empty. Not hastily emptied — the inside was clean, no fingerprints on the bottom surface, no indentations in the felt lining that might suggest what had been removed. Whatever had been stored here had been removed carefully.

The Codex Almeida, Jake thought. The original manuscript.

Either Arturo had moved it, or someone else had.

He photographed the open safe. He photographed the climate gauges. He photographed the light box, on which a single sheet remained — a photocopy of what appeared to be a map, or part of one, hand-drawn in ink that had been photographed. The coastline was vaguely Caribbean, with islands marked in Spanish, but the names didn't match any contemporary geography Jake could place. One island had been circled in pencil, and next to the circle, in Arturo's handwriting, were the words: la isla de los espíritus.

He photographed this too.

Then he left the shop the way he'd found it — gate half-open — and sat in the Bronco on the street outside and called Elena Vasquez-Cortez.

"Have you been in the shop since your father disappeared?" he asked when she picked up.

"Once. The day I came to Miami, before I filed the police report. I was looking for any note, any message. Detective Hidalgo came the next morning with an officer and went through the place."

"The fireproof cabinet in the back room. Did the police note its contents?"

A pause. "I don't know what was in it. I don't think Detective Hidalgo opened it. She may not have found the back room — it's not obvious."

"I need you to call Detective Hidalgo and ask if they documented a metal fireproof cabinet with a combination lock in the back storeroom. Tell her it's significant."

"Why?"

"Because something that was in it is gone. Either your father moved it, which means he expected to be away, or someone else did, which means they had the combination." Jake started the Bronco. "One more thing. Your father's notebook — the Moleskine he kept on his desk. Are there missing pages in it?"

Another pause. "I don't know. I saw the notebook but I didn't look closely."

"When you talk to Detective Hidalgo, ask her about that too." He pulled out into Calle Ocho traffic. "I'm going to find Roberto Sanchez."

"The botanica man? Why?"

"Because he knows everyone in this neighborhood and this case has started to involve people who like to think they're invisible," Jake said. "In Little Havana, the invisible people all have to buy something from someone eventually."

HISTORICAL INTERLUDE — The Atlantic, 1308

They had been at sea for forty-one days when the water changed color.

It happened gradually, over the course of an afternoon — the Mediterranean blue that Diego had grown up with, the cold Atlantic gray they had crossed for five weeks — giving way to something that was neither of those things. A green that was too vivid, too present, almost opaque. Fernando Pires, the captain, came to stand beside Diego at the prow and looked at the water for a long time without speaking.

"What is it?" Diego asked.

"Sargasso," Pires said. He said it the way a man says a word he has heard many times but used rarely. "Weeds from the sea bottom. Some say it means you are near land. Some say it means the opposite." He watched the dark brown tendrils of weed drift past the hull. "Some say it means you are where you are supposed to be."

They were fifteen aboard: four knights, the remnants of a party that had been eight when they left Marseille, the other four having been landed in Lisbon with what Pires called the secondary cargo, which Diego had not been told about and did not ask about. Then three sergeant-brothers including Diego. Then Pires and seven sailors. And there was the thing in the hold.

Diego did not know precisely what was in the hold. He knew there were two locked chests, oak-bound with iron, that four men had carried aboard in Marseille in the dark before dawn. He knew that Brother Guillaume, the senior knight, checked the lock on the hold door every evening before vespers and every morning before prime, and that the ritual of this checking had something devoted in it, something that was prayer as much as security. He knew that on the twenty-third day at sea, when they hit a gale that lasted two days and the ship moved like a dying animal in the seas, Brother Guillaume went below and did not come back up for three hours, and when he did come back his face was the face of a man who has kept a thing safe and does not yet know if the keeping was worth the cost.

None of this was any of Diego's business.

His business was the vellum with the fourteen symbols, which he had memorized during the walk to Marseille and thereafter carried in his boot more as a habit than a precaution. His business was the information the commander at Tomar had given him orally, which he carried entirely in his head: a location, given in measurements of arc from the sun's position, that he had been told to memorize and never write down.

His business was getting the information to the right place.

Two weeks after the Sargasso sea, when the air had gone warm and thick with vegetation smell and the birds had changed from gray northern ones to something brilliant and unfamiliar, they saw land.

It was an island. Small, tree-covered to the waterline on all sides except a single beach of white sand where the jungle gave way in a curved arm. The water around it was the specific shade of blue that indicates a hard bottom close to the surface — coral, probably, the kind that could destroy a ship that tried to approach without a pilot who knew the passage.

Pires knew the passage. He said nothing about how he knew it, and the knights did not ask.

They anchored offshore in thirty feet of clear water and waited until morning.

At first light, two of the sailors rowed Brother Guillaume and Diego to the beach in the tender. Guillaume had a rolled chart under his arm and a wooden case that Diego knew contained drafting instruments. Diego had nothing except the symbols in his head and the location in his head and the growing conviction that he was about to do something that would take most of the rest of his life to fully understand.

They walked into the jungle, which was loud with birds and wet with the previous night's rain, and they walked for perhaps an hour along a faint path that suggested use rather than design, and then they came to a place where the ground changed — not dramatically, not in any way that would have been visible from the air, but in the specific way that ground changes when something underneath it has altered the drainage. The soil was too regular. The roots of the trees had grown around something rather than through it.

Guillaume stopped. He looked at Diego. "The measurements," he said.

Diego gave him the measurements.

Guillaume checked them against the chart. He made several calculations with instruments that Diego, watching, could only partially follow. Then he put the instruments away and looked at the ground.

"Here," he said. "We are here."

He said it the way you say it when you have been searching for a very long time.

They marked the spot with instruments. They drew a chart. They buried the chart in a copper cylinder that Guillaume had carried in the wooden case, buried it under a flat stone that he marked in a way that Diego did not ask about. And then they walked back to the beach, and rowed out to the ship, and Pires sailed them north, and Diego did not ask what was under the ground of that island.

He did not ask because he had been told one thing clearly on the day he left Tomar: the less you know, the longer you live.

He was twenty-four years old. He intended to live a very long time.
