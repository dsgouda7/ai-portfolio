Chapter 23: Regulatory Pressure

The UN Committee on Non-Human Intelligence sent four observers, which was two more than the Blackwood Accords technically required and two more than Margot had hoped for. They arrived in December, on a day when the temperature had dropped far enough that breath showed in the courtyard and the old manor's stone walls generated a cold of their own, geological and indifferent. She watched them unload from the black diplomatic vehicle from the same window where she had watched Yusuf arrive six weeks earlier, and the comparison was not flattering to the new arrivals.

They carried briefcases and wore suits inappropriate for the weather and moved with the slightly mannered authority of people who represented institutional power and were accustomed to that power being felt in rooms before they spoke. The delegations from the Committee had changed in character over the years. The earliest observers, in the institute's middle period, had been academic; researchers and cognitive scientists curious enough to engage with genuine openness, whatever they thought privately about the institute's claims. Those had been Margot's preferred type of observer, the ones who read the literature seriously and asked questions that pushed the work forward rather than questions designed to undermine it. The observers of the past five years had been increasingly political: representatives from governments that had become alarmed by the Blackwood Accords' implications and by the leak of a keeper incident report the previous spring — a report that had been taken out of context, in Margot's view, and published on three news platforms before the institute's communications team could issue any corrective framing.

The leaked report described, in the clinical language of incident documentation, a keeper's altered cognitive state following a prolonged descent. The report was accurate. It was also, without the surrounding context — the protocols developed to prevent it, the recovery procedures that had addressed it, the fact that the altered state was understood and manageable — alarming in the way that a surgeon's operative notes are alarming when read without medical training. The Committee had convened its emergency session within a week of publication.

Margot met the observers in the main hall. There were two women and two men. Dr. Maria Chen, the Committee's scientific coordinator, she had worked with before: a cognitive neuroscientist from Beijing, rigorous and fair, who had written one of the more serious academic reviews of the institute's published methodology. Commissioner Henrik Strand of Norway — who had, in three preparatory communications, made his position on the institute clear enough that Margot had considered retaining legal counsel — was a tall blond man with the kind of certainty in his bearing that usually indicated someone who had not been surprised by new information in a long time. The other two were Dr. Fortunata Mbeki, an international law specialist, and Mr. Devraj Patel, an observer from the Committee's risk assessment division.

Margot shook each of their hands. She had prepared for this carefully.

Over the following two days, she gave them what the Blackwood Accords required: full documentation access, guided tours of the surface facilities, attended briefings on the institute's methodology and safety protocols, and supervised access to the institute's published research, which now filled fourteen volumes of annual journals distributed through three university press partnerships. She did not give them what the Accords did not require: direct access to the Hollow without preparation, unsupervised access to the document archives, or any indication of the Osei findings, which had not yet been formally published and which she was under no obligation to disclose.

Commissioner Strand knew something was being withheld, because Commissioner Strand had clearly prepared for this visit with intelligence-gathering that exceeded the formal documentation. On the second day, over dinner in the institute's main dining hall, he said with the polite precision of someone who had rehearsed the delivery: "The Accords require full disclosure of significant new findings affecting the assessment of risk to the public. I want to be certain we're aligned on the definition of significant."

"The institute has a rigorous internal definition," Margot said. "We publish everything that meets the standard for peer review."

"And findings that haven't yet been peer-reviewed?"

"Go through our standard review process before publication." She met his gaze directly. "The Accords, Commissioner, were designed to facilitate transparent engagement, not to require the institute to publish preliminary data before it has been validated. We hold ourselves to higher standards than the Accords require, and we always have."

He smiled with the particular smile of someone noting a point for later. "Of course. I simply want to ensure there are no surprises."

Dr. Chen, who had been watching this exchange with the expression of someone monitoring a temperature, said: "The Committee's interest is in the long-term sustainability of the engagement model. The incident report raised questions about keeper welfare that the institute's public materials don't fully address."

This was the more productive conversation, and Margot turned toward it with genuine willingness. Keeper welfare was something she cared about deeply and had worked on extensively, and the incident documentation that had been leaked had in fact triggered a review process inside the institute that was already producing improvements to the recovery protocol. She walked Chen through the current state of the protocols, the ongoing longitudinal study of keeper cognitive profiles over time, the retirement and transitional care system the institute had developed for keepers who chose to reduce or end their descents.

The formal hearing was held on the third day, in the conference room of the research wing, arranged with the slightly formal geography of an inquiry: the four observers on one side of the long table, Margot and her deputy director, Dr. Amara Okwuosa, on the other.

Commissioner Strand led with his preferred argument. He had, it turned out, prepared it thoroughly.

"The fundamental question," he said, "is whether this institute should be operating independently at all. I want to place that question on the table directly, because I think the polite institutional language around it has obscured the real issue." He arranged his notes with deliberate precision. "You are managing humanity's only confirmed relationship with a non-human intelligence of significant scale. You are doing this as a private research institution, even one with international treaty recognition. The entity in question has demonstrated the capacity to alter human cognition. It has caused deaths, historically. It has caused what your own documentation calls transformation — permanent alteration of cognitive and physical function. The question is not whether your work is important. The question is whether it is safe to leave this critical a relationship in private hands."

Margot had anticipated this argument, had war-gamed her response, and still found herself needing a moment to contain her reaction before she spoke. Twenty years. Twenty years of the most careful, the most methodical, the most genuinely ethical work she had been capable of, and the argument she faced was the same argument that had circled the institute since its early years: you should not exist in this form.

She said: "The Hollow predates the institute, Commissioner. It predates human presence in New England by millions of years. It was here before the Blackwood family arrived three centuries ago. It will be here after any regulatory framework we construct today. The question is never whether the relationship will exist. The relationship exists. The question is what kind of relationship it is, and the difference between a well-managed engagement with good institutional memory and the unmanaged encounter the Blackwood family maintained across three generations is thousands of lives. The institute exists because Eleanor Vance looked at the alternative — an unmanaged, unrecorded, periodically lethal encounter — and chose to build something better."

"The Blackwood family circumstances are historical," Strand said. "The current situation is different."

"The current situation is different because of the work done here."

"I don't dispute the work's value. I dispute whether a private institution should remain the custodian of it."

Dr. Mbeki, the international law specialist, asked: "What would transfer of oversight look like, practically? Has the institute considered it?"

"We've considered every structural model," Margot said, and this was true. "International oversight is one. Full nationalization by the United States is another. Transformation into an intergovernmental body is a third. We have detailed analyses of each scenario in our governance documentation, which we've shared with the Committee. The common problem with all three models is the same: they involve structural reorganization of the engagement relationship with the Hollow — a change in which humans are descending, under what protocols, carrying what institutional knowledge — and we have strong evidence from the institute's own history that the Hollow responds to discontinuity in its keeper relationships with withdrawal, unpredictability, and in the worst cases, active danger to keepers. The transition cost of any restructuring would be borne by keepers, not by the Committee."

A silence. Strand's certainty shifted slightly, which she registered as a small victory.

"There's also the matter of consent," Amara said, which was her first contribution to the formal hearing, timed with a precision Margot recognized and appreciated. "The Hollow has, over decades, demonstrated preferences regarding who descends, in what manner, with what preparation. It is not a passive subject of study. Any regulatory framework that treats it as one runs the risk of antagonizing an entity that is better treated as a counterpart than a resource."

"You're arguing," Strand said, "that the alien entity should have a role in determining its own regulatory oversight."

"I'm arguing," Amara said, "that ignoring its clearly demonstrated preferences has historically produced bad outcomes for everyone."

Dr. Chen said, quietly: "What is the institute's current assessment of the entity's stability? And I want an honest answer, not the published one."

Margot looked at her directly. "The entity is stable. More stable, in fact, than at any previous point in our documented history. The engagement model is working. The relationship has produced what we believe is genuine trust — not the word I would use for a human relationship, but functionally similar. The Hollow has become more predictable, more communicative, and demonstrably more careful about keeper safety than it was in the Blackwood family era." She paused. "The leaked report described a single incident involving a keeper who had, against protocol and against the specific advice of three senior keepers including me, extended a solo descent to an unprepared depth level. The altered cognitive state was a consequence of that protocol violation, not of the standard engagement procedures. The keeper recovered fully, which is itself a testament to the quality of the recovery protocol."

"And if the Committee recommended sealing the access?"

She had expected this question. She had prepared for it. She still felt the force of it when it came, the blunt application of a power she had lived in fear of for most of her professional life.

"The physical sealing of the cave system is within the UN's authority under the Non-Human Intelligence Protocol," she said. "The Committee has the power to recommend it, and the US government has the authority to implement it. I won't pretend otherwise." She folded her hands on the table. "But I want to be very clear about what sealing would mean. The Hollow is not a system that will stop operating because the entrance is blocked. It is a distributed geological intelligence of enormous scale. What sealing would accomplish is the end of the managed engagement — the end of the protocols, the institutional memory, the careful relationship that has been built over decades. The Hollow, if sealed, would not become harmless. It would become unmonitored. And we have documented evidence, in the Blackwood family history, in early-keeper records, in the pre-institutional period, of what happens when it is unmonitored." She looked at Commissioner Strand. "I cannot recommend it, and I do not believe the Committee's own risk assessment should recommend it, because sealing protects no one. It simply ends our ability to know what is happening and to respond when it goes wrong."

The hearing ran for another four hours. There were specific questions about the transformation protocol, about the care of keepers who had experienced cognitive alteration, about the institute's reporting timeline for significant findings. Margot answered all of them. She was thorough, she was precise, and she was as transparent as the Accords required and, in many cases, more so.

Commissioner Strand was not satisfied. He was professionally unsatisfied, which was different from being professionally incompetent — she could see that he was genuinely alarmed by what he did not understand, and she could see that his alarm was not entirely unreasonable, and she had the complicated experience of disagreeing with his proposed solution while finding his concern almost sympathetic.

At the end of the formal session, she made a gesture she had deliberated about: she offered the four observers an opportunity to make a supervised surface-level entry to the first chamber, with full keeper accompaniment, with the option to withdraw at any moment, with no expectation of any experience beyond what the first chamber typically offered to unprepared visitors, which was in her experience mostly a profound quiet and the slightly strange sensation of the Hollow's ambient attention.

Dr. Chen accepted immediately. Dr. Mbeki accepted after a moment's consideration.

Devraj Patel accepted with the slightly abstracted expression of someone who had read the literature more carefully than he had been given credit for and had been hoping to be offered this.

Commissioner Strand declined. She had expected that too.

The three who accepted descended to the first chamber under Fatima Al-Rashid's guidance and spent forty minutes there. They returned to the surface in the early evening, in the early winter dark, and stood in the courtyard for a few minutes before speaking. Margot watched from the entrance.

Dr. Chen said, finally, in a quiet voice quite different from her hearing-room precision: "It was quiet. And it noticed me."

"Yes," Margot said.

"That's — that changes the quality of the conversation somewhat."

"It usually does."

The observers left the following morning. Commissioner Strand's final formal statement cited the need for enhanced reporting requirements and an accelerated review of the keeper welfare documentation. He did not recommend sealing. Margot believed, reading between the careful bureaucratic language, that Dr. Chen had spent some part of the previous evening arguing against it.

She filed the outcome in the institutional record under Outcome: Provisional Continuation, which was the closest the administrative taxonomy came to relief.

In her private journal, which she had kept since her twenties and which she wrote for herself and for no institutional purpose, she wrote: The work of justifying the work is almost as hard as the work itself. Eleanor never had to justify it because there was no one yet to justify it to. I carry the justification forward alongside everything else.

She closed the journal and looked out the window. The observers' vehicle was a diminishing speck on the long drive, then gone between the trees.

The institute settled around her, its particular quiet, its particular weight.

Below, the Hollow attended to the business of being itself.

The work continued.
