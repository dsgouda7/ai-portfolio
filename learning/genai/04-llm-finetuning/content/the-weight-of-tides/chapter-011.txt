Chapter 11: Congressional Testimony

The hearing room in Washington was designed to intimidate. High ceilings, formal portraits of past committee chairs, cameras positioned to capture every nervous gesture. Claire sat at the witness table, Maya in the gallery behind her, surrounded by lawyers and supporters.

Chairman Senator Douglas Harding gaveled the hearing to order. "We're here to examine claims made by Dr. Claire Merritt regarding alleged underwater structures off the Maine coast. Dr. Merritt, you're under oath. Do you stand by your published findings?"

"I do, Senator."

"You claim to have documented evidence of what you describe as engineered structures and mobile objects that defy conventional explanation. That's an extraordinary claim."

"Extraordinary claims supported by extraordinary evidence, Senator. I've submitted comprehensive documentation: sonar data, underwater footage, independent verification from multiple institutions. All of it available for review."

Senator Patricia Chen leaned forward. "Dr. Merritt, I've reviewed your credentials. Impressive. But this footage could be anything. Unusual geological formations, shipwrecks, even sophisticated hoaxes. Why should we accept your interpretation?"

"Because I'm not interpreting, Senator. I'm documenting. The objects I've filmed move through water in ways that violate known principles of marine propulsion. They emit regular acoustic patterns that don't match any catalogued source. They exist in environments where they shouldn't be, covered in biological growth that suggests significant age. These aren't interpretations. They're observable facts requiring explanation."

"And your explanation is... what? Aliens?"

"My explanation is that I don't have an explanation, Senator. I'm documenting phenomena and calling for serious scientific investigation. The origin and nature of these objects remains unknown."

Representative William Bradford jumped in, hostile. "Dr. Merritt, isn't it true that your father spent forty years obsessing over these same claims? That you've inherited his fixation and are using scientific credentials to legitimize what amounts to a family obsession?"

Claire felt anger rising but kept her voice level. "My father spent forty years carefully documenting environmental changes and unusual phenomena. His methodology was sound, his data valuable. I'm continuing that work with additional resources and expertise. That's how science advances, Representative. Through accumulated observation and analysis."

"Or through confirmation bias. Seeing what you want to see because you're emotionally invested in vindicating a parent's dismissed claims."

"If I were suffering from confirmation bias, Representative, I wouldn't have invited independent verification from Woods Hole, MIT, and other prestigious institutions. I wouldn't have published my methodology for peer review. I wouldn't have welcomed scrutiny. Confirmation bias seeks to avoid challenge. I've actively sought it."

The questioning continued for three hours. Some senators were genuinely curious, asking detailed questions about methodology and evidence. Others were clearly skeptical, looking for inconsistencies or admissions of uncertainty they could exploit. A few seemed terrified by the implications, uncomfortable acknowledging possibilities that challenged their worldview.

Dr. Sarah Chen testified next, presenting technical analysis with calm authority. "These structures exhibit characteristics inconsistent with any known construction methods. The materials, insofar as we can analyze them remotely, don't match terrestrial manufacturing processes. The propulsion systems leave no detectable exhaust or traditional propeller wash. Whatever they are, they represent technology or biology we don't understand."

"Dr. Chen," Senator Harding asked, "in your professional opinion, are we dealing with evidence of non-human intelligence?"

"In my professional opinion, Senator, we're dealing with evidence that demands we ask that question seriously. I can't conclusively prove non-human intelligence. But I can conclusively state that the conventional explanations don't fit the observed phenomena."

Dr. Priya Sharma testified last, describing the 1978 program and its findings. Her testimony was devastating to official denials. She provided documents, names of other researchers, specific details of classified work.

"So the government has known about these phenomena for decades," Senator Chen said slowly. "Known and kept silent."

"Yes, Senator."

"Why?"

"Because acknowledging it raised questions without answers. How do you tell citizens that non-human intelligence operates in American waters when you don't understand who they are, what they want, or how to protect against potential threats? Better to study quietly, maintain observation, wait for more information."

"Better to lie, you mean."

"Better to avoid panic, Senator. Whether that constitutes lying is a philosophical question above my pay grade."

The hearing adjourned with no clear resolution. Senators promised further investigation, demanded additional testimony, called for inter-agency coordination. It was political theater, Claire realized. They would investigate until the story faded from public attention, then quietly let it die.

But something unexpected happened. A senator's aide approached Claire as she left the chamber.

"Dr. Merritt? Senator Chen would like to speak with you privately."

They met in a small office away from cameras. Senator Chen closed the door and removed her formal manner like a mask.

"My grandmother was from Willowport," she said. "I spent summers there as a child. I've heard the stories. My grandfather was one of the men on your father's boat in 1975."

Claire stared. "Paul Chen? You're Paul Chen's granddaughter?"

"He died when I was twelve, but not before telling me what he'd seen. I thought it was just an old man's stories, the kind of tales fishermen tell. But then I saw your testimony, your evidence, and I realized he'd been telling the truth. They all had been."

"Why didn't you say so during the hearing?"

"Because I'm up for reelection and admitting I believe in underwater aliens would be political suicide. But Dr. Merritt, I want to help. Behind the scenes, away from cameras. I can pressure agencies for disclosure, demand proper investigation, provide political cover for research funding. Let me know what you need."

"Why?"

"Because my grandfather asked me to. His last words to me were 'if anyone ever takes this seriously, help them. Don't let them be dismissed the way we were.' I didn't understand then. I do now."

Claire left Washington with an unexpected ally and growing awareness that the story was larger than she'd imagined. Families across generations, bound by shared knowledge of phenomena they couldn't explain. The old fishing communities had carried this secret like an inheritance, waiting for someone with credentials and courage to bring it into light.

Back in Willowport, fifteen days remained before the vote. The congressional hearing had energized both sides. Believers pointed to serious scientists testifying under oath about unprecedented discoveries. Skeptics pointed to the lack of definitive conclusions, the absence of physical specimens, the reliance on footage that could be interpreted multiple ways.

Seabrook had intensified their campaign, emphasizing jobs and economic stability. They brought in workers from previous developments to testify about the prosperity they'd experienced. They promised Willowport residents first access to construction jobs, guaranteed interview slots, preferential treatment for local businesses.

It was effective. Claire saw the needle shifting in polls. Economic anxiety was overwhelming environmental caution. People wanted to believe in the promise of prosperity more than they wanted to protect mysterious entities they'd never seen.

"We're going to lose," Jake said bluntly at a strategy meeting. "The vote is fifteen days away and we're behind by six points. That's outside the margin of error."

"Then we change the conversation," Claire said. "We stop defending scientific uncertainty and start emphasizing economic opportunity. If these entities are real, if they've been here for centuries, then Willowport is sitting on the most significant discovery in human history. That has value. Research tourism, documentary filming, scientific conferences. We could build an entire economy around being the place where humanity first confirmed non-human intelligence."

"That's speculative."

"So is Seabrook's promise of prosperity. At least our speculation is based on something real and unprecedented."

They pivoted the campaign, creating new materials that emphasized opportunity over preservation. "Protect Willowport's Most Valuable Resource" replaced "Save Our Waters." They brought in economists who discussed potential revenue from research facilities, tourism, educational programs.

It started to work. The polls tightened. People began imagining a future where Willowport was famous for something other than declining fish catches. Where their town was the center of scientific attention, drawing researchers and tourists and investment.

But Seabrook wasn't finished. With ten days remaining, Richard Caldwell held a press conference.

"Dr. Merritt's claims are distracting from real issues facing Willowport," he said smoothly. "She's asking the town to bet its future on unproven theories about underwater aliens. We're offering concrete jobs, real investment, guaranteed economic growth. The choice should be clear."

"Mr. Caldwell," Angela Reyes called out, "how do you respond to evidence that Seabrook Development is a front for energy companies interested in deep-sea resource extraction?"

Caldwell's smooth expression cracked slightly. "That's a baseless allegation."

"I have corporate ownership records showing your connections to Meridian Ventures, which is partially owned by three energy companies and a defense contractor specializing in underwater technology. Is that coincidence?"

"Our ownership structure is a matter of public record and entirely legal. Who our investors are doesn't change what we're offering Willowport."

"But it suggests your real interest is ocean access, not land development. That you want these waters for purposes you're not disclosing."

Caldwell ended the press conference abruptly. But Angela's questions had planted doubt. People began asking what Seabrook really wanted, why they were pushing so hard for a development that didn't make obvious financial sense.

The polls shifted again. Eight days out, they were tied.

That night, Claire's phone rang. Unknown number, but she answered.

"Dr. Merritt." The voice was strange, slightly distorted, like someone speaking through water. "We need to speak. Not by phone. Tomorrow night, midnight. Come to the coordinates your father visited most frequently. Come alone."

"Who is this?"

"Someone who has been watching you. Someone who appreciates what you're trying to do. Come alone, Claire. We'll show you what your father never quite managed to photograph. We'll show you what everyone's been fighting over."

The line went dead. Claire stared at her phone, heart pounding.

A direct contact. After all the observation and speculation and careful avoidance, someone—something—was reaching out.

She should tell Sarah. Should inform the research team. Should absolutely not go alone into deep water at midnight to meet an unknown entity.

But she knew she would go. Had to go. This was what her father had spent forty years hoping for: direct contact, clear communication, understanding. She couldn't refuse the invitation he'd never received.

Eight days until the vote. And tomorrow night, Claire Merritt would meet whatever swam in the deep waters off Willowport, face to face with the mystery that had consumed five generations of her family.

The weight of tides had pulled her home. Now those tides would carry her into encounter with entities that had observed humanity for centuries, waited patiently for humans to be ready, and finally decided the time had come.

Claire didn't sleep that night. She prepared equipment, wrote letters to Maya and Dottie in case something went wrong, updated her will. She was terrified and exhilarated in equal measure.

Tomorrow night she would meet them. Tomorrow night everything would change. Tomorrow night, finally, she would understand.
