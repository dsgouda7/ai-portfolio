Chapter 11: The Death That Wasn't

Marcus Chen arrived in Seattle the next morning, his expression grim. They met at Elena's hotel, spreading out the case files across the desk.

"David Mordecai died six weeks ago," Chen said, reading from the Seattle Police Department's report. "Death was ruled natural causes. Heart failure, consistent with his age and medical history. No investigation was conducted because there was no indication of foul play."

"Someone thinks Anna killed him."

"Someone sent you an anonymous text message. That's not evidence." Chen leaned back, thinking. "But it's worth investigating. If David's death wasn't natural, if Anna did kill him to steal the inheritance, then everything changes."

"Can you get access to the autopsy report? The medical examiner's files?"

"I can request them through official channels, but it'll take time. My jurisdiction is Ashmont Bay, not Seattle. I'll need to coordinate with Seattle PD."

Elena made a call to Catherine Reed. "I need to know more about David Mordecai's death. Medical records, autopsy findings, who found the body, everything."

"Why? I thought this was a genealogy investigation, not a criminal case."

"It might be both. I received a tip that David's death wasn't natural. If that's true, we're not just talking about inheritance fraud. We're talking about murder."

Catherine was silent for a moment. "I'll get you what I can. But Elena, if David was murdered, this case becomes exponentially more complicated. The inheritance will be tied up for years."

"Better complicated than letting a killer inherit forty million dollars."

Over the next several days, Elena and Chen dug into David Mordecai's death. The autopsy report showed heart failure, as stated. David had a history of cardiac issues, was on medication, and his death was not unexpected given his age and condition.

But there were details that troubled Elena. David had been found by his home health aide three days after he died. He had apparently collapsed while working at his desk, surrounded by his research papers. The aide, a woman named Patricia Gomez, had called 911 immediately.

Elena interviewed Patricia at the care facility where she worked. She was in her forties, tired-looking, wearing scrubs covered with cartoon animals.

"Mr. Mordecai was a good man," Patricia said. "Very kind, always interested in my life and my family. I worked for him for two years, visited three times a week to help with medication, meals, light housekeeping."

"When did you last see him alive?"

"Three days before I found him. I came on Monday as usual. He seemed fine, healthy as could be expected. Excited about his research, as always. He said he was close to finishing something important, some historical project he'd been working on for years."

"Did he mention Anna Chen?"

"Oh yes, Anna visited frequently. She was lovely, very respectful. She and Mr. Mordecai would spend hours going through old documents, discussing history. He thought of her almost like a granddaughter."

"Were there any other regular visitors?"

"Not really. Mr. Mordecai was quite isolated. His only living relatives were distant cousins he barely knew. Anna was his primary connection to the world, besides me."

"What about his attorney, Catherine Reed?"

"She visited occasionally for will updates and estate planning. The last time was maybe two months before he died."

Elena made notes. "When you found Mr. Mordecai on Thursday, what exactly did you see?"

Patricia's eyes welled with tears. "He was at his desk, slumped over his papers. I thought at first he was just sleeping. But when I touched him, he was cold. He'd been dead for a while."

"What was on his desk? What papers was he working on?"

"Historical documents, like always. Old letters, photographs, genealogical charts. And his laptop was open to a document he'd been writing. Some kind of journal or memoir about his family history."

Elena's interest sharpened. "What happened to that laptop?"

"The police took it initially, then returned it to Ms. Reed as part of the estate. Why?"

"Because if David was writing about his family history right before he died, that document might contain important information about who he believed should inherit his estate."

After leaving Patricia, Elena called Catherine Reed. "I need to see David Mordecai's laptop. Specifically, the last document he was working on before he died."

"I'll have it brought to my office. But I should warn you, when the police returned the laptop, several files appeared to have been corrupted or deleted. Digital forensics specialists tried to recover them but had limited success."

"Corrupted or deleted? That's suspicious."

"Or just bad luck. David was elderly and not particularly tech-savvy. He could have accidentally damaged files."

"Or someone could have deleted them deliberately. I want to see that laptop as soon as possible."

That afternoon, Elena examined the laptop with Chen looking over her shoulder. Most of David's files were intact: genealogical research, correspondence, financial records. But the document he had been working on when he died, the one Patricia had mentioned, was gone. Deleted and partially overwritten, making recovery difficult.

Chen pulled out a USB drive. "I have forensic software that might be able to recover fragments. Let me try."

While Chen worked on data recovery, Elena examined the rest of David's files. She found the correspondence with Anna Chen, hundreds of emails over three years discussing their shared research. The tone was warm, collegial, genuinely affectionate.

She also found correspondence with Thomas Crane, much more recent and much more hostile. Thomas had contacted David six months before David's death, demanding to be included in any inheritance plans. David had refused, stating that Thomas's involvement in the Guardians conspiracy made him unworthy of the Mordecai legacy.

Thomas had responded with legal threats. Then, a month before David's death, the correspondence stopped abruptly.

Chen looked up from the laptop. "I've recovered some fragments of the deleted document. Not complete, but enough to get the gist."

Elena read over his shoulder. The recovered fragments showed David writing about his research into Liu Wei's partnership with J. Mordecai and the subsequent theft. He wrote about his correspondence with Anna and his conclusion that she was likely descended from J. Mordecai through Liu Wei's family.

But there was another fragment, one that made Elena's blood run cold:

"...discovered that Thomas Crane has been attempting to suppress evidence of alternative Mordecai heirs. He hired investigators to track down anyone researching the Mordecai family history. He's threatened legal action against genealogy websites that include Chinese connections to the family. He's systematically trying to erase this branch of the family tree the same way the Guardians conspiracy erased Liu Wei from Ashmont Bay's founding.

I've decided to exclude Thomas entirely from my will. He doesn't deserve the Mordecai legacy. Instead, the inheritance will go to Anna and any other legitimate heirs who can be verified. I'm meeting with Catherine tomorrow to finalize these arrangements.

I hope I'm making the right choice. I hope..."

The fragment ended there. David had died before finishing whatever he was writing.

Chen sat back. "Thomas Crane had motive to kill David. If David was planning to exclude him completely from a forty-million-dollar inheritance, that's substantial motive."

"But the death was ruled natural causes. How would Thomas have killed David and made it look like heart failure?"

"There are ways. Certain drugs can induce cardiac arrest and are difficult to detect in elderly people with existing heart conditions. If Thomas had access to David's home before the body was found..."

"We need to check visitor logs, building security, anything that shows who came to David's apartment in the days before his death."

They spent the evening tracking down building security footage and visitor logs. David's apartment building was old, with minimal security. There were no cameras in the hallways, only at the main entrance.

But the entrance camera had captured something interesting. Three days before David's body was found, the same day Patricia had last seen him alive, Thomas Crane had entered the building. He had signed in under a false name, but facial recognition software confirmed his identity.

Thomas had visited David on Monday. David had been found dead on Thursday. And sometime between those days, David's final document had been deleted.

Elena called Catherine Reed. "Thomas Crane visited David Mordecai three days before David died. He used a false name. He had motive to prevent David from excluding him from the inheritance. I think we need to request an exhumation and a more thorough toxicology examination."

Catherine's voice was troubled. "That's a serious accusation. If we request an exhumation without sufficient evidence, Thomas can sue for defamation. But if you're right, if David was murdered..."

"Then a killer is about to inherit forty million dollars while trying to blame an innocent graduate student."

"I'll file the motion first thing tomorrow morning. But Elena, be careful. If Thomas killed once to protect his inheritance, he'll kill again to protect himself."

That night, Elena and Chen reviewed everything they knew. Two potentially fraudulent claimants, Sarah Mordecai and Richard Grant, with sophisticated forgeries. One complex claimant, Anna Chen, with legitimate historical research but unproven biological descent. And one excluded heir, Thomas Crane, who had motive, opportunity, and means to kill David Mordecai.

"We're missing something," Elena said. "The pieces don't quite fit. If Thomas killed David, why send me an anonymous text blaming Anna? He'd want to stay invisible, not draw attention to the possibility of murder."

"Unless he's trying to frame Anna. Kill David, blame Anna, eliminate both obstacles to inheriting. With David dead and Anna accused of murder, Thomas challenges the will and wins by default."

"That would work, except we found evidence of Thomas visiting David. He wasn't as careful as he thought he was."

"Or someone else wants us to think Thomas is the killer. Someone who knew about his visit, who deleted David's files, who's manipulating the investigation to point at Thomas."

Elena's phone buzzed with another text from the unknown number: "You're looking at the wrong person. Check David Mordecai's medical records more carefully. Check who had access to his medications. The truth is in the details you're missing."

"Someone is feeding us information," Elena said, showing the text to Chen. "Someone who knows what we're investigating and wants to direct us to specific evidence."

"Could be Thomas trying to deflect suspicion. Could be Anna trying to frame Thomas. Could be someone else entirely."

"Or it could be someone who knows the truth and wants it exposed but can't come forward openly."

The next morning, Elena obtained David's medical records through Catherine Reed's legal authorization. She studied them carefully, looking for the details the anonymous texter had mentioned.

David had been on several cardiac medications, prescribed by his doctor and filled monthly at a local pharmacy. The prescriptions were straightforward: beta-blockers, statins, aspirin, standard treatment for someone with his condition.

But there was something odd. David's last prescription refill had been four days before his death. The pharmacy records showed he had picked up all his medications as usual. But when police had inventoried his apartment after his death, one medication was missing from his supply.

Digoxin, a cardiac medication that in high doses can cause heart failure.

"Someone took David's digoxin," Elena said. "And probably used it to kill him. High doses of his own medication, causing an overdose that would look like natural heart failure in someone with his condition."

Chen examined the pharmacy records. "The refill was picked up by Patricia Gomez, his home health aide. She had authorization to collect his medications."

"But Patricia found the body. She called 911. She seemed genuinely grief-stricken."

"She also had complete access to David's apartment and medications. She could have administered a fatal dose of digoxin, removed the evidence, waited a few days, and then 'discovered' the body."

Elena thought about the friendly, tired-looking woman in scrubs covered with cartoon animals. Could she really be a murderer?

"What would be her motive? Patricia wasn't in David's will. She wouldn't inherit anything."

"Maybe Thomas Crane hired her. Maybe she was paid to kill David and frame it as natural causes."

Elena called Patricia's employer, the home health agency. "I need background information on Patricia Gomez. Has she worked for you long?"

The agency director checked records. "Patricia has been with us for ten years. Exemplary record, no complaints. She's worked with dozens of elderly clients, many of whom remembered her fondly in their wills."

"Remembered her in their wills? How often?"

"Let me check... oh. Oh, that's interesting. Seven of Patricia's clients over the past decade have left her bequests. Small amounts, usually five to ten thousand dollars. Appreciation for her care and devotion."

"And how many of those clients died of heart failure?"

A pause. "Let me review the files. This will take a minute."

Elena waited, her heart pounding. She looked at Chen, who had understood the implications immediately.

The director came back on the line. "Miss Voss, five of the seven clients who left Patricia money in their wills died of cardiac-related issues. At the time, the deaths were ruled natural given their ages and conditions. But now that you mention it, the pattern is concerning."

"Did Patricia have access to their medications?"

"Yes. As their home health aide, she managed their medication schedules."

Elena ended the call and immediately contacted Seattle Police Department. "I have evidence of a serial killer operating in Seattle. A home health aide named Patricia Gomez has been murdering her elderly clients and making it look like natural deaths."

Within hours, Seattle PD had Patricia in custody. Her apartment was searched, revealing detailed records of her victims, including David Mordecai. She had been systematically targeting wealthy elderly clients, gaining their trust, manipulating them into leaving her money in their wills, and then killing them with medication overdoses.

She confessed within hours of arrest, explaining with disturbing calmness that elderly people died all the time and nobody questioned it. She had killed twelve people over fifteen years, accumulating nearly half a million dollars from their estates.

But she insisted she hadn't killed David Mordecai.

"I was planning to," she told detectives with Elena and Chen observing through one-way glass. "I had cultivated David for two years, gotten him to trust me. But before I could act, someone else killed him. I found him already dead, and I couldn't report it immediately or I'd lose my chance to be in his will. So I waited three days, like he'd died naturally while I wasn't there."

"Who else would have killed him?" the detective asked.

"I don't know. But when I found him, his papers were scattered, his laptop was open, and he looked... surprised. Like someone he trusted had betrayed him. That wasn't my MO. My clients died peacefully in their sleep. David died at his desk, mid-sentence, shocked."

After the interrogation, Elena sat with Chen in silence. They had found a serial killer, had solved twelve murders, had brought a monster to justice.

But they still didn't know who had actually killed David Mordecai.

Patricia had been planning to kill him but hadn't. Thomas Crane had visited him days before but hadn't necessarily killed him. Anna Chen had been his trusted research partner but might have betrayed him.

Or someone else entirely had wanted David dead and had seized the opportunity when Patricia's suspicious behavior created a convenient scapegoat.

Elena's phone buzzed. Another text from the unknown number: "You found Patricia. Good work. But she's not David's killer. I am. And I'm ready to confess. Meet me at David Mordecai's apartment tomorrow at noon. Come alone. I'll tell you everything."

Elena showed the text to Chen. "Our killer wants to confess."

"Obviously another trap."

"Obviously. But also our only lead. We have to go."

"Not alone. We go in with backup, surveillance, full tactical support."

"Agreed. But Chen, I think I know who the killer is. And if I'm right, this is about to get very personal."

She didn't explain further. But as she reviewed everything she knew about the case, one name kept coming up, one person who had access, means, and motive that they had all overlooked.

Tomorrow, she would learn if she was right. And if she was, the Mordecai inheritance case would end in a way none of them expected.
