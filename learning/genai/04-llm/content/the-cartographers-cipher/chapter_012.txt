Chapter 12: The Confession

David Mordecai's apartment had been sealed as a crime scene after Patricia's arrest, but Catherine Reed had obtained authorization for Elena and the police to access it for the arranged meeting. The apartment was on the sixth floor of an old building in Seattle's Capitol Hill neighborhood, with high ceilings and views of the sound.

Elena arrived at eleven-forty-five, accompanied by Chen and four plainclothes Seattle PD officers. They positioned themselves throughout the building, ready to move in if needed. Elena wore a wire, every word she spoke would be recorded.

She entered the apartment alone, as requested. The space was frozen in time, David's research still spread across his desk, his books lining every wall, his life preserved in paper and ink.

At precisely noon, someone knocked on the door. Elena opened it.

Catherine Reed stood in the hallway.

"Hello, Elena. Thank you for coming."

Elena had suspected, but confirmation still hit like a punch. "You killed David Mordecai."

"Yes. May I come in? I'd like to explain."

Elena stepped aside, letting the attorney enter. Chen and the officers would be listening, recording everything. All Elena had to do was keep Catherine talking.

"Why?" Elena asked. "You were his attorney, his trusted advisor. He relied on you."

Catherine moved to the window, looking out at the city. "I killed him because he was making a terrible mistake. Because his obsession with historical justice was blinding him to reality. Because he was about to destroy lives based on incomplete information and misplaced guilt."

"By excluding Thomas Crane from the inheritance?"

"By giving forty million dollars to Anna Chen, a woman who isn't actually descended from J. Mordecai, who manipulated an elderly man into believing a false genealogy, who was positioning herself to steal a fortune she had no right to."

Elena kept her expression neutral. "You don't believe Anna's claim is legitimate?"

"I know it isn't. I've done extensive research into the Mordecai family tree, far more thorough than David's amateur investigations. Liu Wei really was J. Mordecai's business partner, and he really was cheated out of money by the Guardians conspiracy. But Liu Wei's daughter, Anna's great-great-grandmother, was not Mordecai's biological child. She was Liu Wei's daughter with his wife, no white heritage involved."

"How do you know?"

"Because I found medical records from Liu Wei's wife's physician. She was examined for fertility issues in eighteen seventy-three, the year before the daughter was born. The records explicitly state she was Chinese and had no history of relations with non-Chinese men. The daughter was conceived in wedlock, both parents Chinese."

"So Anna's claim of mixed heritage is false?"

"It's family mythology that became belief, became 'truth' through repetition. Anna genuinely believes she's descended from Mordecai because her family has told that story for generations. But belief doesn't make it real."

"You could have presented this evidence to David. You could have challenged Anna's claim legally. You didn't have to kill him."

Catherine turned from the window, her expression haunted. "I tried to present evidence. David wouldn't listen. He was so committed to the narrative of Chinese investors being erased from history that he couldn't accept evidence contradicting it. He accused me of being racist, of trying to suppress Chinese American contributions to Pacific Northwest development. He told me he was going to finalize his will giving everything to Anna and there was nothing I could do about it."

"So you killed him before he could sign the final version?"

"I tried one more time to reason with him. I went to his apartment with the medical records, determined to make him understand. We argued. He became agitated. I thought he was going to have a heart attack right then. And I realized... if he did have a heart attack, if he died before finalizing the will that excluded Thomas, the problem would be solved."

"You had access to his medications through the information in your files."

"I knew he had digoxin in his apartment. I knew that a high dose could induce cardiac arrest in someone with his condition. While he was in the bathroom, I crushed several pills and dissolved them in his water glass. He drank it without noticing. I left. He was alive when I left, but I knew what would happen within hours."

"And the files? The deleted document on his laptop?"

"I went back the next morning, using the key he'd given me for emergencies. He was dead at his desk, exactly as I expected. I deleted the files that discussed his final will intentions. I removed his digoxin bottle to make the overdose less obvious. And I left."

"What about Patricia? You knew she was planning to kill David?"

"I suspected she was grooming him, positioning herself for an inheritance. But I didn't know she'd killed others until your investigation revealed it. In a way, she was the perfect scapegoat. Everyone would assume she'd killed David like she'd killed her other clients. I never expected you to look deeper."

Elena felt disgust rising. "You killed a man to prevent him from giving his money to someone you deemed unworthy. You decided you knew better than David what should happen to his estate."

"I killed a man to prevent a fraud. Anna Chen is not a Mordecai heir. Giving her forty million dollars would be as much a theft as the original conspiracy that stole from Liu Wei. Two wrongs don't make a right."

"But murder does?"

Catherine's composure finally cracked. "I didn't want to kill him. I cared about David. But he was being manipulated, deceived by a woman who saw him as a meal ticket and a research opportunity. Someone had to protect him from his own misguided sense of justice."

"By killing him?"

"By making sure the Mordecai fortune went to legitimate heirs, not opportunistic frauds."

"Heirs like Thomas Crane? The man you're now representing in his challenge to the will?"

Catherine's silence was answer enough.

"You killed David and framed Patricia to make Thomas the primary heir. As his attorney, you'll manage the estate, collect substantial fees, and maintain control of the Mordecai fortune. This was never about justice. It was about money and power."

"It was about protecting the truth. About ensuring that historical wrongs weren't compounded by modern frauds. Anna Chen doesn't deserve forty million dollars. Thomas Crane, despite his faults, is the legitimate heir."

"And what about Liu Wei? What about the Chinese investors who were erased from history and never compensated? Doesn't someone deserve restitution for that?"

"Restitution should go to actual descendants and victims, not to graduate students with fabricated family trees and unproven claims."

The apartment door opened. Chen entered, followed by Seattle PD officers.

"Catherine Reed, you're under arrest for the murder of David Mordecai. You have the right to remain silent..."

Catherine didn't resist as they handcuffed her. She looked at Elena with something like sadness.

"You think I'm the villain here. But you're wrong. Anna Chen manipulated a lonely old man into believing a false genealogy. I just prevented her from profiting from that manipulation."

"Maybe Anna's genealogy is false," Elena said. "Maybe she's not biologically descended from J. Mordecai. But David Mordecai chose to believe her claim and leave her his money. That was his choice to make, not yours. You had no right to take that choice from him."

They led Catherine away. Elena stood in David's apartment, surrounded by his research, thinking about justice and inheritance, truth and belief.

Later that day, Elena met with Anna Chen at a coffee shop near the university. The graduate student looked exhausted, overwhelmed by everything that had happened.

"Catherine killed David because she thought I was lying about my heritage?"

"She believed your claim was false and that David was being manipulated. She decided murder was preferable to fraud."

"Was she right? Is my claim false?"

Elena pulled out the medical records Catherine had found. "According to these, your great-great-grandmother was not J. Mordecai's biological daughter. She was Liu Wei's daughter by his wife, no European ancestry involved."

Anna read the documents, her hands shaking. "But the family stories... we've always been told..."

"Family stories become myths become truth through repetition. Someone in your family started a story, possibly to explain physical features or to create a connection to the Mordecai wealth. Over generations, the story became believed as fact."

"So I'm not a Mordecai heir. I have no right to the inheritance."

"Biologically, probably not. But that doesn't change the fact that Liu Wei was Mordecai's partner and was cheated out of money. It doesn't change the historical wrong that was committed against your family. And it doesn't change the fact that David Mordecai wanted you to have his inheritance."

"But if I'm not really his heir..."

"He believed you were. More importantly, he believed you would use the money to continue fighting for historical justice, to expose the wrongs that were committed against Chinese investors and indigenous peoples. That's what mattered to him."

Anna sat in silence for a long moment. "I don't feel right taking money I'm not entitled to. Even if David wanted me to have it, even if he believed I was his heir, I know the truth now. The honest thing would be to refuse the inheritance."

"That's your choice to make. But consider this: Liu Wei invested in Ashmont Bay and received nothing. His descendants have lived for generations with the knowledge that their ancestor was cheated. If you take the inheritance, you could use it to establish a foundation supporting Chinese American historical research, documenting the contributions that were erased. You could do exactly what David wanted: fight for historical justice."

"Even though I'm not a blood heir?"

"David cared more about your character and your commitment to truth than about biology. That's why he excluded Thomas, even though Thomas is definitely a biological descendant. Inheritance isn't just about blood. It's about values, legacy, what you do with what you're given."

Anna nodded slowly. "I need to think about this. I need to figure out what's right."

Over the following weeks, the legal situation resolved itself in unexpected ways. Thomas Crane's challenge to David's will was dismissed after Catherine's arrest. The court ruled that Catherine, as the drafter of the will, had violated her duty to her client by killing him to prevent the will's execution. Thomas's case was tainted by his association with Catherine's crime.

Sarah Mordecai and Richard Grant were prosecuted for inheritance fraud and received suspended sentences in exchange for admitting their fabrications.

Patricia Gomez was sentenced to life without parole for twelve counts of murder.

And Anna Chen, after weeks of deliberation, accepted the inheritance with the condition that it be placed in a trust dedicated to researching and documenting Chinese American contributions to Pacific Northwest development. She would serve as the trust's director, continuing the work she and David had started together.

The trust's first project would be a comprehensive history of Liu Wei's business empire and his hidden partnership with J. Mordecai, ensuring that the Chinese investors who built the region would never be erased from history again.

Elena attended the trust's announcement ceremony, watching Anna speak about historical justice and the responsibility that came with inherited wealth. Detective Chen stood beside her, his pride in his cousin evident.

"She's doing the right thing," he said.

"She's doing what David wanted. Sometimes that's the same as the right thing."

After the ceremony, Elena and Chen walked through Seattle's streets, past buildings that had stood for over a century, past monuments to pioneers and founders, past the carefully curated history that left out as much as it included.

"Are you going back to Ashmont Bay?" Chen asked.

"Eventually. I still have paperwork to finish from the Guardians conspiracy trials. But I think I'll take a few days first. Decompress. Process everything."

"Good. You've earned it. Two major cases in three months, both involving century-old conspiracies and multiple murders. You need a break."

"I need a vacation somewhere with no historical secrets and no inheritance fraud."

"Is there such a place?"

"Probably not. But I'm going to look."

They parted ways at the ferry terminal. Chen headed back to Ashmont Bay, back to his job and the ongoing aftermath of the founding families' exposure. Elena boarded a ferry to the San Juan Islands, seeking a few days of peace before returning to her complicated life.

As the ferry pulled away from Seattle, Elena thought about Adelaide Thorne and David Mordecai, both killed for trying to expose historical wrongs. Both died before seeing justice achieved, but both had set in motion changes that would outlast them.

The truth was messy, incomplete, and often painful. But it was always worth pursuing, even at cost, even in danger.

That's what Elena had learned from Adelaide. That's what she would carry forward.

The ferry moved through the sound, past islands and inlets, past waters that had witnessed centuries of history. Some of that history was heroic, some shameful, most a mixture of both.

But all of it deserved to be known, understood, and when necessary, corrected.

That was the work Elena had chosen. That was the burden and privilege of seeking truth in a world built on convenient lies.

She would rest for a few days, then return to it. Because there would always be another case, another secret, another conspiracy waiting to be exposed.

And Elena Voss would be there to expose it.

One investigation at a time, one truth at a time, until the world looked a little more like it should and a little less like it did.

That was enough. That was everything.

The sun set over the sound, painting the water gold and crimson. Elena watched until the light faded, then went below deck to sleep.

Tomorrow would bring new challenges. But tonight, she could rest in the knowledge that justice, imperfect and incomplete as it was, had been served once more.
