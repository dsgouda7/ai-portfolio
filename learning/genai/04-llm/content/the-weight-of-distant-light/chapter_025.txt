Chapter 25: The Shape of Sixty-Three Years

The joint scientific council held its founding session in a broad, open-walled chamber the Kirenn called the Long Listening Hall, a structure grown from the same living architecture as the rest of Vessa Meridian, its curved wooden ribs rising overhead in a pattern that Dr. Marlowe, attending despite the visible toll his advancing years now took on even a short walk from the settlement's shuttle pad, observed with quiet delight bore an unmistakable resemblance to the ribbed, curved decks of the Meridian's Promise itself.

"Two peoples, half a galaxy apart in origin," he said to Aria, as they settled into seats arranged in the hall's careful circular pattern, "and we both built our most important gathering places in the shape of a ship's hull, or a hall shaped like held hands. I don't think that's coincidence, Aria. I think it's something closer to convergence. Perhaps any people patient enough, and frightened enough, and hopeful enough to build for the long, uncertain future, arrives eventually at the same instinct: build the shape that holds people close, and holds against the wide, empty dark outside."

The council's membership reflected the delegation's own careful diversity, drawing equally from human and Kirenn expertise: Wren participating through a translation relay alongside its Kirenn counterpart, an artificial intelligence system the Kirenn called the Attendant, built over centuries of their own careful engineering tradition and bearing, Aria was fascinated to learn across the council's early sessions, its own distinct philosophical commitments regarding the proper relationship between artificial minds and the biological peoples they served, commitments that echoed, in ways both peoples found mutually illuminating, the careful boundaries the First Charter had once placed on Wren's own authority.

The council's founding mandate, established jointly by the Continuance Council and the Kirenn city-state assembly, was direct and urgent: to develop, within the sixty-three years remaining before the boundary's advance reached Aeon-Verdant's orbit, some genuine countermeasure capable of protecting the world's now-shared population, drawing on every resource both peoples could bring to bear, including, crucially, continued correspondence with the Choir itself, whose own centuries of desperate study offered both a foundation to build upon and a sobering reminder of how thoroughly that same study had ultimately failed to save its own makers.

It was this last point that produced the council's first genuine point of friction, raised during its third session by a Kirenn representative named Voss Ilaren Thet, a sharp, formally reserved biologist whose careful, skeptical questions Aria came to respect deeply over the following years, even as she found herself, in that first tense exchange, unexpectedly defensive on the Choir's behalf.

"The Sky-Elders studied this danger for centuries longer than either of our peoples has yet existed as civilizations," Thet said, through Wren's careful translation, "and their study, however thorough, did not save them. I do not raise this to dismiss the value of their knowledge, which I do not doubt has already advanced our own understanding considerably. I raise it because I believe this council does this world's people a disservice if it proceeds as though the Sky-Elders' failure carries no lesson beyond the raw data they left behind. I want to understand, clearly, why we believe two younger, less advanced civilizations, working together for a mere sixty-three years, can succeed where a single vastly more advanced civilization failed across centuries of dedicated effort."

The question hung in the Long Listening Hall's open air for a long moment, and it was, in the end, Wren itself that offered the answer Aria found most persuasive.

"I do not believe the comparison is as unfavorable as it may first appear," Wren said. "The Choir's own transmitted knowledge indicates that their civilization's study of the boundary began only after its advance had already become an immediate, undeniable crisis, following centuries of earlier complacency during which the phenomenon was dismissed as a distant curiosity rather than a genuine existential threat. Our own two peoples begin this effort with sixty-three years of confirmed advance warning, a resource the Choir's own ancestors were never given, having discovered the danger only when it was already reshaping their nearest worlds. I believe advance warning, properly used, is not a small advantage. It may, in fact, be the single most valuable resource either of our peoples could have been given for this specific challenge, precisely because it allows us to proceed with careful, patient research rather than the desperate, reactive study the Choir's own ancestors were forced to undertake."

Thet considered this for a long moment, her feathered brow furrowing in a way that Aria had learned, across the preceding weeks, indicated genuine consideration rather than simple skepticism. "That is a meaningful distinction," she said finally. "I withdraw my concern, though not my caution. I would ask that this council commit, from its very first working session, to treating time as the precious resource it clearly is, rather than allowing sixty-three years to feel, as such spans of time always eventually feel to those living through them, like an inexhaustible luxury."

The council took her caution to heart, establishing within its first month a structured, staged research program that Aria found herself, over the following years, deeply and personally involved in shaping: an initial fifteen-year phase dedicated to fundamental research, drawing on the combined observational data both peoples' astronomical traditions could provide, alongside continued correspondence with the Choir regarding the deep physical principles underlying the boundary's advance; a subsequent phase focused on developing and testing candidate countermeasures at increasing scales, informed by whatever the fundamental research phase revealed; and a final phase, its precise timeline left deliberately flexible pending the results of the earlier work, dedicated to full-scale implementation of whatever solution the joint effort ultimately developed.

It was not, Aria understood from the earliest weeks of that ambitious, decades-spanning program, a plan without genuine political risk, both among the Kirenn city-states, several of whom harbored real and understandable anxiety about committing so much of their world's resources and attention to a partnership with visitors who had, after all, arrived in their sky less than a year before, and among the ship's own population, where a small but vocal faction, echoing in its rhetoric an argument Aria remembered all too well from Magistrate Ashe's own earliest opposition to the Lantern investigation, began to raise a question that the Council's leadership had hoped, perhaps too optimistically, might not resurface in this new chapter of the ship's long journey: whether committing to Aeon-Verdant at all, given the confirmed sixty-three-year threat now hanging over the world, represented genuine wisdom, or a second, more consequential version of the same fear-driven recklessness Ashe himself had once warned against.

It was Corvin Ashe himself, in fact, now well into his seventies and living quietly in his modest quarters near the Under-Hold's uppermost access hatch, who addressed this resurgent faction directly, in a rare public statement delivered before the full Council some eight months into the settlement's first year, a statement that Aria, present in the gallery that day alongside Nyla, would remember as one of the most moving pieces of political rhetoric she had ever witnessed.

"I understand the fear driving this new hesitation," Ashe said, his voice thinner now than the confident, patient authority Aria remembered from her youth, but no less carefully considered for its new fragility. "I understand it because I once carried that same fear myself, and built an entire career on the conviction that this ship's highest duty was caution over courage, distance over engagement, safety over the harder, more uncertain work of genuine trust. I was wrong then, and I believe those now arguing that we should abandon this world, and this partnership, in search of some hypothetical safer destination elsewhere, are wrong for the same reason I once was: because the safety such caution promises is an illusion, purchased at the cost of exactly the kind of courage and partnership that has, across every crisis this ship has faced in my own lifetime, proven to be our actual salvation. We do not know, with certainty, that another world exists within reach of whatever fuel and time this ship still possesses, uninhabited and untouched by any comparable danger. We know, with certainty, that we have found, on this world, a people who waited sixteen generations to meet us as partners rather than strangers, and a shared sixty-three-year window in which two civilizations working together may yet achieve what neither could achieve alone. I do not believe that is a chance either of our peoples should abandon out of fear. I believe, having spent my whole life learning this lesson far too slowly, that it is instead the single greatest opportunity either of our peoples has ever been given, and I ask this chamber, and every soul who calls this ship or this world home, to meet it with the same hard-won courage that first taught us how to answer a lantern in the dark."

The faction advocating departure did not entirely dissolve in the wake of Ashe's address, and Aria understood, watching the debate continue in various forms across the following months, that some genuine, reasonable disagreement about the wisdom of the joint council's ambitious commitment would likely persist for years to come, a fact she had, by this point in her long, transformed life, come to accept as simply the ordinary texture of a free people governing themselves honestly rather than a crisis requiring resolution. But the Council's formal vote, convened some weeks after Ashe's address, confirmed the settlement's continued commitment to Aeon-Verdant and its joint research partnership with the Kirenn by a margin nearly as decisive as the Registry petition's own historic vote fourteen years before, and Aria, watching the tally resolve, felt a fierce, hopeful pride in a ship, and now a settlement, that had learned, across two full generations of hard, patient crisis, exactly the kind of people it wanted to be.

Corvin Ashe did not live to see the joint council's research program reach even its first milestone. He died peacefully, in his modest quarters near the Under-Hold, some three years after that address, and it was Nyla, at Speaker Renn's specific request, who delivered his eulogy before a Council chamber that had, across the span of his long, complicated career, watched him transform from the crisis's most formidable early opponent into one of its most eloquent later champions.

"He taught me something I did not expect to learn from a man who once tried to have my own people's petition buried in committee out of fear," Nyla said, her voice carrying the same fierce, considered honesty Aria had loved in her friend since childhood. "He taught me that the measure of a person is not whether they are ever wrong, because every one of us, given a long enough life, will be wrong about something that matters. The measure of a person is whether they are capable, when shown the truth clearly enough and honestly enough, of choosing to change, even when that change costs them everything they once believed defined them. Magistrate Ashe changed, more than once, over the course of a long and difficult life, and every time he changed, this ship, and now this world, became a little more capable of the courage it needed to survive. I hope, when my own time comes to be measured, that I will have proven half as capable of that kind of change as the man we honor today."

Aria, listening from the gallery with Oskar's hand in hers and Mira, now nine years old, quiet and solemn beside her, found herself thinking, as she often did in moments of genuine grief, of eleven pulses and seven pulses in the quiet hours of a single ordinary night, and of just how far that first small, patient signal had carried an entire people, across fear and courage and loss and hope, toward a green world beneath an alien sun that both her own people and the Kirenn now called, in the fullest and most hard-won sense of the word, home.
