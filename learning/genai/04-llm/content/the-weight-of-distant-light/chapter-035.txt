Chapter 35: The Second Generation

Eight years after Aria Voss Delane's death, the joint school held its founding-day ceremony on a mild morning in late spring, as it had held it every year since the school's establishment, with the story told fresh and the luminous night-moths released into the settlement's gardens and the two peoples gathered in the Long Listening Hall's outer courtyard while the morning light came in off the harbor and the Choir's patient signal, received and re-broadcast through the hall's primary array, moved through the stone of the building's foundations and into the soles of everyone's feet in the soft, barely-perceptible way it always did.

Mira had told the story herself for the first three years after Aria's death, because the role of primary storyteller had fallen to her as naturally as the role of archive-keeper had, and because there had been no one yet prepared to take it from her. In the fourth year she had begun handing portions of it to others: to the joint council's cultural liaison, to the Kirenn elder Vranna-ai-Sessil, who had served as one of the settlement's first Choir communicators and who could tell the story's Kirenn dimension with an authority Mira could not replicate, and to the school's head teacher, a woman named Joss Tanner whose gift for the right word at the right moment for a young audience had made her the natural custodian of the version told to children. By the eighth year, Mira's role in the ceremony had become primarily that of witness: present, contributing a brief formal statement of welcome in her capacity as archive-keeper, but no longer carrying the story's full weight on her own.

She was seventy-five, and her back had acquired the mild but consistent opinion about extended standing that she associated with certain kinds of maturity, and she had positioned herself near the courtyard's stone bench in the second row of the assembled adults, beside her daughter Renna, who at forty-five was old enough to remember Aria clearly and young enough to attend the ceremony with the specific combination of reverence and impatience that characterized her generation's relationship to the founding history. And beside Renna, cross-legged on the courtyard stones with the complete physical ease of the young, was Sael.

Sael was twenty-two, and she was the reason Mira had been thinking, for the past several months, about the nature of aptitude and whether it was a thing that could travel through a family across generations, depositing itself in unexpected configurations, arriving in a grandchild as something that had been, in the grandmother, a specific exercise of will and attention. Aria's gift had not been, strictly speaking, a gift at all, in the sense of an innate capacity that existed prior to its application: it had been, as the official record correctly documented and as Eddan Perris had helped Mira understand in more personal terms, a cultivated habit, the practiced art of paying closer attention to a small and easily ignored pattern than any institutional protocol required her to do. There was nothing genetic about that, nothing that could be passed through blood; it was a discipline, and disciplines were taught or they were developed through temperament, and Sael's temperament was in many ways so different from Aria's that the comparison was almost misleading.

Except in this one thing.

Sael had come to the archive six months before the ceremony, on a morning in the preceding autumn when the harbor light had the particular amber quality of the season's last warm weeks, and had sat across the reading table from Mira with the self-possessed calm that Mira had always associated with her, even as a child, and said: I've been hearing something in the Choir's signal that I can't find documented anywhere, and I'd like to know if you think I'm imagining it.

Mira had asked her to describe it.

Sael had thought for a moment, in the way that indicated she was not uncertain but was trying to find language adequate to something that did not naturally resolve into language, and then she had said: it's like a secondary texture. Underneath the primary pulse sequence, underneath everything that's been analyzed and documented, there's something that doesn't have the same rhythmic structure as the main signal. It's not random, but it's not counted the way the eleven-and-seven pattern is counted. It's more like — she had stopped and started again — it's more like the difference between the beat of a piece of music and the melody that moves above it. Everyone has been listening to the beat for forty years. I think there's a melody.

Mira had spent a long time looking at her granddaughter across the reading table.

She had then spent six weeks going through every signal analysis in the archive, from Aria's original recordings to the most recent Choir communication logs maintained by the Long Listening Hall's current staff, and she had found, at the end of those six weeks, that Sael was correct: that there was a sub-pattern in the Choir's signal that had been logged by the analysis systems as noise, as the kind of sub-threshold variation that the systems were programmed to filter out in the interest of producing clean, processable data, and that had therefore been sitting in the filtered-noise logs for forty years, unexamined, not because it was unimportant but because no one had thought to look at the filtered noise with the specific suspicion that it might be worth attending to.

Aria would have recognized this immediately. She had spent three years of the contact program's earliest work arguing that the things the institutional protocol had decided to filter out deserved the same attention as the things the protocol had decided to keep, and the argument had seemed, by the time of Aria's death, to be so thoroughly established that the settlement's historians wrote about it as though it had always been obvious. And in the specific technical domain that Aria had been working in — the domain of signal analysis, of distinguishing structured patterns from interference — the argument had, in fact, been incorporated into the analysis protocols, and the systems had been updated accordingly. But the update had applied to the primary analysis suite, the one that processed the main pulse sequences. The filtered-noise logs, which were a separate system, had never been re-examined in light of it.

Sael had not known this history when she heard the secondary texture. She had not, she told Mira, been thinking about her great-grandmother's analytical methods or their institutional legacy. She had simply been sitting in the Long Listening Hall's lower receiving room on a quiet afternoon, as she sometimes did because she found the signal's presence in the building's stone calming in a way she could not explain to people who hadn't experienced it, and she had heard something she hadn't heard before, with the freshness of hearing that belongs to people who have not yet accumulated the assumptions that tell more experienced listeners what is possible and what is background.

"Your great-grandmother would have known immediately what this was," Mira told her, the afternoon she had finished reviewing the analysis. "Not because she was uniquely brilliant, though she was more than usually acute, but because this is exactly the kind of thing she spent her whole career paying attention to. The thing that the official analysis had decided didn't matter."

Sael had said, without particular vanity: I know. I've been reading her papers.

This was, Mira had come to understand over the preceding six months, not the whole truth, or not quite. Sael had been reading her great-grandmother's papers, but she had also been reading the signal itself, in a way that was different from the way the archive's documented analysts had read it, and the difference was not entirely explicable by the papers. There was something in Sael's relationship to the Choir's signal that Mira could not trace to any particular influence, any particular education or experience, that existed prior to the reading, prior to the training, as a kind of native orientation, a way of being present to a particular kind of information that felt, to Mira watching it, like recognition rather than acquisition.

She had not said this to Sael. She had not said it to Callum, who had heard about Sael's discovery through the archive's natural information channels and had spent two weeks examining the filtered-noise logs with an excitement that was more than scholarly, though he kept it carefully professional. She had not said it to Renna, who was Sael's mother and who had the particular relationship to her daughter's unusual abilities that parents of unusually talented children often had: a combination of pride and the quiet, persistent worry of someone who understood that exceptional aptitude in an exceptional domain tends to bring with it exceptional demands.

What she had done, instead, was sit with the observation in the patient, recursive way that the most important observations required, testing it from different angles, allowing it to develop density before she attempted to act on it.

The founding-day ceremony was, in its eighth year, a more elaborate occasion than it had been in its first. This was partly the natural consequence of institutional maturation, the accumulation of tradition that any yearly event acquires when enough iterations have produced enough participants with enough memories of previous versions to want to see those memories honored in the current one. But it was also the consequence of genuine growth: the settlement had expanded, and the joint school had expanded with it, and the ceremony now drew not just Vessa Meridian's population but delegations from the three secondary settlements that had been established, in the years since the original landing, along the northern and eastern coastlines of the archipelago's main island and on the nearest of the chain's smaller islets. People came by boat, some of them, a journey of several hours for the most distant settlements, and the outer courtyard, which had in the first years been adequate to hold the entire gathered population, was now supplemented by a secondary overflow space in the hall's lower gardens where the ceremony was broadcast through ambient speakers for the additional hundreds who could not fit in the original space.

It was, Mira thought, watching the overflow area fill from her position near the courtyard bench, exactly the kind of growth that her mother had always said was the truest measure of a settlement's health: not the growth of infrastructure, not the growth of institutional complexity, but the growth of the population's need to gather for this particular purpose, the need to hear this particular story told fresh every year, which was not nostalgia but something more functional, the maintenance of a shared understanding of why the settlement existed and how it had come to be, the ongoing work of a community reminding itself that it had been built on choices and that the choices were worth remembering.

The second generation — the people born on Aeon-Verdant who had never known the ship — had their own relationship to the ceremony that differed, in ways Mira had come to find interesting, from both the original settlers' relationship and the children's relationship. The children heard the story as story, which was appropriate; they were still in the phase of encountering it primarily as narrative, as the foundational tale that explained the world they were growing up in. The original settlers heard it as memory, or as memory's shadow, the particular quality of hearing a story about events you lived through and finding the story both accurate and somehow insufficient, missing the texture of what the living had actually been like. The second generation heard it as inheritance: neither the story they had lived nor a story new to them, but the story that had formed the world before they arrived to experience it, and that therefore had a kind of prior claim on everything they were, whether they had chosen that claim or not.

One of Mira's ongoing research interests, in the years since she had formalized the oral history project, was the way the second generation talked about Earth. Not about the voyage or the ship, which they engaged with as history in the more conventional sense — documented, analyzable, their parents' and grandparents' experience — but about Earth itself, which was necessarily different in a way she had spent several years trying to characterize accurately. The second generation spoke of Earth the way the ship's second and third generations had spoken of it, as a place that existed in the register of story rather than memory, but with a crucial added distance: the ship's second generation had at least had parents who had stood on Earth's surface and breathed its air and carried, in the specific texture of their grief for it, a transmission of the place's reality that was not information but something closer to presence. The second generation of the settlement had parents who had never seen Earth either, who were themselves carrying an inherited grief, and the inheritance was now two removes deep, the grief of the grief, which had a different quality than the original and a different stability.

What Mira had found, in interview after interview with second-generation residents, was not that Earth had become less important to them but that it had become more completely symbolic, which was to say more completely theirs rather than less. Earth as the origin, the direction from which the oldest light came, the world that existed in the cultural archive but not in anyone's living memory, had, paradoxically, become a richer conceptual inheritance as it had become a more distant one, the way a founding myth deepens across generations as it loses the contingency of recent history and becomes purely the story it was always in the process of becoming.

She thought about this every founding day, watching the children and the second generation and the original settlers and the Kirenn observers all hearing the same story at their very different removes from its events, and finding that the story held them all, which was not a simple achievement but was perhaps the most important function of the specific ceremony they had built: not to preserve the contact's history in aspic but to keep it alive as a common present, something that was always arriving rather than always receding, always about now as well as about then., the story told with Joss Tanner's particular gift for the dramatic pause, the children in the front rows wide-eyed at the moment of the first pulse's transmission, the Kirenn adults standing in a loose arc to the courtyard's east side with the patient attentiveness they brought to all human cultural events, which was one of the things Mira had always loved about them: the Kirenn did not merely tolerate the settlement's human ceremonies but attended them with a genuine quality of interested presence that suggested they found the ceremonies informative rather than irrelevant, as if the human way of encoding meaning in narrative and repetition were a form of communication worth continuing to learn.

The children seated on the courtyard's stone were, for the most part, the third generation. The first children born on Aeon-Verdant, the ones who had never known the ship at all, were now in their late thirties and forties, settled into the settlement's professional and civic life with the practical confidence of people for whom this world was simply the world, not a replacement for another one or the fulfillment of a promise made by dead ancestors but a straightforward given, the context within which everything else happened. Their relationship to the founding story was the relationship of people whose parents had lived through an event they themselves knew only through account, which gave them a particular clarity about it, unmuddied by the freight of personal experience, but also a certain distance, the distance of all secondhand knowledge.

And then there were the children's children, the third generation, who knew the founding story the way Mira's generation had known the stories of the ship's launch: as mythology, which was to say as something true but not personally verifiable, belonging to a category of knowledge that was held on trust rather than confirmed by memory. The ship was real to them in the way that very old buildings were real: as structure, as material, as a fact of the settlement's infrastructure, the alloy panels and the composite sheeting and the repurposed hull sections that showed up in the older parts of Vessa Meridian's construction, still recognizable to anyone who knew what they were looking at, carrying in their molecular structure the legacy of a voyage that had ended before these children's parents were born.

"Grandmother," Sael said quietly, leaning slightly toward her during the story's middle section, "the woman in the second Kirenn row, behind Vranna-ai-Sessil. Can you see her?"

Mira looked without turning her head, which was something she had learned to do at ceremonies, the skill of peripheral attention. She saw a Kirenn adult of indeterminate age — the Kirenn's physical aging was different enough from the human pattern that she had never fully learned to read it — standing slightly apart from the main Kirenn group, oriented not toward Joss Tanner but toward Sael, with the particular quality of fixed attention that the Kirenn expressed through the alignment of their entire upper body rather than just their eyes.

"I see her," Mira said.

"She was there last year too," Sael said. "In the same place. And the year before, I think, though I'm less sure about that. She doesn't seem to be with any of the regular Kirenn visitors."

Mira was quiet for a moment, watching the ceremony around them while turning this observation over in her mind. The Kirenn attended the founding-day ceremony in consistent groups, the same elders and cultural liaisons who had attended for years, the same cohort of young Kirenn adults who served as cultural exchange participants in the joint school's reciprocal program, the same children who were, in the joint school's third and fourth forms, studying alongside their human counterparts the history of first contact from both peoples' perspectives simultaneously. She knew most of the regular Kirenn attendees by sight, if not all of them by name, and the woman Sael was describing was not among the faces she recognized.

"I don't know her," she said.

"She knows something about me," Sael said, with a matter-of-fact quality that made the observation sound less strange than it was. "Or she knows something I have. I've been trying to work out which one."

Mira looked at her granddaughter, who was watching the ceremony with the same expression she always wore at the founding-day events: attentive, slightly inward, as if she were simultaneously present in the courtyard and listening to something just beyond the register of ordinary conversation.

It was, Mira thought, looking at that expression, almost exactly the expression her mother had worn in the Long Listening Hall in the years when the contact program was most intense, the expression of someone whose attention was distributed between the immediate social world and a different kind of information that existed alongside it and required a different kind of listening to receive. She had not seen that expression on anyone in the forty years since.

She saw it now, on her granddaughter's face, in the morning light of Aeon-Verdant's late spring, while the story of the beginning was told fresh to the settlement's children, and the harbor sound moved through the courtyard's stone, and the signal moved with it, patient and mathematical and older than anyone present could entirely comprehend, still doing what it had always done, still finding new listeners in every generation, still, in some way she was only beginning to understand, still learning who was listening.

The ceremony ended with the release of the night-moths, which rose in a luminous cloud from the garden baskets and dispersed into the morning with the particular quality of beauty that comes from things that exist very briefly and very completely, and Mira watched them go, and then watched the Kirenn adult Sael had noticed, who had turned away from the released moths before the rest of the crowd had, and was moving, with unhurried purposefulness, toward the Long Listening Hall's eastern entrance.

She put her hand briefly on Sael's shoulder, which was her way of saying I noticed too, without requiring the ceremony's end to actually say it aloud, and Sael covered her hand with her own for a moment, which was her way of saying I know, and the two of them stood in the brightening morning while the night-moths were lost in the larger light, and the archive's east window caught the sun, and the harbor moved, and the signal moved with it, and something that had been in the process of becoming for several years became, in that moment, just perceptibly more possible.
