Chapter 8: Three Decks Down

The ladder into the Under-Hold began behind an unmarked maintenance hatch on the lowest accessible level of Deck Nine, disguised by decades of habit and deliberate neglect as nothing more than another equipment access point, and Aria had descended it so many times since childhood that her hands found the worn rungs without needing her eyes to guide them, even in the dim, flickering light of the crawlspace's ancient emergency lamps.

The Under-Hold, when she finally dropped from the last rung onto its packed-earth-and-composite floor, always struck her the same way it had struck her as a child sneaking down with Nyla for the first time: as a whole hidden world folded into the ship's forgotten margins, warmer and closer and louder with human voices than the wide, echoing official decks above, strung with improvised lighting and hung laundry and the smell of cooking that used ingredients the Garden Ring's official ration formulas never quite accounted for. Three generations of Unlisted families had built something genuinely alive down here, in defiance of a Registry that refused to acknowledge their existence, and Aria had never been able to decide, across all the years she had spent slipping between the ship's two worlds, whether that fact made her prouder of her friend or angrier on her behalf.

She found Nyla exactly where she expected to, in the low, wide chamber the Under-Hold's residents called the Common, a repurposed cargo bay that had become the crawlspace's informal gathering hall, seated at a long table with three other organizers Aria recognized from previous visits, papers and handwritten ledgers spread between them in the careful, deliberate penmanship of people who had learned to keep their own records because the ship above them refused to keep those records for them.

"You made it," Nyla said, looking up, and something in her tired, drawn face eased at the sight of her. "I was starting to think the Lantern had eaten you."

"Not yet," Aria said, sliding into the empty seat beside her. "Though it's had a good try. How's the petition?"

Nyla's expression answered before her words did. "Dead in the water, for now. The full Council session got postponed twice this month, once for the emergency session on your discovery, once because half the Magistrates apparently couldn't focus on Registry law with a twelve-thousand-year-old warning sign sitting in their inbox. I don't blame them, exactly. But Tomas has been waiting four years for this, Ari, and every week it slips is another week he spends sorting scrap instead of training for something that actually uses what's in his head." She rubbed her eyes, a gesture Aria recognized from years of watching her friend push herself past reasonable limits. "It's not just Tomas, either. There's a rumor going around down here, and I want to know if there's any truth to it before I let it spread further, because if it's true, it changes everything about how we push this petition forward."

"What rumor?"

"That some of the Preservationist bloc up top are starting to talk about the Under-Hold specifically, in connection with the Lantern." Nyla's voice had gone careful, watchful, the tone she used when she was testing whether a piece of information was safe to trust. "Something about how the Unlisted population, being technically unregistered and therefore not entitled to a Council vote, could be quietly relocated or restricted in movement if the Council ever declared a formal emergency footing, on the theory that unregistered residents represent an unaccounted variable in any evacuation or defense planning. I heard it secondhand, from someone who heard it from an aide who works two decks up from your Speaker's office. I don't know if it's real policy talk or just fear finding a convenient target, the way fear always seems to find its way to blaming the people with the least power to fight back."

Aria felt something cold and furious settle into her chest. "I haven't heard anything like that in the working group. But I also haven't been in every room where the Council actually talks. I'll ask Oskar. He'd know if Deck security's been given new orders about the Under-Hold."

"I'd appreciate that." Nyla was quiet for a moment, turning a stylus over in her fingers, and when she spoke again, her voice had softened into something more personal, less organizer and more oldest friend. "You look exhausted, Ari. More than exhausted. What's actually happening up there? The real version, not the sanitized Council briefing version half this ship's already memorized from the information campaign."

Aria hesitated, the old habit of caution warring with the deeper, older habit of trusting Nyla with everything that actually mattered, and found, as she usually did, that the second habit won. She told her, quietly, in the low hum of the Common's improvised lighting, everything Wren had shared in the Deck Two office: the boundary that unmade whatever it touched, the twelve-thousand-year-old warning, the Lantern's apparent purpose as a waystation built to outlast its makers, the ninety-day clock ticking down toward a decision that might reshape the fate of everyone aboard, Listed and Unlisted alike.

Nyla listened without interrupting, her sharp eyes growing steadily more distant as the scale of it settled over her, and when Aria finished, she sat quietly for a long moment before speaking. "You know what strikes me hardest about all that? It's not even the size of it, though God knows that's enough to keep anyone up at night. It's that whoever built that thing, whatever they were, they didn't just die out quietly and disappear. They spent their last effort, whatever resources they had left, building something meant to help whoever came after them. A warning. A gift, maybe, if what your Wren's been decoding is right. That's not nothing, Ari. That's not the behavior of something that stops being worth learning from just because it's terrifying." She turned the stylus over again, thoughtful. "You know what the Under-Hold's whole history is, really, underneath all the politics and the Registry fights? It's people the ship above decided weren't worth counting, who kept building anyway, kept teaching their children anyway, kept a whole world alive down here because they refused to just disappear quietly the way the Council's neglect wanted them to. Maybe that's not so different from whoever built your Lantern. Maybe that's worth remembering, when your Council starts arguing about whether it's safer to just look away."

Aria found, listening to her, that something in her chest that had been tight and cold since Wren's first mention of the boundary's advance eased, just slightly, at the unexpected resonance of her friend's words. "I'll remember that," she said. "I mean it."

"Good." Nyla managed a tired smile. "Now, selfishly, before you get pulled back into saving the ship from ancient cosmic horrors: I need you to actually sign the petition, formally, with your Hold credentials attached, not just tell me you support it in a commissary. We're trying to get forty Listed signatures alongside the full Under-Hold roll before we resubmit for the next open session, whenever that ends up being. Every name matters, especially names from decks the Council actually has to answer to."

"Give me the terminal," Aria said, and signed it there, in the low warm light of the Common, her name added to a list of forty Listed residents willing to stand publicly beside three generations of people the ship above had spent a century and a half pretending did not exist.

She stayed another hour, sharing a simple meal with Nyla and the other organizers, listening to the easy, familiar rhythms of the Common's evening business, children chasing each other between the support pillars, an old woman singing something low and wordless while she mended clothes by lamplight, and found, for the first time in weeks, a version of quiet that had nothing to do with Council secrecy and everything to do with simply being, for an hour, somewhere that felt like home.

It was nearly ship-midnight by the time she climbed back up through the maintenance hatch onto the official decks, and she was halfway back to her bunkroom, tired and thoughtful, turning over Nyla's words about civilizations that refused to disappear quietly, when her handset chimed with a priority alert that drove every warm, unhurried thought from her mind at once.

It was from Oskar Delane, timestamped four minutes earlier, three words that carried, in their sheer brevity, more urgency than any message she had received since this whole strange season had begun.

Deck Two office. Now.
