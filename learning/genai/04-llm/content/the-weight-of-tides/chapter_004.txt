Chapter 4: The Other Papers

Dottie's temporary home with Judith Pemberton was a Victorian on Elm Street, one of the grand old houses that testified to Willowport's nineteenth-century prosperity. Claire climbed the porch steps with Maya beside her, feeling like a child summoned to explain herself.

Judith answered the door, a tiny woman with steel-gray hair and sharp eyes. "Claire Merritt. About time you came home for more than funerals."

"Hello, Mrs. Pemberton."

"It's Judith now. We're both adults. Come in. Your mother's in the sitting room."

They followed Judith through rooms filled with antique furniture and family photographs spanning generations. Willowport history documented in faces and fashions, the town's gradual evolution from whaling port to fishing village to tourist destination.

Dottie sat in a wing-backed chair, a banker's box at her feet. She looked tired, older than her seventy-three years, worn down by grief and secrets.

"Thank you for coming," she said. "Judith, would you mind...?"

"I'll make tea. And keep Maya company in the kitchen, if that's all right. I have cookies, dear. Judith looked at Maya expectantly."

Maya glanced at Claire, who nodded. They disappeared, leaving Claire alone with her mother.

"Sit down," Dottie said. "This is going to take a while."

Claire sat, eyeing the box. "What's in there?"

"Family history. The parts Tom never wanted you to know while he was alive, but left instructions for me to share after his death. He was protecting you, Claire. From the truth, from danger, from responsibilities he didn't think you should have to carry. But now you've stood up at a town meeting and committed to investigating exactly what he spent forty years trying to understand. So you need to know everything."

"You're scaring me."

"Good. You should be scared." Dottie pulled a leather journal from the box, old and water-stained. "This belonged to your great-great-grandfather, Samuel Merritt. He was a whaling captain in the 1860s. Read the entry marked July 19, 1867."

Claire opened the journal carefully. The handwriting was archaic, difficult to parse, but she managed:

*Encountered something off Georges Bank today that defies explanation. Crew spotted what they thought was a whale, but it moved unlike any whale I've seen in thirty years at sea. Dove deep when we approached, leaving the water roiling and our compass spinning. Temperature dropped noticeably. Several men swear they saw lights in the depths, though I saw only disturbed water. Have ordered crew to silence on this matter. Superstitious talk is bad for morale.*

Claire looked up. "He saw something."

"The Merritts have been seeing something for five generations. Always the same area, always the deep water off Whitehead Island. Always strange temperature drops, unusual behavior, lights in the water." Dottie pulled out more documents. "Your great-grandfather wrote about it. Your grandfather kept notes. Tom documented it most thoroughly, but he wasn't the first."

"Why didn't anyone talk about it? Go public?"

"Some did. They were dismissed as drunks or liars or crazy. This is a fishing town, Claire. Reputation is everything. Men who told strange stories couldn't get crew. Couldn't sell their catch. Couldn't be trusted with valuable equipment. So most kept quiet. Logged what they saw privately, passed the knowledge down through families, waited for someone to understand."

Claire felt reality shifting, the ground becoming unstable. "What do you think it is? Really?"

"I don't know. Tom had theories. Some scientific, some... less so. But whatever it is, it's been in these waters for at least 160 years, probably longer. And it's connected to the ecological changes. Tom was certain of that."

Dottie pulled out a folder of scientific papers, modern printouts. "Tom taught himself oceanography, marine biology, climate science. Ordered textbooks, took online courses, corresponded with researchers. He understood more than most people realized. And he found patterns."

The papers were annotated in her father's handwriting. Studies of deep-sea life, bioluminescence, thermal vents, undiscovered species. Research on unexplained sonar readings, mysterious ocean sounds, objects that appeared on naval sonar but couldn't be identified. Her father had been trying to understand scientifically what his ancestors had recorded as wonder and fear.

"The developers know," Dottie said quietly.

Claire's head snapped up. "What?"

"Not everything. But they know there's something unusual about this stretch of water. That's why they want to build here specifically. Seabrook Development isn't really a development company. It's a front for a consortium of investors interested in marine resources. Energy, minerals, genetic patents from deep-sea organisms. They've been buying up coastal land wherever there are reports of unusual marine activity."

"That's..." Claire struggled for words. "That's conspiracy theory thinking."

"Is it? Tom got hold of their internal documents. He had help from someone inside the company, someone with a conscience. The development proposal is real, but it's a cover. What they really want is unrestricted water access, the ability to conduct deep-sea exploration and extraction without oversight. The condos and hotels are just to establish legal claim to the waters offshore."

Dottie handed Claire a flash drive. "It's all on here. Emails, financial records, exploration permits filed under subsidiary names. Tom was going to give it to federal investigators. Then he died."

"Mom, this is... if this is true, it's massive. It's fraud, possibly corruption. It's—"

"It's why Tom died, maybe. I can't prove that. His doctor said it was a heart attack, stress-induced, exactly what you'd expect from a man who'd already had two cardiac events and refused to slow down. But the timing was convenient for Seabrook. Very convenient."

Claire felt cold. "You think they killed him?"

"I think they wanted him dead, and he accommodated them by dying of natural causes. But Claire, these are serious people with serious money. Standing up to them is dangerous. Tom knew that. He tried to keep you away from it. But you stood up at that town meeting anyway, and now you're in it whether you want to be or not."

"I can't just back down. I committed to an environmental assessment."

"I know. Tom knew you wouldn't back down either, once you were engaged. That's why he left everything to you. The evidence, the responsibility, the danger. He trusted you to be smarter than he was, more careful. To use your credentials and institutional backing to do what he couldn't."

Claire looked at the documents spread around her, five generations of Merritt observations. Her family had been guardians of a mystery, watchers of something that shouldn't exist but did. And now that responsibility had fallen to her.

"What do you want me to do?"

"The environmental assessment. But do it properly, with full documentation and public transparency. Make copies of everything, store them with multiple people. Work with Maya's generation, people who can use social media and digital documentation. Make it impossible for you to disappear or for your work to be suppressed."

"You really think they'd try to suppress it?"

"Tom thought so. And whatever else your father was, he wasn't paranoid. He was careful, methodical, evidence-based. If he thought there was danger, there was danger."

Maya appeared in the doorway, Judith behind her. "Sorry to interrupt, but I've been showing Maya the Pemberton family archives. We have something you should see."

They followed Judith to a library lined with books and document boxes. She pulled down a leather-bound volume, opened it to a marked page.

"Judith's ancestor, Captain William Pemberton, commanded a fishing fleet in the 1880s," Maya explained. "He recorded an encounter with something unusual. Same area, same description. But there's more. He drew it."

Claire looked at the drawing: crude but clear. Something large and curved, covered in irregular protrusions, breaking the surface of the water. Lights visible through what might be windows or bioluminescent organs. Scale notations suggesting something massive, possibly several hundred feet long.

"That's not a whale," Claire said.

"No," Judith agreed. "William Pemberton was an experienced seaman. He knew whales. Whatever he drew, it wasn't any known marine life."

"How many families in Willowport have records like this?" Claire asked.

"Most of the old fishing families," Judith said. "We don't talk about it much. Tradition of silence, or maybe shame. But the records exist. I've been the town historian for thirty years. I've seen dozens of accounts, going back to the earliest colonial records. The Wampanoag called this area 'the place where the spirit swims.' They had legends about something that lived in the deep water, something old and powerful that shouldn't be disturbed."

Claire felt the pieces shifting into place. Not a single anomalous event, but a pattern spanning centuries. Something that had always been here, documented but dismissed, observed but never seriously investigated.

"I need to see all the records," she said. "Every account, every drawing, every mention. I need to compile a complete historical record."

"I can help with that," Judith said. "So can Maya. She's been quite helpful already, actually. Very organized."

Maya grinned. "I like research. And mysteries. This is way better than AP exams."

They spent the next two hours reviewing documents. Claire photographed pages, took notes, began assembling a timeline. The encounters clustered around certain dates, certain conditions. July and early August, usually. Clear nights with calm seas. Always in the deep water off Whitehead Island. Always the same elements: temperature drops, strange lights, something large moving beneath the surface.

By early afternoon, Claire had compiled 147 documented observations spanning 310 years. The pattern was undeniable. Something was in these waters, had been for centuries, and appeared regularly enough to be studied scientifically if anyone had the courage and resources to try.

"I need to talk to some people," Claire said. "Marine biologists who specialize in deep-sea life. Oceanographers who've studied unexplained phenomena. I need equipment, funding, institutional support."

"Tom tried that," Dottie said gently. "He reached out to dozens of researchers. Most ignored him. A few were interested but couldn't get funding or approval to investigate. Academia doesn't reward studying things that probably aren't there."

"But I'm already in academia. I have credentials, publications, grant-writing experience. And I have 310 years of historical documentation. That's not nothing."

"It's not enough either," Dottie said. "Not without physical evidence. Tom knew that. He spent his last years trying to get a clear photograph, a tissue sample, irrefutable proof. He never succeeded."

Claire thought about her father, aging and obsessed, spending his declining years chasing something that eluded him. She felt a wash of grief and admiration.

"I'll succeed where he couldn't. I have better technology, better training, better resources. And I have Maya's generation, people who grew up with documentation tools in their pockets."

"We should set up a website," Maya said suddenly. "Crowdsource the investigation. Ask people to submit sightings, photographs, data. Make it a community science project."

"The developers would attack it as amateur nonsense."

"So we make it professional. You're the lead scientist. Judith is the historian. Jake can represent the lobster fleet. We document everything to academic standards, make the data publicly available, invite peer review. It's harder to suppress something when thousands of people have copies."

Claire looked at her daughter with fresh appreciation. "That's actually brilliant."

"I know. I'm going to study environmental policy in college. This is like a practice run."

They left Judith's house with boxes of documents and a plan forming. Claire felt energized despite the exhaustion, purpose clarifying through the fear. This was real work, important work, work that connected her to her father's legacy and her family's history.

"Are you okay?" Maya asked as they drove home.

"I don't know. This is bigger than I thought. More dangerous maybe."

"But important."

"Yes. Important."

"Then we do it anyway. That's what Grandpa would say. That's what you taught me, actually. Do the work that matters, even when it's hard."

Claire reached over and squeezed her daughter's hand. "When did you become the wise one?"

"I've been paying attention. You're a good teacher, Mom. Even when you don't realize you're teaching."

They spent the evening organizing documents, setting up file systems, preparing for the work ahead. Claire reached out to colleagues, carefully explaining her project: historical analysis of long-term ecological data in a coastal Maine ecosystem. She didn't mention mysteries or anomalies, just solid science and interesting patterns.

Three colleagues responded positively. One offered to consult on statistical analysis. Another suggested equipment suppliers who might donate instruments for a community science project. The third mentioned a small grant program for rapid-response environmental assessments.

By midnight, Claire had the framework of a legitimate research project. Sixty days to collect data, analyze patterns, and prepare a comprehensive report. Sixty days to prove or disprove her father's life work. Sixty days to discover what swam in the deep water off Willowport, and whether it mattered to anyone beyond her family's long obsession.

She fell asleep at her father's desk, head on her arms, surrounded by 310 years of careful observation. In her dreams, she swam through dark water, following lights that receded ahead of her, always just beyond reach. Her father's voice echoed through the depths: Keep swimming. Don't stop. The truth is down here, if you're brave enough to find it.

She woke at dawn to Maya shaking her shoulder.

"Mom. You need to see this."

Claire followed her daughter to the window. Below, in the driveway, someone had spray-painted a message across her car windshield:

LEAVE IT ALONE

"Should we call the police?" Maya asked.

Claire stared at the warning, feeling anger cut through fear. "No. We should document it, photograph it, and then clean it off. They're trying to scare us. That means we're onto something real."

"Or that we're in danger."

"Maybe both." Claire took out her phone, started photographing. "Your grandmother was right. We need to make everything public, multiple copies, maximum transparency. Let's start a blog, post everything we find. Make it impossible for them to silence us without everyone knowing."

Maya smiled fiercely. "Now you're thinking like Grandpa."

"No," Claire said, photographing the threat from multiple angles. "I'm thinking like a scientist who's been pushed too far. They picked the wrong Merritt to intimidate."

She finished documenting, then went outside to clean the windshield. The paint came off easily, suggesting it had been applied recently, probably during the night. Whoever did this wanted to frighten without causing permanent damage. A warning, not an attack.

Claire looked toward the harbor, where the first boats were heading out into morning waters. Somewhere out there, beneath the surface, mysteries waited. Her father had died trying to understand them. She wouldn't make the same mistake. She would understand, and she would survive, and she would make sure the truth survived with her.

The weight of tides had pulled her home. Now she would ride those tides wherever they led, into deep water and deeper truth, following her father's path but carrying better lights to pierce the darkness.
