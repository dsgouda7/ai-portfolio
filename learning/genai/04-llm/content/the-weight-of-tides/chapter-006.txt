Chapter 6: The Community Response

The blog launched at dawn. Maya had worked through the night designing the site: clean, professional, scientifically rigorous. "Willowport Water Watch" featured Claire's credentials prominently, included peer-reviewed methodology, and presented findings with careful academic language.

The first post was simple: announcement of a sixty-day community science project to assess environmental changes in local waters. Claire included background on her father's forty-year dataset, acknowledgment of other historical observations, and invitation for community participation. She deliberately avoided mention of the unusual footage. That would come later, after proper verification.

By noon, the site had three hundred visitors. By evening, over two thousand. Someone had shared it on regional news networks and marine science forums. Comments poured in, mostly supportive, some skeptical, a few openly hostile.

"You're getting attention," Maya said, monitoring traffic. "Marine biology communities are picking this up. And look, there's a newspaper reporter asking for an interview."

Claire read the message from Angela Reyes, environmental correspondent for the Portland Press Herald. "Professional interest in your research project. Would like to discuss historical observations and current findings. No sensationalism, just science."

"Should I respond?"

"Absolutely. Legitimate news coverage gives us credibility. Plus it makes it harder for anyone to make us disappear." Maya grinned. "I'm learning to think strategically."

Claire scheduled the interview for the following day. That night, at the weekly community dinner at the church hall, she faced Willowport residents directly.

The hall was packed, more people than usual. Word had spread about the blog, the research project, the implication that something unusual might be in the waters. Claire stood at the front with a projector, ready to present preliminary findings.

"Thank you all for coming," she began. "I know there's been speculation about what I'm investigating. I want to be transparent about my methods and findings so far."

She clicked through slides: her father's temperature data showing accelerating warming, charts of species migration, graphs of catch decline. Standard environmental data, carefully presented, making the case that something was changing in the ecosystem.

"These changes are consistent with global climate change patterns," she explained. "The Gulf of Maine is warming rapidly, causing species to move north or into deeper water. This affects the entire food chain, from plankton to whales, and obviously impacts the fishing industry."

"We know all that," someone called out. "What about the other stuff? The things your father saw?"

Claire took a breath. This was the moment of choice: stay safe with conventional science, or acknowledge the mystery.

"My father documented phenomena he couldn't explain. So have several generations of Willowport families. I've been compiling historical accounts of unusual observations in these waters. Lights, temperature anomalies, sonar readings that don't match known marine life. Individually, each account can be explained away. Collectively, they suggest a pattern worth investigating."

She clicked to the next slide: a map showing the concentration of reports around Whitehead Island over three centuries.

"This concentration is statistically significant. Something about this area, this particular stretch of water, generates unusual observations. It could be unique geology creating strange sonar echoes. It could be rare species we haven't properly documented. Or it could be something else entirely. My goal is to investigate systematically and report findings honestly."

"Have you seen anything yourself?" an elderly woman asked. Claire recognized her: Mrs. Winters, who had taught her high school English.

"Yes. I have sonar data and visual footage that I'm currently analyzing with experts from Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution. When we've completed proper verification, I'll share those findings."

The room buzzed with excitement and speculation. Claire held up her hands for quiet.

"I need to emphasize: I'm conducting legitimate science, not chasing sea monsters. Whatever is generating these observations has a rational explanation. My job is to discover what that explanation is. To do that, I need community support. I need sightings reported to the blog. I need water access for sampling. And I need patience while we work through the data properly."

Richard Caldwell stood from the back of the room. Claire's stomach tightened.

"Dr. Merritt, don't you think this mysterious phenomena investigation might undermine the credibility of your environmental assessment? The town council is expecting objective analysis of development impacts, not speculation about underwater anomalies."

"Mr. Caldwell, I'm documenting what's in the water. If there are unusual phenomena affecting the ecosystem, that's relevant to any development proposal. Ignoring observed reality because it's inconvenient wouldn't be objective science."

"But your father's observations, the historical accounts you mention, they're all anecdotal. Unverified. Building an assessment on anecdotal evidence is exactly the kind of bias I'm concerned about."

Claire felt anger rising but kept her voice level. "Which is why I'm seeking verification from established institutions and following rigorous methodology. My father's data may have started as anecdotal observations, but forty years of consistent measurements is actually a quite valuable dataset. And historical accounts from independent sources showing consistent patterns are worth investigating, not dismissing."

"I'm simply concerned that the town council and residents understand the difference between proven science and speculation."

"As am I, Mr. Caldwell. That's why everything I publish will clearly distinguish between verified data, preliminary findings, and areas requiring further study. Can Seabrook's environmental consultants say the same?"

The room murmured approval. Caldwell's expression hardened, but he sat down without responding.

After the presentation, people crowded around Claire. Some shared their own stories: strange sounds heard while fishing at night, unusual water colors, equipment failures that couldn't be explained. Claire took notes, collected contact information, began building a database of observations.

Jake appeared at her elbow. "That was well handled. Caldwell tried to undermine you, and you flipped it back on him."

"I'm tired of being defensive. We're doing good science. Let him prove otherwise."

"Just be careful. He's used to winning."

"So am I."

The next morning, Angela Reyes arrived at the house with a photographer. She was younger than Claire expected, maybe thirty, with sharp eyes and a recording device that she placed on the table with explicit permission.

"I appreciate you talking to me," Angela said. "I've been covering coastal development and environmental issues for five years. I know who the major players are, and I know when something doesn't smell right. Seabrook's proposal has been bothering me."

"Why?"

"Too much money for a marginal market. Willowport's nice, but it's not Bar Harbor or Camden. Building luxury condos here doesn't make financial sense unless there's another angle. When I heard about your father's research and your investigation, things started clicking."

Claire made a decision. "I'm going to show you something. It's not public yet because we're still verifying. But I think you need to see it."

She showed Angela the underwater footage. The reporter watched in silence, then asked to see it again. And again.

"That's not a whale," Angela said finally.

"No."

"That's not any known marine life."

"Correct."

"What is it?"

"I don't know. I'm working with Woods Hole to analyze it. But Angela, this stays off the record until we have verification. I'm showing you because I trust that once this goes public, we'll need good journalists asking the right questions. Not sensationalists chasing sea monster stories."

"Understood. But Claire, when this does go public, it's going to explode. This is either evidence of unknown species, classified military tech, or something even stranger. Any of those stories is huge."

"I know. That's why I need careful handling, not tabloid speculation."

Angela agreed to hold the story until Claire had academic verification, but to prepare a comprehensive piece that could run immediately when the embargo lifted. In exchange, Claire would give her exclusive first access to findings.

"I should warn you," Angela said as she was leaving. "I've been investigating Seabrook Development. They're connected to some interesting investors. Energy companies, deep-sea mining interests, even some defense contractors. Whatever they really want in Willowport, it's not just condo profits."

"Do you have proof?"

"Corporate ownership records, mostly. It's all technically legal, just tangled in shell companies and subsidiaries. But the pattern is clear: they buy coastal land wherever there are reports of unusual marine activity. Willowport is the seventh site they've targeted in five years."

"Can you send me what you have?"

"Already emailing it. Be careful, Claire. These are people with serious resources."

After Angela left, Claire reviewed the corporate documents. The connections were there, obscured but traceable. Seabrook was a subsidiary of Meridian Ventures, which was partially owned by several energy companies and a defense contractor called Prometheus Systems. Prometheus specialized in underwater technology and ocean resource exploitation.

"They're not just developers," Claire told Maya. "They're resource extractors. They want access to whatever is in the water off Whitehead Island."

"Do they know what it is?"

"I think they suspect. Maybe they have their own evidence, their own sonar readings. Maybe they've been tracking this longer than we know."

That afternoon, Dr. Sarah Chen called with preliminary findings.

"I've shown your footage to three colleagues," Sarah said. "Marine engineers, biomechanics specialists, image analysis experts. Consensus is that it's genuine footage of an actual underwater object, not CGI or fakery. Size estimates put it between fifty and seventy feet long. Shape analysis shows geometric regularities inconsistent with natural forms. The lighting patterns appear to be electroluminescent, not bioluminescent."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning it's engineered. Built, not evolved. Whether it's human-built or something else, we can't determine from this footage alone. But Claire, this is significant. We need to get more evidence, better images, maybe even physical samples."

"I'm going back out tomorrow. Sonar, cameras, better equipment this time. Jake and Vernon have been preparing."

"I'd like to join you if possible. MIT has offered access to some advanced imaging equipment. We could get much higher resolution."

They scheduled the dive for three days out, enough time for Sarah to arrange equipment and travel. Claire felt momentum building. Real scientists, institutional support, verifiable methodology. Her father's lonely obsession was becoming legitimate research.

That night, she reviewed his later journals, the ones from his final years. His handwriting was shakier, his entries more philosophical.

*July 15, 2019: Another sighting today. It stays deep now, as if aware of increased human activity. Or perhaps the warming water drives it to colder depths. I wonder if future generations will know it was here, if anyone will remember the old fishermen's stories. Will Claire ever forgive me for pushing her away? I did it to protect her, but maybe I was wrong. Maybe she needed to know, needed to be part of this mystery. It's her inheritance, after all. The Merritts have watched these waters for generations. Someone has to keep watching.*

Claire felt tears on her cheeks. He had wanted to tell her. Had struggled with the decision to keep her away. She wished she could tell him she understood, that she'd come home, that she was continuing his work.

Maya found her crying in the study. "Mom?"

"I'm okay. Just reading Grandpa's journals. Wishing I'd talked to him more. Wishing I hadn't stayed away so long."

"He knew you loved him. I could tell from his letters to me. He was proud of you, proud that you became a real scientist with credentials and respect. He said you could finish what he started because you had the tools he didn't."

"He wrote that?"

"His last letter to me. Three weeks before he died." Maya pulled out her phone, found the email. "Here: 'Maya, your mother is brilliant and brave and more capable than she knows. When I'm gone, she'll come home. When she does, show her everything. Help her understand. Together, you'll solve the mystery that's consumed my life. And she'll do it properly, the way a trained scientist should. I'm proud of her, even if I never told her enough. Make sure she knows.'"

Claire read the words through blurred vision. Her father had known he was dying. Had made plans for her return. Had trusted her to continue his work even though she'd been absent for years.

"I won't let him down," she said.

"I know. That's why you're kind of scary right now. Obsessed scientist mode."

Claire laughed despite the tears. "Is it that obvious?"

"Mom, you've worked until midnight every night for a week. You talk about water temperatures in your sleep. Yes, it's obvious."

"I'll try to be less obsessed."

"Don't. Just be careful too. Obsessed is fine. Dead is not."

They ordered pizza, watched a movie, pretended to be normal for a few hours. But Claire's mind kept returning to the upcoming dive, the enhanced equipment, the possibility of better evidence. She was close to something. She could feel it, the way she imagined her father had felt each time he went out on the water, hoping this would be the day he'd finally capture irrefutable proof.

Her phone buzzed. Unknown number again: "Final warning. Stop investigating or face consequences."

Claire screenshot it, added it to her growing file of threats, and texted Jake: "I think we should have someone else on the boat next dive. Someone who can serve as an additional witness and, frankly, security."

Jake responded immediately: "Already arranged. Harbor patrol will escort us. Official capacity, environmental research support. Plus I've invited Danny's brother, who's former Coast Guard and doesn't trust developers. We'll have plenty of eyes."

Claire felt grateful for Jake's protectiveness, even as she chafed at needing it. She was a scientist conducting legitimate research. The fact that she needed security said everything about what they were up against.

Outside, Willowport settled into night. The harbor lights reflected on dark water, the town peaceful in its innocence. Most people had no idea what was happening beneath the surface, what mysteries waited in the deep water, what forces were aligning around their small town.

But Claire knew. Her father had known. And now it was her responsibility to bring that knowledge into light, to protect what he'd died fighting for, to finish the work that had consumed five generations of Merritts.

Three days until the next dive. Three days to prepare, to gather equipment, to steel herself for whatever they might find. Three days until she followed her father's path into deep water, armed with better tools but the same questions that had haunted him for forty years.

What swam in the darkness off Willowport? Who built it? Why was it here? And what would happen when the world finally learned the truth?

The weight of tides had pulled her home. Soon, very soon, those tides would carry her into the depths, and she would discover what waited there. Her father's ghost seemed to hover in the study, watching, waiting, believing in her as he always had.

Claire returned to her work, analyzing data, preparing equipment lists, building the case that would either validate her father's life work or prove him wrong. Either way, she would know. That was what science demanded: truth, regardless of what you hoped to find.

Outside, the tide was rising. Inside, Claire Merritt prepared to dive deeper than her father ever had, into waters that held secrets centuries old. She was frightened and exhilarated in equal measure, aware that she was approaching a threshold beyond which everything would change.

Three days. She could wait three days. But barely.
