Chapter 3: Lessons in Resonance

Davin's tide-garden looked different in the morning light. The plants that had glowed softly in the darkness now seemed almost ordinary, though Kerra could sense the power thrumming through them. Each one a different frequency, a different voice in the great symphony of the tides.

"Good, you're early," Davin called from the center of the garden, where he knelt beside the petrified coral tree. "That suggests either enthusiasm or terror. With resonants, it's usually both."

"Both sounds about right," Kerra admitted, stepping carefully along the wooden walkway that wound between the planted sections. Up close, she could see that each bed was separated from its neighbors by lines of salt and crushed shell, physical barriers that Davin had clearly reinforced with weavings she couldn't quite perceive.

"The barriers keep the tides from bleeding into each other," Davin explained, reading her expression. "A water-tide plant growing next to a flame-tide plant would normally interfere with each other's resonance. The salt and shell create neutral ground, and the weavings I've laid reinforce that separation. You'll need to learn to do something similar with your own perceptions."

"Maren said you'd teach me to focus on one tide at a time."

"Eventually, yes. But first, you need to understand what you're focusing on. Come here." Davin gestured to a small pool of water fed by a bamboo pipe that carried seawater up from below. "Tell me what you sense."

Kerra approached the pool and closed her eyes, reaching out with that new awareness that had awakened when the pillar rose. The water-tide sang to her immediately, strong and clear, filled with motion and depth and the memory of waves. But tangled within it were other notes—stone from the pipe, wind in the air above the pool's surface, even a hint of flame from the sun's heat.

"I feel water, mostly," she said slowly. "But the other tides are mixed in. I don't think they can be fully separated."

"Correct!" Davin looked pleased. "The tides are not truly separate forces. They're aspects of a single underlying reality, divided by human perception and bound by human magic. A resonant like you perceives that unity more clearly than single-tide weavers. That's both your strength and your challenge."

"Because the Hollow King perceived it too clearly?"

"The Hollow King tried to control the unity directly, to wield all five tides as a single overwhelming force. For a time, he succeeded. But that kind of power doesn't just flow through a person—it consumes them. The human mind isn't meant to channel that much raw force." Davin moved to another section of the garden, where silver-leaved vines grew in intricate spirals. "Your task is different. You must learn to work with each tide individually while maintaining awareness of the whole. Like a musician playing a five-stringed instrument—you can pluck one string at a time, even though all five resonate together."

He gestured to the vines. "These respond to wind-tide. Touch them and try to call only that aspect of your awareness. Ignore water, stone, flame, and void. Focus solely on wind."

Kerra reached out tentatively and brushed her fingers against the silver leaves. They trembled at her touch, and she felt the wind-tide immediately—the same sweeping, soaring sensation she'd felt on the Windskimmer, multiplied a thousandfold. But with it came water, the moisture in the air, and stone, the soil anchoring the vines' roots.

"I can feel wind, but the others are still there," she said, frustration creeping into her voice.

"Of course they are. They always will be. The goal isn't to eliminate them completely but to move them to the background of your awareness. Think of it like looking at a painting. You can focus on a single figure without forgetting the rest of the canvas exists."

Kerra tried again, this time consciously acknowledging the other tides before deliberately shifting her attention to wind alone. It was like squinting her inner eye, narrowing her focus until the wind-tide filled her perception and the others became distant murmurs.

The vines responded, their trembling becoming a purposeful dance as they swayed in patterns that had nothing to do with the actual breeze.

"Good!" Davin's encouragement made her grin despite the headache building behind her eyes. "Hold it for a count of ten, then release slowly."

Kerra counted, fighting to maintain her focus even as the other tides pressed at the edges of her awareness like waves against a dam. When she reached ten, she let go, and the vines settled back into their natural state.

"That was harder than it should have been," she panted.

"You're trying to do in minutes what takes most weavers years to develop," Davin said. "But you don't have years. None of us do. So we work with what time we have."

They spent the next three hours cycling through the different sections of the garden. Stone-tide with the petrified coral tree, its deep enduring presence like mountains sleeping beneath the sea. Flame-tide with flowers that opened and closed in response to heat, their petals carrying the consuming hunger of fire. Water-tide with the pool, teaching Kerra to feel the difference between surface motion and deep currents.

And finally, reluctantly, void-tide.

"There are no plants that resonate purely with void," Davin explained as they stood in the center of the garden, in a small circular space where no plants grew at all. "Void is the absence that makes presence possible. It's the silence between sounds, the darkness that defines light. Most weavers never perceive it at all because it has no substance to grasp."

"But I feel it," Kerra said. "Like the space between all the other tides."

"Which is why you're dangerous. A true void-weaver doesn't create or destroy—they manipulate boundaries. They can make water become steam, or steam become rain, by adjusting the spaces where elements transform. In theory, a powerful enough void-weaver could unmake reality itself by removing the boundaries that hold it together."

"That's what the Hollow King did."

"That's what he tried to do. The legends say he wanted to dissolve all boundaries, to return the world to some primordial unified state where he would be one with everything. Madness, obviously. But powerful madness that nearly succeeded."

Kerra shivered despite the warm morning sun. "And you think I could become like him?"

"I think you could if you tried to wield all five tides simultaneously as he did. But if you can learn to work with them individually, to respect the boundaries even as you perceive their artificial nature, then you might be the first resonant in history to avoid that fate." Davin's expression was grave. "It won't be easy. The power will call to you, tempting you to reach for more and more until you lose yourself in it. You'll need an anchor, something human to hold onto when the tides threaten to sweep you away."

"Like what?"

"That's for you to discover. For some weavers, it's love. For others, duty or fear or simple stubbornness. Whatever it is, find it and guard it fiercely. Because the moment you forget your humanity is the moment you become the monster you're trying to prevent."

They broke for lunch, sharing bread and dried fish while sitting on the edge of Davin's platform with their feet dangling over the water. Below, Kerra could see Deepwater weavers swimming between the submerged levels of the Enclaves, their adapted bodies moving with graceful efficiency.

"Do the Deepwater folk know about resonants?" she asked.

"Some of their eldest might remember the stories, but they're as eager as anyone to forget that era. The rise of the Hollow King nearly destroyed Vaetheris. When he was finally sealed, the surviving weavers swore they'd never let such power concentrate in one person again. Hence the binding ceremony and the careful division of territories between the Enclaves and Holds."

"Divide and control."

"Exactly. It's kept the peace for centuries, more or less. But it's also made us weaker. The old weavers, before the division, could work together in ways we've forgotten. That's what it took to seal the Hollow King—resonants working in concert, each channeling different aspects of the tides in perfect harmony."

"You said resonants, plural. There were others?"

"According to the records Davin managed to save before his expulsion, yes. Five of them, one for each tide-affinity, though all were capable of sensing the others. They formed the first binding circle and created the seal in the Abyssal Rift. But the effort killed three of them, and the survivors made sure no more resonants would be born by establishing the forced binding ceremony." Davin tossed a bread crumb into the water and watched it sink. "They saw themselves as monsters. Perhaps they were right."

"Or perhaps they were just people doing the best they could with impossible circumstances," Kerra countered. "Isn't that what we all are?"

Davin smiled faintly. "Wise words for someone so young. But yes, you're right. Monsters are rarely born—they're made, usually by fear and desperation and the belief that the ends justify the means."

They resumed training after lunch, and Davin introduced more complex exercises. He had Kerra try to sense the tides at greater distances, reaching out beyond the garden to feel the pulse of the city around them. Thousands of people, hundreds of weavers, all drawing on the tide-veins in their daily work. It was overwhelming at first, a cacophony of overlapping resonances that made Kerra's head spin.

"Filter," Davin instructed. "You don't need to hear every individual note. Listen for the patterns, the currents running beneath the surface noise."

Kerra tried, struggling to impose order on the chaos. Gradually, she began to perceive structure—water-weavers clustered in the lower Enclaves, their work creating a low, sustained hum. Wind-weavers on the platforms above, their magic coming in short bursts as they assisted with sail-rigging and cargo-lifting. Stone-weavers maintaining the coral pillars, their presence steady and deep like heartbeats.

And beneath it all, that strange hollowness that marked the tide-veins themselves, the channels through which all this power flowed. Kerra reached toward them instinctively, wanting to understand their structure, and—

Pain lanced through her skull like a white-hot blade. She gasped and reeled backward, her vision going dark at the edges.

"Breathe!" Davin's hands steadied her shoulders. "Don't try to touch the veins directly. They're not meant for individual weavers to access, especially not resonants. You'd burn yourself out in seconds."

Kerra gulped air, waiting for the pain to subside to a manageable throb. "What are the veins, exactly? I know they're sources of tide-power, but what creates them?"

"That's one of the great mysteries. Some scholars think they're remnants of the world's creation, channels carved by the first stirrings of elemental force. Others believe they're alive in some way, vast organisms beyond our comprehension. What we know for certain is that they exist, they can be accessed by trained weavers, and they're essential to all tide-based magic." Davin helped her to a sitting position. "They're also currently dying, which is our immediate problem."

"Someone's draining them. The Hollowed."

"Yes. But how and where, I don't know. The veins run throughout the archipelago, beneath the ocean floor and the island chains. There are thousands of miles of them. Finding the specific points where they're being sabotaged would take more resources than we have."

Kerra's mind raced despite her headache. "Unless we could track the damage back to its source. Like following a river upstream to find where it's being polluted."

"In theory, yes. But that would require sensing the veins across vast distances, distinguishing between natural weakening and deliberate sabotage, and doing so without getting caught by the authorities or alerting the Hollowed to our investigation." Davin shook his head. "Even a trained resonant would struggle with that task."

"But I'm not trained. Which means I might be able to do things trained weavers wouldn't try."

"Or you might kill yourself attempting the impossible."

"Better than waiting for the seal to break and the Hollow King to rise," Kerra shot back.

They stared at each other for a long moment. Finally, Davin sighed. "You sound like Maren. Stubborn to the point of madness." But there was approval in his voice. "Very well. We'll work toward that goal. But first, you need more basic control. The exercises we did today are just the beginning. You'll train every day, building your strength and precision until you can manipulate each tide independently without conscious effort."

"How long will that take?"

"For a normal weaver? Years. For you?" Davin shrugged. "Weeks, maybe months if we're lucky. Resonants learn faster because you're not fighting against your nature the way single-tide weavers do. But faster still isn't fast enough. We'll need every day we can get."

Kerra nodded, determination settling into her bones. "Then let's not waste time. What's next?"

What came next was pain.

Not the sharp, lancing pain of touching the tide-veins, but the deep, grinding ache of muscles worked beyond their endurance. Except these were mental muscles, the parts of her awareness that had to strain and stretch and sometimes tear to accommodate the tides flowing through them.

By the time the sun set, Kerra could barely stand. Her head pounded, her vision swam with phantom colors, and she was fairly certain she'd forgotten her own name at least twice. But she could also consistently call individual tides, hold them for counts of thirty or more, and transition between them without losing focus entirely.

Progress.

"Go," Davin said, though his own face was grey with exhaustion. Teaching resonance, it seemed, was almost as draining as learning it. "Eat something, sleep, and come back tomorrow. We'll start working on manipulating the tides rather than just sensing them."

"Manipulating? Like actual weaving?"

"Like actual weaving. You can sense all five tides, Kerra. It's time to learn to use them."

Kerra stumbled back to the Windskimmer as the city's evening lamps flickered to life, each one fueled by tiny wisps of flame-tide carefully maintained by dedicated weavers. The docks were quieter now, most sailors having retreated to taverns or ships for their evening meals. She found Captain Breslin in the galley, sharing a meal with Maren and a few other crew members.

"Valmont! You look like death. Sit down before you fall down."

Kerra obeyed gratefully, accepting the bowl of fish stew someone pressed into her hands. It tasted like ambrosia, or possibly she was just too tired to notice if it didn't. She ate mechanically, barely registering the conversation flowing around her.

"—more ships arriving every hour—"

"—Enclave's called an emergency council—"

"—Holds are mobilizing their generals—"

"—someone's going to start a war over this—"

War. The word cut through Kerra's exhaustion. She looked up sharply. "What's this about war?"

Breslin grimaced. "Rumors, mostly. But loud rumors. The Skyward Holds are blaming the Deepwater Enclaves for the tide-vein instability, saying their constant harvesting of water-tide has weakened the entire system. The Enclaves are countering that the Holds' wind-weaving is disrupting the natural flow patterns."

"Classic misdirection," Maren muttered. "Both factions pointing fingers while the real enemy works unseen."

"You think there's a real enemy?" one of the sailors asked. "Not just natural tide-vein decay?"

"I think nature doesn't move in patterns this consistent," Maren said carefully, clearly choosing her words to avoid revealing too much. "But what do I know? I'm just an old stone-weaver who fixed broken masts for forty years."

The conversation shifted to safer topics—cargo prices, weather predictions, gossip about various ships' crews. Kerra finished her stew and excused herself, retreating to her hammock with relief. Around her, the ship settled into its nighttime rhythms, familiar and comforting despite everything that had changed.

She should sleep. Her body desperately needed rest.

But Kerra found herself lowering her mental barriers again, reaching for the tides with the new precision Davin had taught her. Water first, isolating its frequency from the background noise. Then wind, then stone, then flame, cycling through each one and holding it for longer and longer periods.

And finally, void.

This was the tide that frightened her most, the one that had driven the Hollow King to madness. But it was also the one that felt most natural, most right, as if some part of her had always existed in those empty spaces between reality.

Kerra let herself drift in the void-tide, feeling the boundaries where it met the other tides. Here, where water became steam. There, where stone became sand. The places where transformation happened, where one thing became another.

She could shape those boundaries, she realized. Not dramatically, not yet, but she could feel her awareness brushing against them like fingers testing the edge of a blade. With practice, with power, she could make water boil with a thought or wind solidify into something almost stone-like.

The possibilities were intoxicating and terrifying in equal measure.

Kerra pulled back before she went too far, before the temptation to experiment became irresistible. Davin's warning echoed in her mind: find your anchor, something human to hold onto.

She thought of Captain Breslin, who'd given her a chance when no one else would. Of Maren, who'd guided her when she was lost. Of her brother Finn, who'd be fifteen now, probably getting ready for his own binding ceremony. Would he succeed where she had failed? Or was he a resonant too, hidden by the same ceremony that had marked her as unbound?

Family. Friendship. The small kindnesses that made life bearable.

Those were her anchors. Not power or glory or revenge, but the simple human connections that gave life meaning.

Kerra held that thought as she finally, mercifully, fell asleep.

Tomorrow she would learn to weave the tides. Tomorrow she would take her first real steps toward becoming what the world needed: not a monster, but a guardian.

If she was very, very lucky.
