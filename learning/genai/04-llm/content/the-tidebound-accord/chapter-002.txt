Chapter 2: Deepwater Crossing

Three days of hard sailing brought the Windskimmer to the outskirts of Deepwater Crossing just as the sun climbed toward its zenith. Kerra stood at her usual post, sextant in hand, but her attention was focused on the city rising from the sea ahead.

Deepwater Crossing was unlike any other settlement in Vaetheris. Built on massive floating platforms chained to the ocean floor by columns of living coral grown by Deepwater weavers, the city was a marvel of engineering and magic combined. Wooden walkways connected platforms of varying sizes, some supporting single buildings while others held entire districts. Ships of all sizes crowded the docks, their masts forming a forest of timber and canvas. And beneath it all, visible through sections of crystal-clear platform flooring, the Deepwater Enclaves themselves descended into the blue depths, their inhabitants moving through the water with the ease of fish.

Kerra had been here only twice before, both times before her failed binding. The sight of water-weavers moving through their native element with such grace had filled her with envy then. Now it just made her feel hollow.

"There's our berth," Captain Breslin called out, pointing to a dock on the eastern platforms. "Bring her in easy, boys. I don't want to pay for dock repairs."

The crew moved with practiced efficiency, furling sails and deploying the oars for the final approach. Kerra helped where she could, grateful for the physical labor that kept her mind occupied. Ever since Maren's revelation three nights ago, she'd been unable to shake the feeling of being watched, though by what she couldn't say.

The pillar hadn't risen again. The seas had been calm, almost unnaturally so. But Kerra kept feeling small tugs at the edge of her awareness, like someone plucking at threads she hadn't known she possessed. Water here, wind there, a resonance of stone from the coral pillars supporting the city. It was maddening and fascinating in equal measure.

They made dock without incident, and Breslin immediately headed off to find the harbormaster and report the pillar sighting. Protocol demanded that any unusual tide-vein activity be logged with the Crossing authorities, who would then alert the appropriate Enclave or Hold. Kerra suspected that three dozen other ships had already reported similar events in the past few days. The tide-veins were growing more unstable across the entire archipelago.

"Valmont." Maren appeared at her elbow, moving with that uncanny silence old sailors seemed to develop. "Walk with me."

It wasn't a request. Kerra followed the old woman off the ship and onto the crowded docks, weaving through sailors, merchants, and the occasional Deepwater envoy whose skin bore the faint blue-green tint that marked them as tide-adapted. Some wore breathing masks filled with water to keep their gills moist in the air; others simply endured the discomfort for however long their business required.

Maren led them away from the main commercial docks and into the quieter residential quarters, where platforms held small homes and gardens rather than warehouses and shops. Children played in the narrow walkways, their laughter carrying across the water. An old man sat mending fishing nets, his movements slow and meditative.

"There's someone you need to meet," Maren said quietly. "Someone who knows more about the old stories than I do."

"I thought you said I should keep quiet about what I felt."

"I said you should tell no one in authority. This person is very much not an authority." Maren's weathered face creased in what might have been a smile. "Though they were once, long ago, before they committed the unforgivable sin of asking too many questions."

They stopped before a small platform at the edge of the residential quarter, connected to its neighbors by a single narrow bridge. The house built there was modest, its walls showing signs of salt damage and age. A garden of some kind occupied most of the platform's remaining space, though the plants growing there were unlike any Kerra had seen before—silver-leafed vines that seemed to shimmer with their own internal light, flowers that opened and closed in patterns that had nothing to do with the sun, and a central tree whose bark appeared to be carved from petrified coral.

"A tide-garden," Kerra breathed. "I've only read about these."

"Watch."

Maren approached the garden's edge and placed her hand flat on the wooden planks. For a moment, nothing happened. Then the boards beneath her palm began to glow with a faint amber light, and the door to the house creaked open.

"Maren Stonetide." The voice that emerged from the doorway was male, cultured, and amused. "I wondered when you'd come calling. And you've brought a guest. How delightful."

The man who stepped out was perhaps sixty, though with tide-weavers it was always hard to tell. His skin had the faint grey cast of a stone-weaver, and his eyes were the color of ancient granite. He wore simple clothes, but they were well-made, and a silver pendant hung around his neck bearing a symbol Kerra recognized from her studies: the five-pointed star of a scholar, once worn by the tide-researchers of the old academies before the Enclaves and Holds had consolidated their power.

"This is Kerra Valmont," Maren said. "And she's got a problem that's about to become everyone's problem."

The man's gaze sharpened as it fell on Kerra. He stared at her for a long, uncomfortable moment, his expression shifting from curiosity to surprise to something that might have been fear.

"Impossible," he whispered. "The binding restrictions should have—" He caught himself, shaking his head. "Inside. Quickly. The walls here have ears, and some of them belong to people who wouldn't understand."

They followed him into the house, which was larger than it appeared from outside, its walls lined with bookshelves crammed with volumes both ancient and new. More tide-garden plants grew in pots throughout the space, their strange luminescence providing a soft, multicolored glow that replaced the need for lanterns. A large table dominated the center of the main room, its surface covered with charts, diagrams, and what appeared to be fragments of carved stone bearing the same spiral patterns Kerra had seen on the risen pillar.

"Kerra, this is Davin Shale," Maren said. "He was the Enclave's chief researcher on ancient tide-lore before he was expelled for heresy."

"Heresy is such a dramatic word," Davin said dryly, already pulling books from shelves and spreading them across the table. "I prefer 'inconvenient truth-telling.' Tell me, young Kerra Valmont, what exactly did you feel when the pillar rose?"

Kerra glanced at Maren, who nodded encouragingly. Taking a deep breath, she recounted the entire experience—the pull she'd felt, the sensation of all five tides responding at once, the name Hollow King that had simply appeared in her mind. As she spoke, Davin's expression grew increasingly grave.

"You're sure it was all five?" he pressed. "Not just multiple tides, but specifically all of them? Water, wind, stone, flame, and void?"

"Yes. Though the void felt different. Less like emptiness and more like..." Kerra struggled for words. "Like the spaces between notes in a song. Not nothing, but the pauses that make the melody make sense."

Davin and Maren exchanged a significant look.

"She's one of them," Davin said quietly. "A resonant. I thought they were just legend."

"What's a resonant?" Kerra asked.

Davin pulled a particularly ancient tome from one of his shelves and opened it carefully, revealing pages written in an archaic script that Kerra couldn't quite read. The illustrations, however, were clear enough: five figures arranged in a circle, with lines of energy connecting them to a central point.

"Before the binding ceremony was standardized, before the Enclaves and Holds divided the tides between them like property, there were weavers called resonants. Individuals born with the ability to sense and potentially manipulate all five tides simultaneously. They were rare—maybe one every few generations—but when they appeared, they were either celebrated as bridges between the factions or feared as potential Hollow Kings."

"The original Hollow King was a resonant," Maren added. "That's why the binding ceremony was created the way it was. To force each weaver to bond with only a single tide, ensuring no one could ever accumulate that much power again."

"But the ceremony doesn't always work," Davin said, gesturing to Kerra. "Or rather, it works exactly as designed when it encounters a true resonant. It refuses to bind them at all, because binding them to a single tide would be like trying to force a river to flow in only one direction. The result is what we call an unbound—someone who appears to have no tide affinity because they actually have all of them."

Kerra's legs felt weak. She found a chair and sank into it, her mind reeling. "So I'm not broken."

"Quite the opposite. You're extraordinarily rare and potentially the most powerful weaver alive." Davin closed the book with a soft thump. "You're also in tremendous danger. If the Enclave-masters or the Hold-generals learn what you are, they'll see you as either a weapon to be controlled or a threat to be eliminated. The old fear of the Hollow King runs deep."

"But I'm not him. I'm not trying to consume the tides or conquer anything. I just want to—" Kerra broke off, realizing she didn't actually know what she wanted. To belong, maybe. To not be ashamed anymore.

"It doesn't matter what you want," Maren said, not unkindly. "It matters what you are. And right now, with the tide-veins destabilizing and ancient pillars rising from the deep, what you are is the only person who might be able to stop what's coming."

"What is coming?" Kerra asked. "Maren mentioned the Hollow King, but you're talking about him like he's already sealed. So what's happening?"

Davin moved to the table and began sorting through the stone fragments, arranging them in a pattern that matched the illustrations in his book. "The Hollow King was sealed, yes, bound in the Abyssal Rift by the first tide-weavers working in concert. But seals require power to maintain, and that power comes from the tide-veins themselves. As long as the veins remain strong and stable, the seal holds. But if the veins weaken—"

"The seal weakens with them," Kerra finished, horror dawning. "And the pillars rising are part of the seal, aren't they? The wards are surfacing because they're failing."

"Precisely. And once enough of them fail, the Hollow King will be free to rise again. But here's the truly terrifying part—" Davin leaned forward, his granite eyes intense. "The tide-veins aren't weakening naturally. Something is draining them deliberately. Something that wants the seal to break."

The room fell silent except for the gentle chime of the tide-garden plants responding to an unfelt breeze.

"His followers," Maren said at last. "The Hollowed. I thought they were all dead."

"So did I," Davin admitted. "But I've been tracking the destabilizations for five years now, and the pattern is unmistakable. Someone is sabotaging the tide-veins at key junctures, weakening them just enough to avoid immediate notice but consistently enough to erode the seal. It's subtle, brilliant, and absolutely malevolent."

"Then we have to stop them," Kerra said. "Warn the Enclaves and Holds, organize a defense—"

"With what proof?" Davin spread his hands helplessly. "I'm a disgraced researcher living in exile. Maren is a retired stone-weaver with no political power. And you're an unbound girl barely out of her teens. Who would believe us? Worse, who would believe us and not immediately arrest you for being what you are?"

Kerra wanted to argue, but she couldn't. He was right. The power structures of Vaetheris were built on the careful separation and control of the tides. The revelation of a resonant would threaten the entire system.

"So what do we do?" she asked quietly.

"We investigate," Davin said. "Carefully, quietly, without alerting the authorities or the Hollowed. We find proof of what's happening and evidence of who's responsible. And in the meantime—" He turned to Kerra with an expression that was both apologetic and determined. "—we teach you to control your gifts. Because if the seal breaks and the Hollow King rises, you might be the only one who can stop him."

"How? I don't know anything about weaving. I can barely sense the tides without getting overwhelmed."

"Which is why we start with the basics." Davin gestured to the tide-garden outside. "Every plant out there responds to a different tide frequency. Stone, water, flame, wind, and yes, even void. You're going to learn to distinguish between them, to call each one individually rather than sensing them all as an undifferentiated mass. It will be difficult and frustrating and occasionally painful."

"But necessary," Maren added. "Because right now you're like a person trying to listen to five conversations at once. You need to learn to focus on one voice at a time."

Kerra looked between them—an exiled scholar and a retired weaver, two people with nothing to gain and everything to lose by helping her. But their eyes held only sincerity and a grim determination that matched the weight settling in her own chest.

The Hollow King was waking. His followers were working to free him. And somehow, impossibly, Kerra was the key to stopping it all.

No pressure, then.

"When do we start?" she asked.

Davin smiled, but it didn't reach his eyes. "Dawn tomorrow. For tonight, rest. The work ahead of you will require every ounce of strength you possess."

Kerra returned to the Windskimmer as the sun dipped toward the horizon, her mind churning with everything she'd learned. The docks were even more crowded than before, filled with ships arriving to report tide-vein disturbances or seek guidance from the Enclave-masters. Fear hung in the air like smoke, unspoken but palpable.

She found Captain Breslin in his cabin, pouring over charts with a troubled expression.

"Valmont. Good, you're back. I've got news, and none of it's good."

"What happened?"

"Harbormaster says reports have been flooding in all week. Pillars rising all over the archipelago, sometimes a dozen in a single day. The Enclave and the Holds are both scrambling to figure out what's causing it, and they're blaming each other as usual." Breslin rubbed his eyes wearily. "Meanwhile, the tide-veins keep getting weaker. My cousin says her daughter's water-weaving is down to maybe half strength. Other weavers reporting similar problems."

"How long before the Enclave realizes it's not the Holds' fault and vice versa?"

"Who knows? Could be days, could be weeks. Politicians and weavers don't exactly work on practical timelines." He glanced up at her. "I'm keeping the Windskimmer here for now. Too dangerous to make the Sunsward run until we know what we're dealing with. Which means you've got some time off, if you want it."

It was the opening Kerra needed. "Actually, Captain, I was hoping to study with someone here in the Crossing. A tide-lore researcher. It might help with navigation if I understand the veins better."

It wasn't technically a lie. Understanding the tides would absolutely help with navigation, assuming she lived long enough to do any more navigating.

Breslin waved a hand dismissively. "Fine by me. Just check in every few days so I know you haven't been press-ganged onto another ship. And Valmont?" His expression softened slightly. "Be careful. Crossing's usually safe, but with everyone on edge about the pillars, there's liable to be trouble. People looking for someone to blame."

"I'll be careful, Captain."

That night, Kerra lay in her hammock aboard the Windskimmer and stared at the ceiling, too wired to sleep despite her exhaustion. Somewhere beneath her, in the depths where the Deepwater Enclaves descended into permanent darkness, the tide-veins pulsed with failing strength. Somewhere even deeper, in the Abyssal Rift, something ancient and terrible stirred against its bonds.

And tomorrow, she would begin learning to fight it.

Kerra closed her eyes and tried to clear her mind, reaching for the meditative state her father had taught her as a child. Back then, he'd hoped she might become a wind-weaver like him, soaring through the skies above the Skyward Holds. The meditation was meant to help young weavers sense their affinity, to feel the call of their destined tide.

She'd never felt anything during those sessions. Now she knew why.

Carefully, cautiously, she lowered her mental barriers just a fraction. Immediately, the tides rushed in—water from the sea surrounding them, wind from the breeze sliding through the ship's rigging, stone from the coral pillars supporting the Crossing, even the faintest hint of flame from cooking fires on nearby platforms. And beneath it all, the void, that strange not-emptiness that connected them all.

Instead of panicking, Kerra tried to do what Davin had suggested: focus on one voice at a time. She reached for the water-tide first, since it was strongest here, and tried to isolate its presence from the others.

It was like trying to separate threads of different colors woven into a single rope. Every time she thought she had the water isolated, she found wind tangled in with it, or stone, or flame. They didn't want to be separated. They existed in relation to each other, each one defined by its interactions with the rest.

But slowly, painstakingly, she began to perceive distinctions. Water flowed; wind swept; stone endured; flame consumed. And void... void made space for all of them to exist.

Kerra held that understanding for perhaps three heartbeats before exhaustion claimed her and she tumbled into a deep, dreamless sleep.

When she woke at dawn, she could still feel the tides humming at the edge of her awareness. But for the first time since the pillar had risen, they didn't feel like a chaotic flood threatening to drown her.

They felt like a song she was just beginning to learn.
