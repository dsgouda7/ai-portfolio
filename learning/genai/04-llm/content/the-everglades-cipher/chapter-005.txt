Chapter 5: What Detective Hidalgo Found

The envelope from Arturo Vasquez-Cortez to Detective Carla Hidalgo contained two things: a handwritten note and a single flash drive.

Carla Hidalgo read the note at her desk with her breakfast empanada finished and a fresh coffee at her elbow and her door closed, which was unusual. She kept her door open. Closing it was a signal in the Violent Crimes bullpen that something significant was happening, and three of the four detectives in the adjacent open space noted it and did not discuss it, because noting and not discussing was the professional social contract.

The note was one page:

Detective Hidalgo —

My neighbor Sonia Rios mentioned your name when I asked her, hypothetically, who in Miami-Dade she would trust with a complicated case. She said you found her nephew two years ago and didn't make it into more than it was, and she said you never made her feel stupid for worrying. These are the qualities I am looking for.

The flash drive contains recordings I made during both visits from the man who came to my shop. I used a small recorder I keep in the desk drawer — I've kept it for years because there are occasional visits in the antiquarian trade that benefit from documentation. I did not know, the first time, that they would benefit from documentation. The second time, I knew.

The man's name, I later discovered through a contact I have in Cuban-American business circles, is Alejandro Reyes-Castillo. He works for a woman named Magdalena Santiesteban. I believe you know this family's name, because anyone who works organized crime in Miami knows this name.

The first visit he asked me to sell him the Codex. I declined. He was polite. The second visit he told me to give him the Codex. He was not polite. He told me that the information it contained was dangerous to certain people and that those people had resources I should not underestimate. He told me that accidents happened to scholars who overreached. He used the word "accident" twice.

I want these recordings to go to someone I trust in law enforcement because I believe they constitute evidence. I want you to have them because when this is over — however it is over — the people who arranged whatever happened to me should not be able to arrange that it was never investigated.

I am not naive. I know that recordings of a man making implicit threats constitute a narrow basis for action. I am giving them to you because I believe in the institution even when I cannot control the outcome.

Find who took me. And find what they want to do with what I know.

With respect,
Arturo Vasquez-Cortez

Carla Hidalgo read the note twice. Then she put it in an evidence bag and put the evidence bag in her desk drawer. She put the flash drive in a different evidence bag and logged it into the system. Then she sat for a moment with her hands on the desk and looked at the wall calendar.

Seven days missing. A man who had left his shoes behind. Recordings of a man who worked for the Santiesteban family. A manuscript that had, apparently, something to do with the Knights Templar.

She had worked Violent Crimes for six years and she had developed a taxonomy of cases. There were the cases that were straightforward (domestic violence, robbery gone wrong, dispute escalation) and the cases that were complicated (organized, political, inter-jurisdictional) and then there was a third category that she thought of privately as the serpent cases — the ones that appeared to have a head and a tail but kept revealing more body the longer you looked. Serpent cases took time and cooperation and the willingness to follow the animal wherever it went, even into territory where she was not the apex predator.

This looked very much like a serpent case.

She picked up her phone and called Malone.

"The recordings," she said, when he picked up. "The Santiesteban name. You got the same from Sanchez?"

"He implied it. Wouldn't say the name directly."

"He's being careful. Smart." She tapped her desk. "Alejandro Reyes-Castillo is a name I know. He's Magdalena Santiesteban's head of operations. Sophisticated professional — not muscle, not a fixer. Operations. He manages the legitimate businesses. Which means his involvement in leaning on a bookshop owner suggests this matter has elevated to the level of strategic concern for the family."

"What's your interest in the family?"

"Their legitimate interests are clean — import, logistics, real estate. Their interests that aren't discussed publicly are the concern of half a dozen agencies. I can't tell you what active investigations look like because you're not inside the tent. But I can tell you that a recording of Reyes-Castillo making implicit threats gives me leverage that I am going to use carefully."

A pause on his end. "You're going to contact him."

"Not immediately. First I'm going to find out if Fairweather has left the country." Another pause. "The consul general's office at the British consulate can confirm or deny on a courtesy basis. If she's still in Miami I want to talk to her before anyone else does."

"I had the same thought."

"I'm sure you did." Carla opened her browser and began searching. "Malone. Two things. First: stay out of my interrogations. If you find something I need, call me before you act on it. Second: the Codex at the library. Talk to your contact Mendes and make sure Special Collections has it in a secured location that isn't the general public-access area. If the Santiesteban family knows where it is—"

"Mendes keeps it in climate-controlled restricted storage. It's not in the catalog."

"That's good but not enough. I'm going to request a campus police presence at Richter. Low-key. Tell Mendes to expect it." She looked at the window. Outside, Miami was going about its business with the serene indifference of a city that has seen everything. "One more thing. Elena Vasquez-Cortez. Tell her not to be at the library today."

"She doesn't know where the Codex is beyond what Mendes told her."

"She's the daughter of the missing man who has the Codex. Anyone watching knows she knows. Keep her out of the line of sight." A pause. "You found the briefcase this morning."

"Yes."

"Then you've read the letter. He told you about the island."

"He did."

"I want a copy of the translation document." She said it flatly, the statement of a professional who doesn't waste words on justification.

"I'll email it this afternoon," Jake said, and hung up.

Carla sat for a moment. A Cuban bookseller who had spent thirty years quietly decoding a medieval map to a Templar island cache. A family with money and reach who had been watching for exactly this moment. An Oxford academic who arrived at a suspicious time and hadn't been seen leaving. And a detective nobody with a houseboat and a yellow legal pad who had gotten there first.

She opened a new case file. She titled it: VASQUEZ-CORTEZ, ARTURO — MISSING PERSON — CLASSIFIED SENSITIVE.

Then she started making calls.

---

Jake drove from police headquarters to Coconut Grove to pick up his binoculars, his long-lens camera, and a second recorder, and then to a coffee drive-through on Dixie Highway where he sat in the drive-through queue for six minutes and thought about the structure of the case and the places where it was still unclear.

What he knew: Arturo had the Codex. Arturo had been working toward a full translation with Fairweather. Someone connected to the Santiesteban family had visited him twice, the second visit explicit enough to record. Arturo had prepared safeguards and then disappeared anyway, taken too quickly for his preparations to protect him.

What he didn't know: where Arturo was. Whether Arturo was alive. Whether the Santiesteban family had what they wanted, and if so, what they were doing with it. Whether Fairweather was still in Miami and whether she was an ally or a complication.

What he suspected: the Codex was not the only leverage in the situation. The full translation existed in Arturo's head as much as on paper, and the final page of the Codex — the verification cipher, the one that required being at the island — was not yet resolved. Which meant that Arturo, alive and cooperative, was worth more than Arturo dead.

Which meant he was probably still alive.

For now.

Jake collected his coffee and drove toward South Beach.

He had an address. Not for Magdalena Santiesteban — her residence was in a gated compound in Coral Gables that was essentially unobservable from a public position. But the import/export business that served as the primary legitimate front for Santiesteban interests — Caribe Logistics, registered in Florida, operating out of a complex on the eastern edge of the Port of Miami — was not gated, not compound. It was a warehouse with offices on the second floor, facing Biscayne Bay, and it was the kind of place where people came and went with enough regularity that a parked car on the access road attracted no particular notice.

He parked on the access road.

He sat with his coffee and his binoculars and watched the building for two hours.

In those two hours he saw twelve vehicles enter or exit the parking lot. He noted plates. He took photographs with the long lens. He watched a loading dock operation with three large trucks and eight workers and the specific choreography of people doing an activity they have done many times.

At eleven-fifteen, a black Chevrolet Suburban pulled into the parking lot and parked close to the main entrance. A man got out of the passenger side.

Very tall. Over six feet. Gray suit. Heavy build, the weight of someone who was once very strong and still carries it. And at forty meters' distance through a 400mm lens, Jake could see, on the left cheek just below the eye, a scar. White-healed, curved like a crescent.

Alejandro Reyes-Castillo.

Jake photographed him from the parking lot to the door. Then he photographed the building, the Suburban, the plate on the Suburban, and the driver, who had stayed with the car and who was a younger man in a dark shirt who kept looking at his phone.

Then he sat back and drank the rest of his cooling coffee.

He had a face. He had a plate. He had a building.

He did not have Arturo. But this was the foundation.

He was about to put the car in drive when his phone rang. The screen read ROBERTO SANCHEZ.

"I'm certain," Roberto said, when Jake picked up. Not hello. Just: I'm certain. "The ring. A signet ring with a stylized S. I've seen that ring before, at a business lunch three years ago where I was a guest of a client — a charity auction for the Calle Ocho festival. The ring was on the hand of a man introduced to me as Alejandro. He worked, they said, for the Santiesteban family."

"I just photographed him going into Caribe Logistics."

A pause. "Jake. Be careful. These are not people who deal in complications. They prefer simplicity."

"I appreciate the warning."

"I'm not just warning you. I'm telling you something specific." Roberto's voice was low and measured, the voice he used when he was giving information rather than impressions. "Three years ago at that auction, I spoke to this man for perhaps ten minutes. He was charming, which in my experience means careful. Educated. Not threatening — exactly the opposite, in fact, reassuring in the way that very controlled powerful people are reassuring. But what I felt, talking to him—" He paused again, and Jake waited, because Roberto Sanchez's felt senses had been useful to him before. "What I felt was that he was someone who had made decisions that most people wouldn't make and that he had made them without much internal resistance. The way some people decide to go to the gym, or not to go to the gym. Easy decisions. You understand what I mean."

"I understand," Jake said.

"The Santiesteban family has existed in this city for seventy years. They have survived many things that kill smaller organizations. The way they have survived is by being willing to do what smaller organizations aren't."

Jake put the phone down and looked at the building.

Inside, somewhere in the architecture of that organization, was the answer to where Arturo Vasquez-Cortez was.

He put the car in drive.

---

He met Elena at Café Sonia at one in the afternoon. She was at the corner table, the one Arturo had used for his morning reading, with a glass of water and the particular stillness of someone who has been thinking very hard and is waiting to see where the thinking leads.

Jake sat down. Sonia brought him coffee without being asked.

He gave Elena the translated pages from the briefcase first — the thirty-page draft paper, not the original notes.

"Your father and Fairweather wrote this together," he said. "It's an academic paper presenting the translation of the Codex."

She read the cover page. Then she turned to the first section. Jake watched her read — quickly, the speed of a scientist used to processing text for information, not pleasure. She read for ten minutes without speaking. Then she put it down.

"It's real," she said. Not a question.

"Your father believed it was. Fairweather seems to have confirmed it."

"No, I mean—" She pressed one hand flat on the table. "I mean it's real to me now. When he wrote me about it I thought — I didn't dismiss it, he's not a man whose judgment I dismiss, but I thought it was the project of an aging man who had something brilliant in his hands and maybe couldn't be objective about what it was. The way all of us can have blind spots about our own discoveries." She looked at the paper. "This isn't that. This is scholarship. Careful, rigorous, footnoted. The kind of paper that would be reviewed and challenged and either held up or taken apart. It holds up."

"The final section is missing," Jake said. "The last page of the Codex is not fully decoded. There's a verification cipher that apparently requires knowledge of the island itself to resolve."

Elena looked at him carefully. "He told you about the island."

"He told me about the island."

"And the people who want what the island contains."

"And those people."

She picked up the water glass. She held it without drinking. "My father is alive," she said. It was the declaration of someone who needs to say something out loud to keep it true.

"I think he is," Jake said. "The people who have him can't decode the final section without his cooperation, and a dead man doesn't cooperate. That gives them an incentive to keep him alive."

"For now."

"For now," Jake agreed.

She put the glass down. "Tell me everything he wrote in the letter."

He told her everything.

She listened the way scientists listen — without interrupting, processing, categorizing. When he finished, the silence held for a moment between them like a held breath.

"He said he was protecting me," she said.

"He said he was."

"He's been doing that since my mother left. Since I was twelve." She looked at the photographs of Havana on the wall. "He never told me I was adopted."

Jake said nothing, because the letter had mentioned this and he had waited to see if she would say it first, and now she had.

"I know I am," she continued quietly. "I have the paperwork — it was in his apartment, in a drawer I opened years ago and didn't ask about because some things you don't ask about until you're ready. My birth certificate has different names. Cuban parents I've never tried to trace." She looked back at Jake. "I am telling you this because it may be relevant. If my biological family has any connection to any of the people involved in this—"

"Do you have any reason to think they do?"

"No. But my father has been hiding the fact of my adoption for thirty years along with the Codex and the Templar history and the island and apparently the Santiesteban connection. At this point I've stopped assuming I know what he has and hasn't hidden." She said this with the precise tone of a person who has processed fury and is now working in the cleaner space beyond it. "What do we do next?"

Jake thought about this. What came next was Fairweather, if she was still in Miami. And the Santiesteban building on the port. And the translation document, and what it said about the island. And the question of resources: who, besides the Santiestebans, had the capability to search for an uninhabited island in the Windward Passage with the resources to actually retrieve something from underwater caverns.

"Tomorrow morning," he said, "we go to find Dr. Fairweather."


---

That evening, in the marina, Jake sat on the deck of the Reluctant Witness with his legal pad and a rum and the peculiar companionship of nighttime Biscayne Bay. Across the water the towers blazed. A cruise ship was moving south, enormous and lit like a moving city, on its way to the open water.

He thought about the letter and the shoes and the fireproof cabinet and the locker and the way a careful man's preparations had not been quite careful enough.

He thought about Roberto's description of Alejandro Reyes-Castillo: someone who had made decisions that most people wouldn't make, and had made them easily. The way you decide to go to the gym.

He thought about Carla Hidalgo sitting at her desk with a closed door and a flash drive.

Then he thought about an island in the Windward Passage, between Cuba and Haiti, that a medieval Portuguese navigator had called Isla de los Espíritus.

The Island of Spirits.

He wondered what the water sounded like in the cavern under the eastern cliff, at high tide, in the specific way that Diego de Almeida had heard it and considered it worth encoding.

Jake finished his rum and went below and looked at the translation document for an hour before it got too late for complex reading and he put it down.

The Codex was real. The island was real. Arturo was somewhere in Miami — or somewhere in Florida, or somewhere in the Caribbean — in the hands of people who had decided, at some point in the last month, that patience was over.

What moved them from patience to action? Fairweather's visit. The confirmation that the translation was complete enough to be meaningful. Someone had known that the visit happened. Someone had known it was the turning point.

Jake wrote on his legal pad: WHO TOLD THEM ABOUT FAIRWEATHER'S VISIT?

Then he stared at the question for a long time.

Not Roberto. Not Sonia. Not Elena, who hadn't known.

Dr. Constance Fairweather herself, or someone who had been watching her.

He circled the question twice.

Then he turned off the light and let the boat rock him and waited, as he had learned to wait, for the morning.
