Chapter 20: Havana

Elena Vasquez-Cortez arrived in Havana on a January morning on a charter flight from Miami, with a single carry-on bag, a notebook, a phone loaded with every contact she had been able to find, and the specific emotional preparation of someone who does not know precisely what she is walking toward and has decided to walk anyway.

She had done three things before coming.

The first was to spend two weeks with a genealogist who specialized in Cuban family research — an elderly woman named Dr. Isabel Cruz who operated from an office in Hialeah full of microfilm readers and index cards and the specific smell of paper that has been handled for decades, and who had spent forty-two years building a network of researchers, archivists, and community contacts in Cuba that she maintained through a combination of formal channels, informal correspondence, and the kind of slow patient relationship-building that was the only methodology that worked across the political and geographic distances involved.

Dr. Cruz had found a record.

The record was in the civil registration archive of the municipality of Old Havana: a birth registration dated July 1968. The mother's name was Rosa Isabel Castillo de Espinosa. The child's name was listed as Elena Castillo. The father's name was listed as unknown.

This was the record. This was Elena's name, before Arturo had renamed her.

Dr. Cruz had also found, through correspondence with a contact at the University of Havana library, a subsequent record: a marriage registration from December 1972. Rosa Isabel Castillo de Espinosa had married a man named Miguel Fuentes Agramonte. A dentist. They had lived in the Vedado district.

And then, from the same contact: Rosa Fuentes (née Castillo) had died in 2003. Miguel Fuentes had died in 2009.

This was the end of the direct parental line.

But: Rosa and Miguel had had children. Two. A son, Jorge Fuentes, born 1973, who was in the civil registration records as residing in Havana, occupation listed as teacher. A daughter, Marta Fuentes, born 1976, who was in the records as married, with a Havana address.

Elena had half-siblings.

She had known this was possible. She had prepared herself for this possibility. She had thought she was adequately prepared.

She was not adequately prepared. Nothing adequately prepares you for the specific reality of it: actual names, actual addresses, actual people walking around Havana who shared her biological mother and had no idea she existed.

The second thing she had done was call her father.

Arturo had answered on the second ring, which meant he was at his desk, which meant it was after seven in the morning in Miami and he was already working.

"I have names," she said.

He was quiet for a moment.

"I know," he said.

"You know?"

"I knew she had remarried. I knew she had children." A pause. "I knew before I left Miami, in 1970. I had sources." Another pause. "I did not know if she had died. I did not — I knew you would want to find out about your siblings eventually. I thought you deserved to find out yourself."

"Why didn't you—" She stopped.

He said nothing.

"You could have told me," she said.

"Yes," he said. "I could have." A very long pause. "I didn't know how to tell you that the woman who gave you up had made another family. Had made, possibly, a complete life in which you were a decision she had made and had moved past." He paused. "I was afraid that would hurt you more than not knowing."

She thought about this.

She said: "Was that your decision to make?"

A long silence.

"No," he said. "It was not."

She held the phone. Across the bay, the Miami skyline did its thing of looking permanent and new simultaneously.

"I'm still going," she said.

"I know," he said. "You should." He paused. "Tell me what you find."

The third thing she had done was write to Jorge Fuentes.

Not a detailed letter. A brief one: her name, her city, her professional affiliation, and one sentence: I believe Rosa Isabel Castillo de Espinosa may have been my biological mother, and I am hoping to make contact with her family. I will be in Havana in January and would welcome a meeting if you are open to one.

She had sent it through Dr. Cruz's network, via the contact at the University of Havana, with a stamped addressed return envelope for response.

Jorge Fuentes had replied three weeks later.

He had replied in a single handwritten page in small, clear script. He said: You may indeed be who you believe yourself to be. Our mother spoke of a child who had been given to someone she trusted, before her marriage. She spoke of it only twice that I know of — once to me, when I was seventeen and asked her directly why she sometimes seemed sad in a specific way; and once to Marta, who I understand told her husband but no one else. We did not know your name or where you had gone. We knew only that you were born in July 1968 and that our mother had given you to a friend who was going abroad. We have thought about this. We would welcome a meeting.

She had read this letter four times.

She had carried it in her coat pocket on the plane.

---

Havana in January was grey and warm in the specific way of a Caribbean city in the dry season: the humidity low, the sky overcast in the morning and clearing by noon, the sea breeze coming through the Malecón and carrying with it the salt and the petroleum smell of the port and the sound of music from somewhere that was always somewhere else.

The city was doing what Havana does, which is to say it was being Havana: magnificent and deteriorating and alive and strange, the colonial architecture in various states of restoration or entropy, the cars from five decades ago moving alongside the occasional new vehicle, the street life dense and particular and governed by codes that took years to fully understand. She did not fully understand them. She had been here twice before, at academic conferences, and had found the city beautiful and complicated in ways that she had not been emotionally equipped to process at the time.

She was not sure she was equipped now.

But she was here.

Jorge Fuentes lived in a third-floor apartment in the Vedado, in a building that had been built in the 1920s and which had a lobby with original mosaic tile and an elevator that was not operational. She walked up three flights of stairs with her bag and stood at his door and knocked.

He opened the door immediately.

He was fifty-one years old and he was tall, which she had not expected, and he had brown eyes with the specific depth of color that meant — she registered this with the precise analytical attention of a biologist — shared pigmentation genetics.

He looked at her.

She looked at him.

Neither of them spoke for a moment.

Then he said, in Spanish: "You look like her."

She said, in Spanish: "I've been told I look like my father."

"You look like her too," he said.

He stepped back from the door. She came in.

The apartment was full of books and the smell of coffee and the sounds of a city that came through the open windows without apology. There was a table. There were chairs. There was, already on the table, a pot of coffee and three cups.

A woman was standing by the window. Marta. Three years younger than Jorge, which made her forty-eight. She had Rosa's chin — Elena could see this in the photograph that was on the bookshelf, which was the photograph she was looking at, the photograph of a woman who was both familiar and unknown.

Rosa Castillo at thirty-something. Smiling at the camera with the specific half-smile of someone who has been told to smile and has complied without full commitment. Dark eyes. Dark hair. The chin.

Elena looked at the photograph for a long time.

Then Marta said, gently: "Sit down. Have coffee."

She sat down.

She had coffee.

She had not thought past this moment. She had prepared for the walk to the door and the knock and the opening and had not imagined what came after it in any detail, because imagining it had been too much while she was trying to get here. And now she was here, and the coffee was in front of her, and Jorge and Marta were on the other side of the table, and the window was open to the January Havana morning.

She said: "I'm sorry to—"

Jorge said: "Don't apologize."

She looked at him.

"We've been waiting for this," he said. "Not waiting — that's the wrong word. We knew it might happen. Marta and I talked about it." He paused. "After our mother died, we talked about it. We decided that if you ever came, we would tell you everything we knew."

"What do you know?" she said.

He and Marta looked at each other.

Marta said: "She talked about you with guilt. Not constantly — she was not someone who performed emotion. But twice I heard her, and she felt guilty. She believed she had made the right choice and she also felt guilty about it. Those two things were both true."

"Why did she do it?" Elena said.

"The year you were born, 1968," Jorge said, "was a very difficult year to be young and unmarried and pregnant with a child whose father was foreign-born. Not impossible. But difficult. She was nineteen years old. She was brilliant — she had just been accepted to medical school." He paused. "The man who was your father was a student she had met at a conference. She described him — the one time she described him to me — as the most serious person she had ever met. She said: he was the most serious and the most kind, and he was going back to Portugal and then to America and there was nothing to be done about that."

Elena thought about Arturo at twenty-three.

"He didn't know," she said.

"We think she didn't tell him," Marta said. "She said she didn't tell him because telling him would have complicated his departure and because the decision was already made."

"She decided to give you to him later," Jorge said. "After she had made initial arrangements with a Havana family. Something changed. She reached out to him through a mutual contact, told him she had a child and that she needed the child to go to him. He apparently agreed without conditions." He paused. "She told me that. She said: he agreed without conditions."

Elena thought about this — about Arturo at twenty-three agreeing without conditions to take a child that wasn't his plan, crossing the Straits of Florida with an infant, opening a bookshop. The whole subsequent life, built around that single decision.

She said: "He's a good man."

"We gathered," Marta said.

Elena looked at the photograph on the bookshelf.

"She had a good life?" she asked.

"A complicated life," Jorge said. "And yes. In the end, a good one." He paused. "She was a doctor. She worked until she was seventy. She was — she was not warm, in the way of very capable people who have organized their feelings around their work. But she was fair. She was honest. She was—" He paused. "She told the truth, even when it was inconvenient. That was her defining quality."

Elena thought: that sounds familiar.

She thought: that sounds like me.

She said: "Can I—" She gestured toward the photograph.

Marta got up and brought it to her.

She held the photograph. She looked at Rosa's face — the half-smile, the dark eyes, the chin that was her chin.

She had expected to feel grief. She had prepared for grief. What she felt instead was something different and less dramatic: recognition. The feeling of finding a piece of information that explains something you'd always half-known. The feeling of a gap in the data being filled.

She thought: I am the daughter of this woman and of Arturo Vasquez-Cortez and of the specific circumstances that brought them together in Havana in 1967 and sent them apart in 1968 and that kept them apart for fifty-five years while each of them, separately, built a life in the direction they could manage.

She thought: and here I am.

She thought: here is exactly where all of that led.

"I'd like to stay for a few days," she said. "If that's all right."

Jorge and Marta looked at each other.

"We have a spare room," Marta said.

She stayed for three months.

---

She and Jorge walked the Malecón on her second evening in Havana, in the specific way that people walk along a seawall when there are things to say that work better in motion, in the open, with the sea beside you.

He told her about Rosa: the medical career, the patients, the house in Vedado, the garden. He told her about their father Miguel, who had been a dentist and who had been a quiet man who made very good food and who had loved Rosa with the specific devotion of a man who understood that he had found something extraordinary and who organized his life accordingly.

She told him about Arturo: the bookshop, the Codex, the thirty-four years, the cipher. She told him the short version and then, when he asked questions, the longer version. She told him about the island and the archive.

He listened with the attention of a man who teaches — a quality of listening that does not prepare its response while the speaking is happening but actually waits for the speaking to finish.

When she was done, he said: "So your father spent his whole life proving something that was, ultimately, real."

"Yes."

"And you spent your life not knowing what he was doing."

"Mostly knowing he was doing something. Not knowing what."

He looked at the sea.

"Our mother did that too," he said. "There was always the sense of something she was processing, that wasn't the life in front of her. It wasn't a problem — she was fully present for the life in front of her. But there was something behind it." He paused. "Now I think that was you."

She looked at the Havana horizon — the sea, the buildings, the particular quality of the evening light.

"It's strange," she said, "to be the thing someone was processing."

"You are also the thing someone was devoted to," he said. "That's what your father was doing."

"Yes."

"Those are not incompatible," he said.

"No," she said. "They're not."

They walked.

The Malecón did what it does: carried them along its edge, the city on one side and the sea on the other, the old and the new, the known and the open.

She thought about Arturo at his kitchen table telling her everything about Rosa, the Sunday afternoon, the rain.

She thought about what he had said at the end: I should have told you sooner.

And what she had said: You told me now.

She thought: that was enough. That is enough.

She thought: the record is never complete. The archive is never fully inventoried. There is always more that could have been documented and wasn't. That is the nature of the archive.

What matters is what has been preserved.

What has been preserved is enough.

She kept walking.

The Malecón stretched ahead of them, salt-stained and magnificent, exactly itself.

She walked it all the way to the end.
