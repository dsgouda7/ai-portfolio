Chapter 27: The Western Crossing (Diego de Almeida, Atlantic Ocean, November-December 1307)

Day One of the crossing proper.

The Canaries were below the horizon by mid-morning. The trade wind was steady and from the right direction. The crew had settled into the watch rotation that Diego had established before departure: four hours on, four off, two watches alternating, with himself and Gaspar each taking the helm for the critical navigation watches at dawn and dusk.

Diego wrote in his pilot's log: departed La Palma anchorage at dawn. Wind northeast, about fifteen knots, steady. Course west by southwest as planned. Crew in good health and order. Provisions and water for sixty days from La Palma, in addition to the staging supplies already consumed. The Nossa Senhora da Paciência handles well in the trades; she is a comfortable vessel in following seas.

He had been keeping pilot's logs since his apprenticeship with Fernão. The practice was standard in the guild: a daily record of position, course, speed, weather, and anything notable, which could be used to verify navigation decisions after the fact and to accumulate knowledge of specific routes for future reference. His logs were more detailed than the guild's minimum standard because Fernão had told him that the minimum standard was what you kept when you were certain of your route, not when you were learning it.

He was learning this route.

---

Day Six.

The daily position calculations, based on the sun's noon altitude and the dead reckoning from the previous noon, placed them approximately one thousand miles west of the Canaries. The trade wind had been steady throughout, and the speed through the water had been consistent at about six to seven knots — somewhat faster than Diego had conservatively planned. They were ahead of schedule.

The crew was in good spirits. The trade wind sailing was, compared to the coast-hugging work of the Atlantic coast runs, easy: no shoals to navigate, no headlands to weather, no unexpected currents to compensate for. The horizon in every direction was empty of everything except sea and sky. This emptiness was different from the emptiness of an overcast night in familiar waters: it was the emptiness of being far from any known point of reference, which was not frightening but was significant, and felt significant.

Gaspar came to the chart board one evening and looked at the marked positions.

"We're further west than anyone I know of," Gaspar said.

"Further than anyone who has reported it," Diego said. The distinction was not pedantic. There were sailors who had gone further than they reported, for various reasons.

Gaspar looked at the chart. The next marked position to the west was the word the cartographer had written in the blank space beyond the known Atlantic: a description in the cartographer's language of unknown ocean.

"What's there?" Gaspar said.

"Land," Diego said. "If the wind charts are right."

"When will we know they're right?"

"When we find the land."

Gaspar looked at this for a moment and then went back to his duties, which was the correct response.

---

Day Nineteen.

They had been in the open Atlantic for nineteen days. The trade wind had been steady throughout: a gift, Diego thought, in the sense that good tool design is a gift — it does not surprise you, it simply works. The daily position calculations placed them approximately three thousand miles west of the Canaries.

He had been checking his position by the night stars as well as by noon sun sights. The latitude was correct for the wind pattern and the current that the charts described. Everything was where it should be.

At dawn on Day Nineteen, one of the sailors on the forward watch reported birds.

They were not Atlantic birds — not the pelagic species that the crew had been seeing since leaving the Canaries. These were different: smaller, different coloring, circling behavior that suggested they were not far from land.

Diego did not alter course. The birds were flying east — toward them, coming from the direction they were heading. That was information, and it was consistent with land ahead.

He wrote in the log: birds observed suggesting proximity to land. Course maintained. Crew informed to maintain watch.

---

Day Twenty-Three.

Land.

Gaspar called it from the forward watch at mid-morning, and Diego was at the bow rail within two minutes. It was low and green on the horizon, barely distinguishable from the ocean's edge, but it was there: a strip of land that was not a cloud.

He wrote in the log: land observed bearing west by northwest. Low profile, heavily wooded, estimated distance twelve to fifteen miles. Consistent with description in wind charts.

He altered course slightly to approach from a favorable angle and took soundings as they came in. The bottom was sand, shoaling from deep to shallow as they approached. The water color changed from deep blue to green to the particular pale green of very shallow water over white sand.

He anchored in the lee of the island in eight fathoms on a sandy bottom.

It was a small island. Flat, not more than thirty feet high at its highest point, covered in low trees and dense scrub. The water was warm — noticeably warmer than Atlantic water at the same season. A group of land birds watched them from the beach with the specific curiosity of creatures who have not been conditioned to fear humans.

This was not where he was going. This was the first landfall, confirming the route.

He wrote in the log: anchor set. Island uninhabited, no people observed. Water appears fresh in a stream visible from the vessel. Will take on water and continue.

He spent three days at the island, taking on fresh water, allowing the crew to go ashore (which they did, with the wonder of men who have been at sea for twenty-three days without land), and studying the chart of the island chain that the wind charts had indicated lay further along his route.

On the morning of the fourth day, he weighed anchor and continued west-southwest.

---

Day Thirty-One.

The islands continued: a chain of them, varying in size, consistently green, consistently warm. He had sailed through several of the passages between islands, navigating by eye and sounding in water that was clear enough to see the bottom in fifteen feet. The crew was working well. The provisions were adequate.

He was now looking for the larger island chain that the wind charts described as lying further south and west — the chain that, in the specific detail of the charts he had studied, was where the intended landing was.

On Day Thirty-One, the character of the islands changed. The flat limestone cays gave way to larger, hillier islands with different vegetation: heavier forest, higher terrain, rivers visible from offshore. He was in the right zone.

He took a careful bearing on the northernmost large island and compared it to the chart. The correspondence was good. He was approximately where he expected to be.

He turned southwest, following the channel between the large island chain and a smaller string of cays to the west.

---

Day Thirty-Eight.

He found the island.

It was on the Windward Passage side — the eastern channel — identified by the specific limestone ridge that ran north to south along its length, which the chart had marked with a notation he had spent two months learning to read. The island was small enough to be unremarkable among the dozens of similar islands in the chain, large enough to have the ridge formation that contained the sea cave.

He anchored offshore and went in by small boat with Gaspar and one sailor.

The ridge's western face was ironshore at the waterline: sharp fossilized coral that made landing difficult but not impossible if you knew where the one sandy access point was. He had studied the chart's notations for this and had found the description of the access: a specific bearing from the anchor point that led to a channel through the ironshore just wide enough for the small boat.

They came ashore.

He walked to the ridge and found what the chart described: a sea cave entrance at the low-tide waterline, partially submerged at present, accessible in approximately two hours when the tide dropped further.

He sat on the limestone above the cave entrance and waited for the tide.

Gaspar sat beside him and looked at the water.

"This is the place," Gaspar said.

"Yes," Diego said.

"Why here?"

Diego looked at the island: the dense tropical forest, the birds he had been hearing since they anchored — a species he had not previously encountered, small and quick in the canopy — the smell of the warm salt water and the vegetation.

"Because it's unreachable," he said. "Nobody comes here. Nobody has any reason to come here. Nobody knows it exists."

"We know it exists."

"We were told to come here," Diego said. "That's different."

Gaspar thought about this.

"In a hundred years," Gaspar said, "nobody will know we were here."

Diego said: "In a hundred years, nobody needs to know we were here. What they need to know is where the boxes are."

He watched the tide drop. When the cave entrance was fully exposed, he went in.

---

The interior of the cave was exactly what the chart had described, and more: the chamber was large enough to work in, dry in its air-pocket section, and the walls had been worked — cut stone, the geometry of deliberate modification. Someone had been here before, a long time before. Diego did not know who. He did not need to know. What mattered was that the cave was sound and that the air-pocket section would maintain the conditions the documents required.

He walked the cave carefully. He measured the air-pocket section: roughly eight meters by five, with ceiling height adequate for a standing person and floor surface dry and relatively level. The humidity felt low — drier than the outside air. The temperature was cool and constant.

He came out and reported to Gaspar.

Gaspar had brought the sounding equipment from the boat. They took soundings around the cave entrance: the passage was navigable by a man swimming, with an underwater section of approximately eight meters before opening into the air pocket.

Diego wrote in his field notes: cave suitable. Air-pocket section adequate for purpose. Conditions favorable. Can proceed.

He sent Gaspar back to the vessel with instructions for the beginning of the unloading operation.

It took eleven days to transfer the cargo from the Nossa Senhora da Paciência to the cave. Eleven days of small-boat loads from ship to shore, the boxes lowered into the water and guided through the submerged passage by two swimmers at each end, stacked in the air-pocket section in the order Diego had specified — the most important materials first and most protected, the administrative documents accessible but secured. The lead-lined boxes were sealed with pitch by the carpenter before each underwater transfer.

On the eleventh day, Diego sealed the cave entrance with a stone plug that he had cut and fitted to the specific geometry of the entrance passage. The plug would not stop a determined excavator but would prevent casual discovery by anyone who found the cave by accident.

He stood on the ridge above the sealed cave and looked out at the Windward Passage.

The water was very blue. The trade wind was from the northeast. A frigate bird circled overhead, riding the updraft.

He thought about the materials sealed in the cave below him. He thought about the people who had created those materials: the officers who had run the intelligence network, the brothers who had kept the financial records, the scholars who had maintained the theological and navigational manuscripts. Many of them were now in French prisons. Some were dead. The network they had built was being systematically destroyed by a man who had used it and feared it in equal measure.

The materials below him were the record of that network and that history. He had placed them where they could survive that destruction.

He thought: it was enough.

He went back to the boat.

---

The return voyage took forty days.

The return route followed the current north and then northeast, away from the trade wind zone, into the variable winds of the higher latitudes, and then east across the Atlantic on the westerlies. It was a harder passage than the outbound: more variable conditions, more weather management, more attention required from the helmsman. But it was navigationally sound — the same route that, he was confident, anyone who understood the Atlantic wind system would eventually work out — and they made it without major incident.

They arrived at the Azores in early March 1308, took on provisions, and proceeded to Porto.

He filed no official report of the voyage. The guild record showed only that the Nossa Senhora da Paciência had departed and returned from an undescribed Atlantic passage. The crew was paid, thanked, and dispersed. Gaspar departed for a berth on a Lisbon-bound vessel without asking any of the questions he had refrained from asking throughout the voyage.

Diego went to the Porto preceptory and reported to Brother Afonso: the mission was complete. The cargo was secured at the designated location. He would provide the precise location to Afonso in sealed form within the week.

He went back to his room above the guild hall and spent three days writing the Codex Almeida.

He wrote it for a reader he could not identify. He wrote it with the care appropriate to correspondence with a person who deserved to find it. He wrote the cipher system with precision, checking each element three times, because the reader who eventually came would not have access to him to ask what he had meant.

He sealed the document in its own lead-lined container. He gave it to Afonso. He gave Afonso the sealed location coordinates separately, as a backup that would be preserved in the Order's internal archive.

He had no way of knowing that the Order's internal archive would be destroyed, that his sealed coordinates would be lost with it, and that the Codex would become the only remaining record of where the archive was.

He could only do what he could do.

He thought: the Codex was sufficient, if the reader was adequate.

He thought: it would have to be.

He went back to work.
