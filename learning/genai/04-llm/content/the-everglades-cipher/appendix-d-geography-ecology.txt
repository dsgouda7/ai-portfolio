APPENDIX D: THE FLORIDA KEYS AND CARIBBEAN — GEOGRAPHY, ECOLOGY, AND SETTING

A Location Guide for The Everglades Cipher

I. Miami: The City Where the Case Begins

Miami is a city that does not behave like other American cities. Its orientation is not toward the continent but toward the sea and toward the Caribbean — it is, in its deepest cultural identity, not a southern American city but the northernmost city of the Caribbean diaspora. This orientation shapes everything about it: the architecture (built for heat and light, with the flat-roofed pragmatism of tropical construction), the food (Cuban, Haitian, Colombian, Brazilian, Nicaraguan, Dominican, in neighborhoods that are simultaneously distinct and intermixed), the language (a private bilingualism that operates below the surface of the city's official English), and the specific urgency of a city that has been, for a century, the place where people come when they need to start over.

The city was incorporated in 1896, which makes it young by American standards and very young by Atlantic standards. It was built on a former Seminole and Tequesta coastline — the Biscayne Bay shore had been inhabited for thousands of years before the first European settlement — and on a foundation of land that is, in geological terms, barely above sea level. The Miami limestone formation that underlies the city and the adjacent Everglades is porous, permeable, and very nearly flat: the highest natural elevation in Miami-Dade County is approximately twenty-five feet above sea level, in the Coconut Grove neighborhood.

This geology means two things: Miami is extremely vulnerable to sea level rise, which is a subject of continuous and urgent local discussion; and Miami's freshwater supply comes entirely from the Biscayne Aquifer, which sits just below the surface and is recharged by rainfall moving through the porous limestone. The Everglades, which cover the western portion of the South Florida landmass, are directly connected to this aquifer system and to Biscayne Bay: the sheet flow of fresh water that moves south through the Everglades and into Florida Bay is the mechanism by which the coastal ecosystem sustains itself.

Little Havana, where La Luz de los Libros is located, is the neighborhood that developed along SW 8th Street (Calle Ocho) as the primary settlement zone for Cuban exiles who arrived after 1960. The neighborhood was already a working-class immigrant district before the Cuban arrivals — it had been home to earlier waves of Italian and Jewish immigrants — and it absorbed the Cuban community in the specific way that Miami absorbs things: thoroughly, transformatively, and permanently. The culture of Little Havana — the coffee windows, the dominoes in Domino Park, the restaurants, the music, the particular pace of a neighborhood that is doing its own internal work — is one of the most densely specific urban cultures in North America.

Coconut Grove, where Jake lives on the Reluctant Witness, is the oldest continuously inhabited settlement in the Miami area, originally established in the 1870s and 1880s by Bahamian Black settlers who worked the coastal fishing and sponging industries. It was subsequently developed as a bohemian and artistic community in the early twentieth century and is now a mixed residential and commercial neighborhood known for its tree canopy (which gives it a specific microclimate noticeably cooler than adjacent neighborhoods), its bayside location, and its position as the point from which much of Miami's recreational boating operates.

Biscayne Bay, which forms the eastern margin of Miami from north to south, is a shallow coastal lagoon — typically less than eight feet deep across most of its area — separated from the Atlantic Ocean by a chain of barrier islands (Miami Beach, Key Biscayne, and the other barrier islands of Miami-Dade). The bay is highly productive biologically: its seagrass beds support the base of a food web that includes sea turtles, manatees, dolphins, wading birds, sport fish, and a complex community of invertebrates. It is also highly stressed: urbanization, nutrient runoff from agriculture and landscaping, boat traffic, and historical dredging and filling have substantially degraded the bay ecosystem from its pre-development state.

The Coral Gables neighborhood, where Magdalena Santiesteban's compound is located, is one of the oldest planned residential communities in the United States, developed by George Merrick beginning in 1921 with a specific Mediterranean Revival architectural aesthetic and a formal street plan based on European precedents. The neighborhood is notable for its street tree canopy (particularly the banyan and live oak trees that form tunnels over the main roads), its coral rock construction (using the Miami limestone that underlies the whole area), and its consistent maintenance of the original aesthetic through historic preservation ordinances. It is, by South Florida standards, old money — the distinction between old money and new in a city that is less than a century and a half old being a relative one.

II. The Florida Keys: Limestone and Light

The Florida Keys are the exposed limestone ridge of the Floridian Platform — the same geological formation that underlies the entire South Florida peninsula, here reduced by water and time to a chain of small, flat islands extending southwest from the mainland for approximately 125 miles, terminating at Key West. The Keys are divided conventionally into the Upper Keys (approximately Largo Key to Marathon), the Middle Keys (Marathon area), and the Lower Keys (from the Seven Mile Bridge to Key West), and the distinct character of each section reflects differences in geology, ecology, and historical settlement.

Big Pine Key, where Nacho Reyes operates his dive charter business, is the largest island in the Lower Keys and is notable primarily for two things: it is the location of the National Key Deer Refuge, which protects the Key deer (a miniature subspecies of the white-tailed deer that is found only in the Lower Keys and which stands approximately twenty-four to twenty-eight inches at the shoulder); and it provides access to the backcountry waterways of the Great White Heron National Wildlife Refuge and the Florida Keys National Marine Sanctuary, which together protect the vast shallow-water ecosystems of the Gulf side of the Lower Keys.

The dive sites accessible from Big Pine Key include the Looe Key Reef, which is one of the more pristine and consistently surveyed sections of the Florida Reef Tract — the only living coral barrier reef in the continental United States and the third-largest in the world. The Florida Reef Tract extends from Fowey Rocks, near Miami, south and west to the Dry Tortugas, approximately seventy miles west of Key West. It parallels the Keys at a distance of three to seven miles offshore, on the Atlantic side.

The reef is an ecosystem of extraordinary biological complexity and extraordinary fragility. Coral reef formation in this part of the Atlantic developed following the last ice age, approximately eight to ten thousand years ago, when sea levels rose to their current position and the warm, clear, shallow Florida Straits provided the conditions for reef growth. The coral organisms that build the reef — primarily stony corals, which secrete calcium carbonate skeletons — are themselves animals, each individual coral polyp living in partnership with photosynthetic algae (zooxanthellae) that provide the polyp with most of its energy through photosynthesis.

This partnership between coral animal and algal partner is the foundation of the reef's biological productivity and also its vulnerability: when water temperature rises above approximately 30 degrees Celsius for extended periods, the coral expels its zooxanthellae in a stress response. The coral turns white — the phenomenon called coral bleaching — and without its algal partner it has limited capacity to survive. If the stress is prolonged, the coral dies. The Florida Reef Tract has experienced multiple significant bleaching events since the 1980s, associated with increasing ocean temperatures, and these events have caused substantial changes in the reef's composition and coverage.

Nacho's logbooks, recording thirty years of observations on Looe Key reef, are a fictional analog of the real-world long-term reef monitoring data that has been collected at various Florida Keys sites since the 1970s. The pattern he notes in the novel — recovering coral coverage at specific stations, measured by careful seasonal observation — is consistent with documented recovery patterns at some monitored sites in the Florida Keys after bleaching disturbances.

III. The Everglades: The River of Grass

The Everglades occupies approximately 1.5 million acres of the southern half of the Florida peninsula, making it the largest subtropical wilderness in the United States and one of the most distinctive wetland ecosystems in the world. Marjory Stoneman Douglas's description of it — "the River of Grass" — captures its essential character: the Everglades is not a swamp in the conventional sense but a slow-moving sheet of fresh water, typically only a few inches deep and a mile or more wide, flowing southward at approximately one hundred yards per day from its historical headwaters in Lake Okeechobee to Florida Bay and the Gulf of Mexico.

The biological communities of the Everglades are organized around the gradient from fresh water (the sawgrass prairies and tree islands of the interior) to brackish transition zones (the estuarine mangroves of the coastal margin) to salt water (Florida Bay and the coastal reefs). Each zone supports its own assemblages: the freshwater interior is home to the wading bird populations — herons, egrets, spoonbills, wood storks — that historically numbered in the millions and that have declined by approximately ninety percent from their pre-drainage populations. The transition zones support one of the most productive fish nursery habitats in North America. The coastal mangroves are the foundation of the coral reef's food web.

The Hialeah-adjacent canal site where Jake finds Arturo being held is located in the eastern Everglades margin — the area of Miami-Dade County where the edge of the Everglades system meets the edge of the suburban development that has been advancing westward from the coast since the 1950s. This margin is ecologically significant: it is the recharge zone for the Biscayne Aquifer, it provides wildlife corridor connectivity between the Everglades proper and the coastal habitats, and it is the area where the conflict between development pressure and ecological function is most acute.

Tommy Okeechobee's restoration work — the cypress planting at the eleven-acre parcel mentioned in her note to Arturo — represents a real practice in Everglades restoration: replanting native vegetation in degraded areas to rebuild habitat connectivity and hydrological function. Cypress trees (Taxodium distichum, the bald cypress, and Taxodium ascendens, the pond cypress) are foundational species in the freshwater swamp communities of the Everglades; they grow slowly (ten to twenty-five years to establish a functional canopy) but are long-lived and structurally complex, providing habitat for numerous species.

IV. The Caribbean: The Windward Passage and Cayo Espiritu

The Windward Passage is the strait between Haiti (the western portion of Hispaniola) and Cuba, approximately fifty miles wide at its narrowest, with depths exceeding two thousand meters. It has been a major shipping route since the Spanish colonial period: it provides the most direct access between the Atlantic and the Caribbean Sea for ships coming from the north and east, and it was the principal route for the Spanish treasure fleets and for subsequent maritime commerce between Europe and the Caribbean.

The passage's name refers to the prevailing northeast trade winds that blow across it, creating a favorable (windward) approach for sailing vessels coming from the Atlantic. A ship coming from Portugal or the Canary Islands in the trade wind belt would approach the Caribbean via the Windward Passage naturally, carried by the same winds that Columbus used.

The fictional island of Cayo Espiritu is located in the Cay Sal Bank area, northwest of Haiti and southeast of Cuba, in waters that are today the subject of various jurisdictional claims (the Bahamas, Cuba, and the United States all have interests in different portions of the Bank). The Cay Sal Bank is a real geographic feature: a large, shallow limestone platform approximately 240 miles in circumference, with multiple small exposed cays and extensive submerged reef systems. It is remote, largely uninhabited, and poorly charted. It is the kind of place where an island could be largely unknown to modern authorities.

The specific acoustic phenomenon described in the novel — water moving through a narrowing sea cave passage, producing a sustained tonal output audible from outside the cliff — is based on the general principles of sea cave acoustics and on specific real examples. The Sea Organ of Zadar, Croatia, built in 2005 by architect Nikola Bašić, uses the principle of wave energy driving air through organ pipes embedded in a stone seawall to produce musical tones; the design demonstrates that natural cave systems with the right geometry can function acoustically as natural instruments.

The Phrygian dominant mode (specifically: the fifth mode of the harmonic minor scale, built on the dominant) is the characteristic mode of the flamenco tradition, of much traditional Arab music, and of certain Eastern European folk traditions. It is distinguished by the minor second between its first and second degrees — the interval that gives it its characteristic tense, quasi-Arabic quality. It is the mode of the fandango, of habanera rhythm, of the genre of music that connects Iberian, North African, and Caribbean musical cultures in exactly the way that the novel's plot connects them. Diego's selection of this mode as his acoustic signature for the archive is, in this context, appropriate: it is the mode of the Atlantic world he navigated.

V. The Florida Straits: Waters Between

The waters between Miami and Cuba — the Florida Straits — are approximately ninety miles at their narrowest, between Key West and Havana. They are among the most actively traveled waters in the Western Hemisphere: commercial shipping, recreational boating, migration (the crossing from Cuba that has been attempted in various vessels since 1960, by hundreds of thousands of people), and the biological migration of organisms that the Gulf Stream carries north along the Florida coast.

The Gulf Stream itself — the great oceanic current that carries warm Caribbean surface water north and east, eventually cooling and sinking in the North Atlantic and driving the Atlantic thermohaline circulation — runs through the Florida Straits with velocities up to five knots. For a sailing vessel moving north from the Caribbean, the Gulf Stream is both an asset (free speed) and a navigational challenge (it moves the vessel east while moving it north, requiring compensation in course). For a vessel moving from the Windward Passage north through the Straits of Florida, as La Determinada does in this novel, the current is broadly favorable: east of the Bahamas, the current sets north.

The Cay Sal Bank shortcut that Nacho takes — cutting through the shallower waters between Cuba and the Bahamas rather than going around the outside — is a real navigational option for small-draft vessels that reduces distance on the route from the Windward Passage to the Florida Straits. It requires local knowledge (the Bank has numerous submerged hazards) and favorable wind conditions, and is not a route that large commercial vessels can use. Small sailing vessels operated by experienced local navigators have used it for centuries.

VI. Key West: The End of the Road

Key West sits at the end of the Overseas Highway — and, from the mainland's perspective, at the end of everything. It is the southernmost city in the continental United States, forty-seven feet at its highest elevation, approximately ninety miles from Cuba, and profoundly aware of both facts. It was first settled by wreckers — the specific profession of maritime salvage operators who profited from the numerous ships that came to grief on the Florida Reef — and has subsequently been, at various times, a major sponging center, a cigar manufacturing town, a naval base, a bohemian artist's colony (it has produced and attracted writers from Hemingway to Elizabeth Bishop to Thomas McGuane), and a tourist destination that has developed around the mythology of all of the above.

The specific Key West character — the combination of genuine eccentricity and self-conscious eccentricity that is the city's permanent mode — is part of what makes it the appropriate waypoint on the novel's return journey. Key West is a place that has decided what it is and performs that decision consistently, which is a form of honesty.

The breakfast on Duval Street is the kind of meal that Key West does well: generous, unpretentious, oriented toward people who have been doing something physical and need to eat. Duval Street at seven in the morning is the specific transitional hour of Key West — after the all-night portion and before the tourist portion — when the city briefly shows its internal life before the performance begins.

VII. Havana: The Other City

Havana sits across the Florida Straits at a distance that is geographical small and politically enormous. It is the closest major city to Miami, ninety miles south, and for most of Miami's Cuban-American community it is simultaneously the city they are from (or that their families are from) and the city that they cannot freely visit or return to — a fact of political geography that has shaped the cultural psychology of an entire community for sixty years.

The city was founded by the Spanish in 1519 (a final location, after several earlier attempted settlements) and was the administrative capital of the Spanish Caribbean empire for three centuries. Its harbor — one of the best natural harbors in the Caribbean — made it the embarkation point for the Spanish treasure fleets and the primary gateway for the trade networks that connected Spain, the Americas, Africa, and the wider Atlantic world. The city's colonial architecture, which survives in substantial form in Old Havana (declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1982), reflects three centuries of wealth derived from sugar, tobacco, and the transatlantic slave trade.

Havana in the contemporary period is a city of profound contradictions: beautiful and deteriorating, controlled and ungovernable, performing stasis while continuously evolving. The Malecón — the seawall boulevard that runs along the Havana waterfront for five miles — is the city's most characteristic public space: the place where Havana's residents come for air, for company, for the relief of being at the edge of something that goes on longer than the city.

Elena's walks on the Malecón with Jorge are the specific experience that Havana's spatial culture provides: the combination of movement along the city's edge and conversation in the open air that is the city's characteristic mode of processing difficult things. The Malecón has served this function for a hundred years. It will continue to.

VIII. The Atlantic World: Diego's Route

The route from Portugal to the Caribbean that Diego de Almeida would have sailed in 1307-1308 is, in broad outline, the same route that Columbus sailed in 1492 and that the Portuguese navigators of the Age of Discovery systematized in the early fifteenth century. The key elements of Atlantic sailing in the trade wind era are:

The volta do mar: the discovery, by Portuguese navigators in the fifteenth century but almost certainly known in practice to earlier Atlantic sailors, that the wind system of the North Atlantic is circular — trade winds blowing westward in the tropical latitudes, prevailing westerlies blowing eastward in the northern latitudes. A ship sailing south from Europe to the Canary Islands or the tropical Atlantic would find trade winds to carry it west; a ship returning would need to sail far north to find the westerlies for the eastward passage. This is the systematic explanation for why Atlantic voyages were possible with the sailing technology of the medieval period: you didn't sail against the wind, you used the wind system.

Madeira and the Canary Islands: the island groups in the eastern Atlantic that served as waypoints and provisioning stops for Atlantic voyages. Madeira, discovered (or rediscovered) by Portuguese navigators in approximately 1418, is described in the Codex as Diego's first Atlantic landfall. This is slightly anachronistic — the documented Portuguese rediscovery of Madeira was in 1418, over a century after the novel's 1307 date — but the islands were known to Arab and Catalan navigators before the Portuguese claims, and the specific date of the earliest European contact with these islands is a matter of scholarly debate.

The Sargasso Sea: the area of the North Atlantic bounded by the major current systems (the Gulf Stream to the west, the Canary Current to the east), characterized by calm winds, warm water, and the abundant floating Sargassum seaweed that gives it its name. For sailors in the trade wind era, the Sargasso was the zone of calms between the reliable wind belts — a navigation challenge that required patience or alternative propulsion. Medieval vessels with their limited sail plans were more dependent on oars in calm conditions than later ships.

The Windward Passage approach: a ship coming from the Canary Islands on the trade winds would make landfall in the Lesser Antilles (the eastern Caribbean island chain) before navigating west through the Caribbean. The most direct route to the Windward Passage from the east runs along the northern coast of Hispaniola and through the passage between Hispaniola and Cuba. This is the route that Diego's navigation text describes.

The specific sailing time from Portugal to the Caribbean via the Canary Islands in a medieval vessel (a cog or early caravel type) would have been approximately six to eight weeks under favorable conditions. The return voyage, sailing north to find the westerlies and then east across the North Atlantic, would have been longer — twelve to sixteen weeks was typical for the Age of Discovery voyages.

Two voyages of this kind — 1307-1308 and 1312-1313 — are described in the novel's historical interludes. The first voyage: assessment of the site and initial deposit. The second voyage: completion of the archive deposit and construction of the sealed containers. The timeline is tight but not impossible for a man who was, by the time of the second voyage, one of the most experienced Atlantic navigators of his era.

Whether such voyages actually occurred is not historical fact. It is the novel's central fiction.

But the Atlantic wind system that would have made them possible is real, and it was there, waiting, seven centuries before Columbus used it.
