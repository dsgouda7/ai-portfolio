Chapter 3: Roberto Sanchez Knows Everybody

The botanica on Flagler Street was called El Corazón Verde, and it was the kind of place that divided people into two camps: those who walked past it quickly without meeting the eye of the window display, and those who walked through the door. Jake had been in the second camp since the first time Roberto Sanchez had talked him through what Santería actually was, on a rainy Tuesday in 2019, which was the day Jake had realized he understood about twelve percent of the city he lived in.

Roberto was sixty years old and built like a man who had been very strong in his forties and carried the memory of it in his shoulders. He had three gold teeth — top left, bottom right, bottom right — and a gray beard that he kept trimmed with the precision of someone who understood that grooming was a form of communication. He wore guayaberas exclusively, always with the top button open, always pressed. Behind the counter of El Corazón Verde, surrounded by candles and carved santos and jars of herbs whose Spanish and Yoruba names he could recite from muscle memory, he looked like a man exactly in his element.

"Arturo Vasquez," he said, before Jake had finished the sentence. He had already come around the counter, already guided Jake to the two chairs he kept in the consultation alcove at the back of the shop, already put water on the small electric kettle. "Sit."

Jake sat.

"Seven days," Roberto said. He settled into the other chair. Between them was a small table with a white cloth and a candle that was lit and working. "Seven days is bad. You know what seven days means in this city."

"It means he's either very far away or very hidden," Jake said.

"Or both." Roberto folded his hands. He had the hands of a man who worked with them — not construction or heavy labor but the precise manual work of someone who handled small objects with attention. "I've been thinking about Arturo since Elena called me. What you want to know about is the men who came to see him."

Jake looked up. "More than the large Cuban man?"

"The large Cuban man and someone else. Three weeks before Arturo disappeared. The large man—" Roberto paused, considering. "I don't know who he is. But I've seen the type. When you have a business like mine, you develop an eye for certain kinds of powerful, and that man has the kind of powerful that comes from organized resources. Not criminal exactly — or not only criminal — but the kind of organized. Do you understand?"

Jake understood.

"And the someone else?" he said.

"A woman. She came two weeks after the large man, which makes it about five weeks before Arturo disappeared. I saw her when she was leaving — Arturo walked her out, which he sometimes does with important customers. Tall, older, white hair. Very composed. She walked to the corner and called someone on her phone and then stood there for fifteen minutes having what appeared to be a very precise argument."

"Dr. Constance Fairweather," Jake said. "Oxford."

"I don't know her name. But she didn't look like a tourist and she didn't look like a customer. She looked like someone who had just been told something important and was deciding what to do about it." Roberto looked at the candle between them. "I asked Arturo about her the next morning. He said she was a colleague. A scholar. He said she had come a long way to confirm something and that she had confirmed it, and that he wasn't sure this was entirely good news." He looked up. "Those were his exact words. He said he wasn't sure whether being right was good news."

"When did he say this?"

"The Thursday after she left. We had coffee. Arturo is — was — is not a person who speaks in mysteries. He was a very direct man in conversation. So when he said something ambiguous I paid attention." Roberto rose to attend the kettle. He made two cups of something that smelled of herbs and cinnamon and brought them back. "There is something else. Something I have been deciding how to tell you."

Jake wrapped his hands around the cup. It was warm and the morning was air-conditioned.

"Four days before Arturo disappeared," Roberto said, "he came in here. Which was unusual — he came in occasionally for supplies, he was not a regular consultee, though he respected the tradition. He came in and he asked me to hold something for him."

Jake kept his face neutral.

"He gave me an envelope," Roberto continued. "A standard manila envelope, sealed, with my name written on it. He said: if something happens to me, give this to someone I trust. I said: what kind of something? He said: any kind of something. I said: who should I give it to? He said: you'll know when you know." Roberto raised his hands. "This is the kind of thing Arturo would say."

"You still have it."

"He said someone I trust. He didn't say to give it to a detective immediately, and for the first few days I thought he had just gone somewhere without telling anyone and would be back." Roberto looked at Jake steadily. "I went to Elena yesterday. She hadn't told me yet she was hiring you. I gave it to her."

Jake put down the cup. "You gave it to Elena."

"Last night. At eleven o'clock. She didn't call me?"

"She called the next morning." He pulled out his phone and rang Elena.

She picked up on the second ring. "Jake?"

"Roberto gave you an envelope last night. Why didn't you call me?"

A pause. "I was going to — I just, I opened it and I needed to think. There's a key inside. And a note. The note says: the copy is at the university library, under Elena's name. If this key doesn't work, ask for Dr. Heliodoro Mendes in Special Collections."

Jake exhaled slowly. "What does the key look like?"

"A small storage locker key. The kind you'd find at a bus station or an airport. Yellow plastic head, the number 44 stamped on it."

"What university?"

"UM. University of Miami, Richter Library. I think — the note is specific — he says Heliodoro Mendes is 'the only honest man in the Special Collections department,' which sounds like something my father would write." Her voice had a catch in it, briefly, then was smooth again. "Jake, the note also says — it says 'tell whoever you trust that the island is real. The coordinates are embedded in the Codex. Do not let the Codex leave Miami.'"

"Is the Codex in the storage locker?"

"I think so. I haven't gone yet. I was waiting — it's eight-thirty in the morning."

"Call Dr. Mendes," Jake said. "Now. Before we do anything else. And don't go to the library without me."

He ended the call and looked at Roberto.

"You knew there was a key," Jake said. It was not an accusation, just a fact.

"I felt it through the envelope," Roberto said, without apology. "I didn't open it because it wasn't mine to open."

Jake nodded. Roberto had his own code and it was not the same code as Jake's, but it was a code, and that was more than he could say for a significant portion of the people he worked with. "The large Cuban man with the scar. Have you seen him before? Outside of the visits to Arturo?"

Roberto was quiet for a moment. This was a different kind of quiet than before — not the quiet of someone gathering information, but the quiet of someone calculating risk. Jake let it sit.

"I've seen the type," Roberto said finally. "Not the man. But the family — the structure. There is a family in this city — a Cuban family, very established, with interests in import, in logistics, in real estate, in several things that are not discussed publicly — and their people have a look. A style. The suit, the ring, the specific way of occupying space that says: I represent something larger than me. This man had that." He looked at the candle. "I don't want to say a name without being certain."

"If you become certain," Jake said.

"I will call you. Yes."

Jake stood. "Thank you for the tea."

"Hierba buena," Roberto said. "Good for the stomach and the mind. Take the cup, there's more where that came from."

Jake left the cup on the table. Outside, Flagler Street was waking into its full morning self — a delivery truck double-parked outside a bodega, three schoolchildren in uniforms negotiating the cracked sidewalk on bicycles, an old man in a guayabera reading La Nueva Frontera at a bus stop with the focus of someone for whom reading a newspaper was still an act with weight.

Jake stood for a moment in the morning sun and thought about the shape of what he had.

A bookshop owner had discovered something. Had been hiding something, specifically from someone — the large man with the scar who had come twice and argued once. Had made a dead drop with a trusted neighbor. Had gone missing.

The dead drop key led to a copy of something Arturo had been studying. The original — the Codex Almeida, presumably — had been removed from the fireproof cabinet in the shop. By Arturo himself, or by someone with the combination.

The Oxford historian had visited, confirmed something, and been upset by the confirmation.

An island was real. The coordinates were embedded in the Codex. The Codex should not leave Miami.

Jake walked to his Bronco and drove toward the University of Miami.

---

The Richter Library at the University of Miami was a building that looked, from certain angles, as if it had been designed by someone who was given the architectural brief "a building that is primarily about shelving" and had embraced the brief wholeheartedly. It was large, air-conditioned to the precise temperature of mild discomfort, and staffed at this hour by three students at the circulation desk who gave Jake the particular look that civilians give people who don't look like academics but are walking with academic purpose.

He met Elena in the lobby. She had changed since last night — same professional efficiency in the dress, but her eyes were different. The envelope had changed them. It had moved her from worried to something more specific.

"The storage locker key is for Coral Gables bus station," she said, without preamble. "Number forty-four. My father has used that station — we took buses from there when I was a child." She held the key up briefly, then put it in her jacket pocket. "Dr. Mendes is expecting us. Third floor, Special Collections."

Dr. Heliodoro Mendes was seventy-three years old and had the air of a man who had spent fifty years in service to objects more patient and reliable than people. He was a small man with an enormous white mustache and the hands of a scholar — fine, careful, always slightly curled at rest as if ready to hold something fragile. He shook Jake's hand with both of his.

"Arturo talked about you," he said. "He said there was a detective on the water who found things." He said it with the tone of someone for whom this was high praise.

"He talked about me?"

"When he asked me to register his daughter as an authorized user and to keep a package in our restricted storage, about six weeks ago. He said: if something goes wrong, Elena will come, and possibly a detective called Malone. He spelled your name."

Jake absorbed this. "He was preparing."

"He was being careful. Come."

They followed Mendes through the specialized quiet of Special Collections — a room where the air itself seemed archived, filtered of the ordinary atmospheric noise of living — to a storage area at the back where materials not currently on display were kept in labeled acid-free boxes on metal shelves.

From one shelf Mendes produced a box labeled, in Arturo's handwriting, EV/2023-C/ORIGINAL.

"He brought this personally," Mendes said. "Six weeks ago, on a Thursday morning. He was — not frightened, exactly, but alert. Like someone who has reached a point of commitment and is now executing the plan." He set the box on a reading table. "He said: if someone comes for this who isn't Elena or Malone, call the police and then call this number." He produced a business card from his jacket pocket and passed it to Jake.

Jake looked at the card. It was from Detective Carla Hidalgo, Miami-Dade Violent Crimes.

Arturo had known Carla Hidalgo's name.

"He seemed to know Detective Hidalgo?" Jake asked.

"He said she was thorough and honest and the only person in local law enforcement he would trust with this." Mendes opened the box with careful hands.

Inside, wrapped in archival tissue paper, was a document approximately thirty by twenty centimeters. It was parchment — clearly very old parchment, the kind that has developed its own specific pallor and texture over seven centuries — covered in dense script in what appeared to be multiple hands, with elaborate border decorations that had faded to shadows of what they had been. Several passages were marked with pencil lines in the margins — Arturo's annotations, cautious and small, made with a pencil so the marks could be erased without damaging the document.

Even to Jake's entirely unspecialized eye, it was obviously old. And obviously significant. The kind of document that changed the size of the room it was in.

Elena looked at it without touching it. Her expression was one he had not seen from her yet — something that mixed grief and wonder in a proportion that suggested the wonder was winning by a narrow margin.

"The Codex Almeida," she said quietly.

"The Codex Almeida," Mendes confirmed.

Jake took photographs. Seventeen of them, from different angles and distances, making sure to capture every visible margin annotation. Then he put his phone away and looked at Mendes.

"No one else has asked about this? In the past week?"

"No one." Mendes looked at the document with the calm possessiveness of a man who has spent his life in custody of objects that don't belong to him. "But two days ago, I received a phone call. The caller identified themselves as a researcher from Oxford University. A Dr. Fairweather. She asked whether we had any recent donations or deposits from a private collector named Vasquez-Cortez." He folded his hands. "I told her we could not confirm or deny information about individual depositors. She thanked me and hung up."

"She's still here," Jake said. "Or she's come back."

"Whoever she is, she is looking for this." Mendes placed the archival tissue carefully back over the document. "Arturo trusted this library with it. I intend to be trustworthy."

Jake and Elena walked out into the campus heat, which after the archival cold felt like being welcomed back to the planet. Jake put on his sunglasses and looked at the palm trees lining the walkway and thought about the shape of things again.

Arturo had hidden the Codex. Had told only Mendes and Roberto what to do if something went wrong. Had apparently known something was coming, at least as a possibility, and had arranged his safeguards.

What he had apparently not arranged was his own protection.

That was the gap. The gap between the careful preparation and the missing shoes.

Someone had moved before he was ready. Or he had underestimated how quickly they would act.

"The bus station," Jake said to Elena. "I'll go alone. You stay reachable."

"That's my father's things."

"I know. Which is why I'm not bringing you to a bus station locker that someone may already know about." He looked at her. "Your father spent six weeks setting up careful protocols. Let's use them."

She looked at him for a moment. Then she gave him the key.
