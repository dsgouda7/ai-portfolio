Chapter 13: Night Watch and the Second Voyage

They anchored for the night at the edge of the Great Bahama Bank, in water too deep for the anchor in the western passage but with the Bahamas to the east providing the illusion of protection from the Atlantic swell. It was not a comfortable anchorage — the boat moved with the uninterrupted purpose of deep water — but Nacho judged it safer than continuing south through the Providence Channels in the dark, and the judgment of a man who had been navigating these waters since 1989 was not a judgment Jake was inclined to second.

They took two-hour watches. Sofia took the first, eleven to one. Nacho took one to three. Jake took three to five and stood in the cockpit with the satellite phone and the stars and the boat moving under him and thought about everything.

The watch is an ancient institution. Before electronic navigation, a watch was the difference between safe arrival and reef. After electronic navigation it became partly ceremonial — the chartplotter would sound an alarm, the AIS would track approaching traffic — but its core function remained unchanged: someone awake, someone paying attention, someone responsible for the vessel and everyone in it.

Jake had stood watches before. Not on boats but in other contexts — in the particular dark of early morning in precincts, in the waiting that is the detective's version of navigation. You maintain vigilance not because you expect the worst but because the worst tends to arrive when vigilance lapses.

He watched the horizon. He watched the stars, which in open water away from any city were extraordinary — not the thin scatter of a Miami night but the dense unpolluted mass of the actual sky, the sky that most humans had looked at for most of history, the sky from which Diego de Almeida had read his position in the Atlantic and the Caribbean and the unknown waters beyond.

He thought about Diego.

He had read the translation document carefully over two days and had formed a picture of the man that was part inference and part imagination and part the specific knowledge that you develop when someone's choices become your context. Diego de Almeida had been careful. He had been faithful — not in the religious sense, though he was presumably that too, but in the older sense of keeping faith with a commitment made under extraordinary circumstances. He had gone back a second time when he didn't have to. He had made the document when he could have simply remembered. He had built in the verification cipher when a simpler approach would have served.

These were the choices of someone who believed the future mattered. Who built for the future because they believed the future would have people in it who deserved what he was keeping.

Jake looked at the stars and thought about what kind of faith that required. The faith of a person in 1315 that the document they were creating would survive, would be found, would be understood, would be protected by someone who understood what they had protected.

Seven hundred and eight years, give or take. And here they were.

He thought: you did it, Diego. We're on our way.

HISTORICAL INTERLUDE — The Second Voyage, 1312-1313

Diego de Almeida was thirty-seven years old when the Order of Christ sent word to him in Lisbon.

He had been expecting it for five years.

The Order of Christ was the Portuguese reconstitution of the Templars, authorized by King Dinis in 1319 — technically — but operating informally from at least 1312, when the remaining Templars in Portugal, who had been sheltered by a Portuguese king less enthusiastic than his French counterpart about destroying the most effective military and financial organization in Christendom, had begun the quiet work of reform and continuation under the new name.

The word came via a man named Brother Vicente, who rode to Lisbon from the Order's commandery in Castro Marim on the Portuguese Algarve, arriving at dawn on a Monday and presenting himself at Gonçalo's house with a letter sealed with a seal Diego recognized.

The letter said: it is time.

Diego spent two days in preparation. He wrote letters to three people who needed to know he was going. He packed for a long voyage — longer than the first, this time with more resources, because the Order of Christ had money and ships and the benefit of five years of careful planning. He retrieved the folded piece of vellum from his boot, where he had kept it for fifteen years with the habitual security of a man who understands that certain things are worth inconvenience, and put it in his coat pocket where it would be more accessible.

He rode to Castro Marim.

At the Order's commandery, a long stone building overlooking the Guadiana River and the Portuguese-Castilian border, he was briefed by a man he knew only as Brother Prior, whose face he never fully saw because the briefing took place in a room where the light was deliberately insufficient. The briefing told him: the second cargo had arrived. The second cargo was what had been in transit since 1307 — the archive that had been passed from hand to hand across five countries and fifteen years, always one step ahead of the people who wanted it, always moving, never stopping long enough to be found.

He was told: the island. The same island as before. The same location. The cargo goes with the previous deposit.

He was told: Fernão Pires is dead. His son, also named Fernão, has the Santa Providência. He knows the way.

He was told: you are the only living person who knows both the island's location and the full cipher system. When this is done, write the document. The document is the only copy. Keep it secure.

Diego asked: what if I die before I finish the document?

The Brother Prior said: then everything is lost.

Diego said: I see. He said it the way a man says it when he has just understood the full weight of what has been placed on him.

Then he went to Faro, on the southern Portuguese coast, and found the Santa Providência and the younger Fernão Pires, who looked like his father but was less taciturn, which Diego found interesting and potentially more interesting still at sea. They sailed in March, heading for Madeira and then south and west into the ocean, following the course that Diego kept in his head with the precision of something he had not allowed himself to forget.

The second voyage was easier than the first in some ways and harder in others. Easier because the weather was better in the initial passage and easier because Diego was older and knew what was coming. Harder because the cargo — nine sealed chests, oak-bound with iron, heavier than anything the first voyage had carried — occupied two-thirds of the ship's hold and made La Providência ride low and slow. Harder because there were fourteen men aboard rather than fifteen, and two of them were clearly soldiers of the Order of Christ who spoke no Portuguese and who sat apart and watched the horizon and gave nothing away, and whose presence Diego found both reassuring and deeply sobering.

He asked the younger Pires once, on a night watch, what he thought they were carrying.

Pires was quiet for a long time. The stars were very bright. The water was dark and calm.

"Whatever killed my father," Pires said, and then said nothing else.

Diego thought about this for the rest of the voyage. Pires senior had died of what the records called a fever, in Marseille in 1309. Diego had not been there. He did not know if the fever was natural or arranged, and he had decided, at some point in the fifteen years since, that not knowing was the correct state for a person who intended to remain alive.

They reached the island on the thirty-eighth day of the voyage.

It looked the same. The tree-covered rise above the white sand beach, the reef to the east, the blue water over the sand around the western approach. Diego stood in the bow and watched it come toward them with the specific emotion of someone returning to a place that has occupied their mind for five years and finding it exactly as they left it.

They anchored offshore. The same anchorage as before.

At first light, the same routine: tender, the same beach, the same jungle path. Diego and the two Order soldiers and the younger Pires. Carrying the chests was more difficult this time — the weight was substantial and the path was overgrown — but they managed.

They went to the place that Diego had marked on his previous visit. The place was unchanged.

Below it was what the first voyage had left.

Diego examined this — the first cargo, undisturbed, exactly as he had helped place it in 1308. The seal had held. The preservation was complete. Five years of tropical humidity and the archive was intact.

They added the second cargo.

They did this carefully, with the reverence that the soldiers apparently felt their contents warranted. Diego watched the soldiers handle the chests with the same attention he would have given to a physician treating a wound — this is what must be done, and it must be done well.

When it was done, the soldiers withdrew to a distance. Diego and Pires covered everything as before and extended the marking system that Diego had created on the first visit.

Diego asked: should I make a record of what is specifically in the second cargo?

The soldier who spoke Portuguese said: Brother Prior said no. He said the document you create should describe what is there without describing what it is. He said the scholars will know when they find it.

Diego accepted this. He thought about the scholars. Seven hundred years later, if things went correctly.

They walked back to the beach. The smell of the jungle was extraordinary — he always noticed smell at moments of transition, the specific and irreplaceable combination of vegetation and salt air and the particular biological richness of a coral island in the Caribbean. He thought: I will remember this. I will remember this precisely, because it is what I will be carrying for the rest of my life.

On the beach, while they waited for the tender to return, he did something he had not done on the first voyage.

He walked to the eastern cliff.

The cliff rose from the beach to perhaps thirty meters, coral limestone, ancient, with the specific worn quality of rock that has been in the presence of ocean for geological time. At its base the water moved in the complex way that water moves when it has architecture to navigate: current, eddy, backwash, the layered behavior of the tide.

He sat on a rock at the cliff base and waited for the tide.

It was perhaps an hour. The tide came in from the east, across the reef, and it moved through the passages in the reef with the specific momentum of a large volume of water finding its channel. He felt it before he heard it — the vibration through the rock he was sitting on, a low-frequency trembling that was below sound and above silence.

Then he heard it.

It was exactly as the first expedition's survivor — his contact before the first voyage, a man who had been to the island before him on an entirely unofficial reconnaissance — had described it. A resonance. A low-frequency resonance with a complex tonal structure. Not music in any composed sense — not anything with a melody or a pattern that a human mind had arranged. But modal. As he listened with the ear of someone who had been educated in both European and Arabic music theory, he could hear the modal structure: the intervals, the scale, the specific quality that the Arabic theorists called primordial.

He sat there for a very long time.

Then he knew what the third stage of the cipher would be.

Jake came off watch at five AM with the first gray light showing in the east and Nacho appearing in the companionway with two cups of coffee.

"Sleep?" Nacho said.

"Two hours maybe," Jake said.

"Good. That's enough." Nacho took his watch position and Jake went below and lay down in the bow cabin and thought briefly about Diego de Almeida sitting on a rock at the base of an eastern cliff in the Caribbean, listening to the tide, and then he fell asleep.

He woke at eight to the smell of food and the changed quality of the boat's motion — they were moving again, under power, the motor steady and the hull lifting and falling in the way of a boat that has cleared the shelter of the bank and is in open sea.

He went up.

"The Bahamas behind us," Nacho said. "Cuba to the southwest. Windward Passage entrance in approximately six hours."

Jake looked at the horizon. The sky was enormous out here — the specific enormity of open water sky, unobstructed, the dome fully visible.

"Any contact on the radio?" he asked.

"One ship passed us to the east overnight. Coast Guard broadcast this morning about a vessel in the northern Straits requested to report for documentation verification — they didn't say what vessel. Someone south of us, based on the sector."

Jake thought: the Carità.

"If the Coast Guard intercepted them—"

"It would delay them," Nacho said. "Not stop them. Maritime documentation is a short process. An hour, maybe two." He paused. "But two hours is two hours."

Jake stood at the helm and looked south at the approaching Caribbean and thought about the Carità making its way through whatever delay the Coast Guard had arranged.

He thought: we need to be faster.

He thought: we're already going as fast as we can.

He thought: then it's what it is.

He poured a second cup of coffee and watched Cuba grow on the western horizon, which it did slowly and with the specific gradual drama of distant land appearing over the curved surface of the planet, first as a line, then as a shape, then as geography — the green and the rock and the coast becoming specific as they drew closer on a parallel course, maintaining the distance from Cuban territorial waters that was both legally necessary and, given the operational complexities of this voyage, strategically wise.

Arturo emerged at midday with his notebook.

"I've resolved the modal structure," he said.

He said it to the group at large — Nacho and Jake at the helm station, Elena and Sofia on the foredeck. They came together at the stern.

"The third stage of the cipher uses the Phrygian dominant mode as its key," Arturo said. He had written out the symbol correspondence in his notebook, and he turned the notebook to show them: two columns, symbols on the left, their resolved equivalents on the right. The equivalents were numbers. "These are positional coordinates within the coordinate system Diego established in the first two cipher stages. The final stage doesn't give a new location — it confirms the existing location by providing a cross-check via the modal notation." He paused. "But there's still one line I need to verify acoustically. If my identification of the mode is correct, the last line resolves to a description of the approach to the underwater chambers — the specific passage through the cavern from the entrance to the main deposit chamber."

"So you still need to hear it," Elena said.

"I still need to hear it. But I'm more confident now that what I'll hear will match what I'm expecting." He looked at Nacho. "When you were in the cavern — at high tide, in the resonance — did you note the quality of the direction? Was the sound uniform, or was it stronger from a specific direction?"

Nacho thought. "Stronger from the northeast. From inside the cavern. Like the sound was coming from deeper than where I was."

Arturo wrote something. "The deposit chambers are further into the cliff than the main resonant space. The resonance might be channeled from there." He paused. "There may be a connection between the acoustic architecture and the chamber arrangement that Diego noted and that the last cipher line describes. The sound as a guide, not just an authentication."

Jake looked at him. "The sound tells you where to go inside the cavern."

"In addition to telling you the cipher key. Yes." Arturo looked at his notes. "Diego was thorough."

"He was thorough," Jake agreed.

Nacho looked at the chart. "We enter the Windward Passage at approximately eighteen hundred today. We approach Cayo Espiritu from the north-northeast — there's a channel through the reef that I've used before, you have to come in at exactly the right angle." He paused. "If the tide is right, we anchor off the western beach at nightfall. We go into the cavern at the first low tide after sunrise tomorrow."

"And if the Carità gets there first?" Sofia asked.

Everyone was quiet for a moment.

"They don't know the reef passage," Nacho said. "Without knowing the passage, you can't approach the eastern side of the island safely. If they get there before us and try to force an approach, they'll put the boat on the reef."

"If they have a tender, they can row in," Jake said.

"If they have a good diver, they can approach from the north and swim the reef," Sofia said.

"They have Andrea Montaldo," Jake said. "I don't know her diving skills."

"They have a Jesuit and some contractors," Elena said. "Possibly none of them are divers."

"We don't know," Jake said. He looked at Nacho. "Best case we get there before them. Worst case we get there at the same time. Either way, we're going into that cavern."

Nacho nodded. He opened the throttle another notch.

The motor ran steady under them and the Cuban coast moved past to the west and the Caribbean sun reached its noon intensity and the day compressed toward evening with the peculiar acceleration that the middle of long voyages have, when the destination is finally real and the hours between now and arrival have the character of something being used up.
