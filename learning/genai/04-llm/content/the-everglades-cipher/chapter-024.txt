Chapter 24: The Reluctant Witness

Jake Malone filed his taxes on April 15, which was the correct date for a man who had been self-employed for twelve years and had learned not to procrastinate on the things that could be handled on schedule.

He sat at the kitchen table of the Reluctant Witness with his receipt envelope and his laptop and the tax software that he found unreliable but used anyway, because the alternative was paying an accountant, and he was capable of doing this himself with sufficient patience.

The financial picture for the previous year was clear, if not lavish.

Revenue: three completed cases. Two standard backgrounds (corporate employee, custody dispute). One significant case — the Vasquez-Cortez matter — which had generated fees and reimbursements that, net of the three weeks of expenses in the Keys and at sea, had put him in a reasonable place for the first quarter.

He looked at the numbers.

He thought: this is a viable business.

He thought: it has been a viable business for twelve years and it will continue to be a viable business because I keep making it viable, which is the only way any business continues.

He filed the taxes.

He closed the laptop.

He made a cup of coffee and stood on the deck.

It was morning. The bay was silver-grey, the way it was in April before the full summer humidity arrived. A pelican — his pelican, the one who was a regular, who had apparently decided that this dock was worth the commute — was on the outer post, doing what pelicans do: existing with the maximum dignity that a bird of its proportions could manage, which was considerable.

He looked at the bay.

He thought about the case.

He had been thinking about the case in the way you think about something that has been resolved: retrospectively, working backward through the decisions, checking the work. Not second-guessing. Checking. There was a difference. Second-guessing involved imagining outcomes you wish had been different. Checking involved understanding what you did and why.

What he had done:
— Accepted the case from Elena Vasquez-Cortez, a client whose father had disappeared and who had come to him because she had no other options.
— Followed the evidence from the bookshop to Sonia Rios to Roberto Sanchez to the dead drop envelope to the library to the Codex to the Everglades fish camp and back.
— Identified the threat actors: Reyes-Castillo as the kidnapper, Montaldo as the external actor with Vatican connections, Langford as the political opportunist.
— Coordinated with Carla Hidalgo at Miami-Dade, with the FBI, and with Coast Guard to establish legitimate process before acting.
— Delivered the client's father. Found the archive.

He thought: those are the facts of the case.

He thought: the case was a success by every measurable standard.

He thought about the measuring.

The measuring was his private activity. Nobody asked him to measure. Elena had paid the full invoice. Arturo had thanked him. Carla had told him the grand jury testimony was complete and the relevant investigations were proceeding. Nobody needed him to sit on his deck in April and review the work. He did it anyway, because it was the practice that kept him honest.

The honest assessment: he had done the work correctly.

He had also, at two specific points, been lucky.

The first lucky moment was at the Everglades fish camp, where the timing of his arrival — twelve minutes earlier than the backup had been scheduled — had mattered because Arturo had been in the process of being moved. If he had arrived twelve minutes later, the fish camp would have been empty. He had arrived when he did because Nacho had driven fast on the Tamiami Trail because Nacho had understood before Jake said it that there wasn't time to be careful.

The second lucky moment was Father Bellini's arrival at the cavern. Bellini had known where the island was because he had been watching the case — had been watching Arturo's research — through his own network, and had moved when the case moved. Without Bellini on the scene, the negotiation would have been harder and slower and more likely to produce a dispute that the Coast Guard alone couldn't resolve. Bellini's institutional authority had been the thing that turned a potentially contested site into a cooperative one.

He had not arranged either of these things. He had been in the right place at the right time, and he had used the advantages that gave him.

That was luck.

That was not skill.

He thought: luck is part of the practice. You make yourself available for the lucky moment by doing the work correctly. But you do not confuse the luck with the work.

He looked at the pelican.

The pelican looked at him.

He thought: twelve years of this.

He thought: twelve years of morally ambiguous requests and insufficient information and improvised conclusions and adequate outcomes.

He thought: it is, on balance, a good way to spend a life.

He picked up the yellow legal pad from the deck table.

He looked at the last page — the page where he had written a name, the night he'd come back from Key West.

He had taken a new case.

It was a smaller case than the Vasquez-Cortez matter. Most cases were smaller than that, which was a statistic he was grateful for, because you couldn't sustain the intensity of the Vasquez-Cortez case as a regular professional diet without losing the parts of yourself that made the work possible.

This new case involved a woman in Homestead who needed to know whether her business partner of fifteen years was conducting himself honestly. The evidence she had was suggestive. The answer she needed was definitive. It was a case that required careful observation, patient document review, and a clear-headed report.

It was his kind of work.

He wrote a note on the legal pad.

He thought: let's go.

He went below and got dressed.

He drove to Homestead.

He did the work.

---

Three months later, on a Sunday evening in July, Jake got a call from Sofia Reyes.

She was in Miami — she had been in Miami for her master's program since September — and she called to tell him about her conference presentation, which had been accepted at the American Society of Marine Archaeology annual meeting and which she was going to give in San Francisco in October.

"The Acoustic Architecture paper?" he said.

"That one and a new one," she said. "I've been analyzing the full cave system geometry. The acoustic signature isn't just incidental to the cave's shape — I think it was partly engineered. Or, engineered isn't the right word, because they didn't build the cave. But selected. The site was chosen because of the acoustic properties. Diego chose that specific cave system because of the sound."

"He described it in the Codex."

"He described it as the first thing he noticed. Before the interior chamber. Before the archive possibility. He heard the sound and he knew." She paused. "What I'm arguing in the new paper is that the Templar navigational intelligence that led them to Caribbean islands included acoustic surveys — that part of their practice was recognizing natural cave systems by their acoustic profiles, because natural resonant chambers were their preferred archive locations in other parts of their network."

"Are there other Templar archive locations?"

"None yet found," she said. "But based on the profile — limestone coastal formations, specific tide exposure, narrow-to-wide passage geometry — I can identify candidate locations in the Mediterranean that have never been investigated for this purpose." She paused. "I have funding applications pending."

He looked at the bay. The July evening was doing what July evenings in Miami do: being ninety-two degrees and gorgeous and slightly threatening, the cloud architecture in the east suggesting that the nighttime rain was organizing itself.

"You're looking for more," he said.

"I'm looking for more," she confirmed.

"Your father know?"

"He thinks it's a good use of the methodology. He says you have to keep checking your work."

Jake thought about Nacho, keeping the logbook.

"He's right," he said.

"I know." She paused. "How are you?"

"Working," he said.

"Good case?"

"Good enough case." He paused. "Not as interesting as yours."

"Yours had more shooting."

"None of mine had any shooting," he said.

"None of mine had any either," she said. "Though I'm told there was a moment—"

"There was not a moment," he said. "Don't believe everything your father says."

She laughed. It was a brief, precise laugh — Nacho's laugh in miniature.

He looked at the bay.

"Sofia," he said.

"Yes?"

"What the archive means — the Philip letters, the theological documents, the navigational charts. What do you think it means? For history. For the way people understand things."

She was quiet for a moment.

She said: "It means the story we told ourselves was incomplete. Which isn't surprising — the story we tell ourselves is always incomplete. The archive doesn't correct the story. It adds to it. It makes the story more complicated and more accurate." She paused. "My marine archaeology professor says: every new find is an addition, not a replacement. The old evidence doesn't disappear. The new evidence joins it and changes what it means." She paused. "The Philip letters don't erase Philip IV. They erase the simple version of Philip IV."

"The simple version is what people remember."

"Yes," she said. "But the complicated version is what happened." She paused. "Diego understood that. He was preserving the complicated version of the story for the future, when the future would be ready to handle complexity." She paused. "I think we're ready. I think we're getting there."

He thought about this.

He said: "Diego would have been pleased with you."

"I know," she said, without false modesty. "I think he would have been pleased with all of us."

After the call, he sat on the deck until the rain came, which it did around nine, first as distant lightning over the Atlantic and then as the specific drumming of a summer storm on a tin-roofed boat at dock.

He went below.

He poured a rum.

He sat at the salon table with the yellow legal pad and thought about the long chain of people who had kept things: the Templars with their sealed vessels, Diego with his careful coding, Arturo with his thirty-four years, Nacho's grandfather with his island story, Roberto Sanchez with his envelope, Tommy Okeechobee with the knowledge of the land, Sofia with her underwater attention, Elena with her scientific patience.

All of them keeping something.

All of them trusting the future with it.

He thought: that's what the work is.

He thought: the work is making yourself a keeper.

He looked at the legal pad. The case notes. The list of facts and the list of what the facts meant and the list of what was not yet known.

He picked up the pen.

He wrote.

The rain drummed on the roof.

The bay moved outside.

He worked until midnight, and then he went to sleep, and in the morning he got up and went back to it, as he always did, as he would keep doing, as long as there was something worth finding.

There was always something worth finding.

That was the one thing he was certain of.

That was enough to keep going on.
